Summary

Person, place, and time are critical to the study of infectious disease transmission. The risks of acquiring infectious diseases are unequally distributed across the population, as sometimes the exposures to infectious agents depend on geographicallyrelated variables. In 1854, John Snow identified water from the pump at London’s Broad Street as the transmission source during a cholera outbreak by illustrating the cholera cases on a spot map. Since that time, spatial analytics have evolved considerably: with recent developments in geographical information systems and improved computing power, statistical modelling capabilities can be developed to answer epidemiological questions about an infectious disease outbreak. However, to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals, more comprehensive spatial data and analyses have been called for to inform health resource management and improve control measures during outbreaks. Statistical and epidemiological methods are invaluable to investigate the spread of infectious diseases. Modelling capabilities may facilitate informed response during future outbreaks, and such analytic capabilities need to be developed now, in “peace” time, to prepare for “war” time, when decisions have to be made rapidly based on whatever information is available. Some interventions may be spatial in nature, such as vector control around Zika or dengue hotspots, or school closure in response to a respiratory pathogen. This thesis will describe and discuss methods which incorporate location-stratified social contact data and spatial data to study geographical-related exposures for contact-transmissible infectious diseases and vector-borne diseases, to evaluate and inform location-specific interventions such as school closure, workplace distancing, and intensive national vector control

While the applications of these methods are focused on different diseases found in Singapore and the region—tuberculosis (TB), Zika, and respiratory infections, the aspects of the methods are applicable more broadly to other settings and other infectious diseases. A brief overview of the infectious diseases and methodologies discussed in the thesis is provided in Chapter 1. In Chapter 4, I explore the relevance of location types such as homes and workplaces in the transmission of geographically structured infectious diseases, using data from the 2016 Zika outbreak in Singapore to validate our model. Chapter 5 concludes this dissertation and contains suggestions for future research that could contribute meaningfully to the utilisation of spatial techniques to understand the transmission of infectious diseases.

Introduction

The geography of infectious diseases

A century ago, the world witnessed the greatest medical holocaust in history. Infecting a third of the world’s population and killing approximately 50 million people in the process, the 1918 influenza pandemic was surmised to have had more casualties in 18 months than the World War I had in four years (Johnson and Mueller, 2002; Patterson and Pyle, 1991). In the same century, at least two other influenza pandemics—the 1957 H2N2 pandemic and the 1968 H3N2 pandemic—followed, sweeping across the globe and claiming innumerable lives (Cox and Subbarao, 2000; Nicholson et al., 2003). The severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) was one of the first high profile epidemics of the twenty-first century, in which over 8 000 people from 26 countries across five continents were infected with SARS and approximately 10% of the infected lost their lives (Peiris et al., 2003, 2004). While the death toll of SARS was far smaller than the influenza pandemics (Oxford, 2005), the economic impact of SARS was valued at US$30–140 billion (Skowronski et al., 2005). The rapid spread of SARS between countries and continents illustrates how infectious diseases know no boundaries (Petersen et al., 2011). In today’s globalised world, the migration of people, both short-term and long-term, is becoming increasingly ubiquitous. With increased human mobility in frequency and distance, infec1

1.2 Tuberculosis in today’s world tious diseases—like SARS (Al-Tawfiq et al., 2014; Bell, 2004; Leder and Newman, 2005; Ruan et al., 2006), Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERSCoV) (Al-Tawfiq et al., 2014; Oboho et al., 2015), Zika (Bogoch et al., 2016; Faria et al., 2016; Hennessey et al., 2016; Kindhauser et al., 2016; Petersen et al., 2016), dengue (Ito et al., 2007; Stephenson, 2005; Stoddard et al., 2013, 2009)—transmit further and more frequently. In addition to these emerging infectious diseases, other widespread infectious diseases continue to appear in the top ten causes of deaths in the world (World Health Organization, 2018b). However, geographical differences in the leading causes of death are apparent, and more than half of the premature deaths in 2010 in sub-Saharan African communities can be attributed to an infectious pathogen (Bhutta et al., 2014; Lozano et al., 2012). Moreover, the rapid growth of the human population in Asia has led to changes to the ecosystem that make it a hotbed for infectious disease transmission (Lu et al., 2008). Heterogeneities in the disease burden challenge the elimination of infectious diseases. The confluence of susceptible individuals from low disease burden regions with infected cases or ambulant carriers from higher disease regions, because of increased distance and frequency of human mobility, provides a milieu for the global transmission of emerging and reemerging infectious diseases, as well as more established diseases such as HIV or TB (Cliff and Haggett, 2004; Petersen et al., 2016; Tatem et al., 2006).

The fate of the Tuberculosis (TB) bacillus and humankind has been entangled over many millennia, with infections documented in ancient Egypt around 3500 BC (Cave and Demonstrator, 1939; Daniel, 2006; Hershkovitz et al., 2008), and well-known victims of the “white plague” included Frederic Chopin (Dormandy, 2000; Kubba and Young, 1998), Charlotte Brontë (Dormandy, 2000; Helm, 2002), and Nelson Mandela (Lodge, 2007; Meer, 1990; Meredith, 2010). Even after centuries of advancements in science and medicine, humans are still plagued by TB—a communi2

The connection between TB and poor standards of living has been widely established (Das and Horton, 2015; Laxminarayan et al., 2009; Lönnroth et al., 2009; Ortblad et al., 2015). Densely-populated, impoverished living conditions exacerbate the transmission of the contact-transmissible infectious agent. TB thrives in poverty-stricken populations where individuals living in these heavily populated slums may also be malnourished, weakening their immune systems’ ability to suppress disease (Das and Horton, 2015; Dormandy, 2000; Lönnroth et al., 2009; Ortblad et al., 2015). The development of and access to effective chemotherapy, improved living environments with better hygiene and sanitation, and improved nutrition engendered the decline of the TB epidemic in the early twentieth century (Zumla, 2011). The variety of anti-tuberculosis drugs increased from none to more than ten in the aftermath of the Second World War (Chaisson, 2003). The treatment prescribed to remedy the symptoms of this malady and suppress any bacteria includes oral medication over several months (Chaisson, 2003).

The butcher’s bill Over the last few decades, TB has resurged in both developed and developing countries, particularly among poorer populations. It is still the leading infectious cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for 10.4 million incident cases and 1.3 million deaths in 2016 (World Health Organization, 2017). As mentioned earlier, TB, which is often a disease of poverty (Das and Horton, 2015; Laxminarayan et al., 2009; Lönnroth et al., 2009; Ortblad et al., 2015), is disproportionately concentrated in many regions of lowand middle-income countries. Aside from taking advantage of the densely populated environment and the inequitable access to healthcare, the TB epidemic was exacerbated by the high prevalence of susceptible individuals with immunodeficiency disorders, in particular AIDS, in these countries (Corbett et al., 2003; Lönnroth et al., 2010). Over 95% of TB deaths occur in lowand middle-income populations (Kyu et al., 2018; World Health Organization, 2018c). Like many countries in Asia, Cambodia ranks amongst the 30 highest TB burden countries. The End TB Strategy and the Sustainable Development Goals seek to eliminate the pestilence that is TB (Uplekar et al., 2015). They set ambitious targets of reducing the 2015 TB deaths and incidence by 95% and 90%, respectively, and challenge public health institutions to achieve these goals by 2035 (Uplekar et al., 2015; World Health Organization, 2017). The key to conquering TB and realising the End TB Strategy’s goals is a transdisciplinary and multi-sectoral approach which, among other aims, seeks to improve standards of living of individuals, ensure equitable healthcare access and treatment compliance, reduce stigma of infected individuals, and advance new technologies, including drugs, vaccines and diagnostic tools (Carter et al., 2018; Lönnroth et al., 2010).

Mixing patterns and contact-transmissible infectious diseases

Although TB remains one of the leading infectious diseases burden, public health institutions worldwide recognise that the emergence of other novel strains of infectious diseases could also threaten the lives of humans and other animals (Mills et al., 2004; Webby and Webster, 2003). These public health institutions stress the importance of preparing for such pandemics and outbreaks and prioritise strategies to mitigate the spread of infectious disease outbreaks with pandemic potential, such as SARS (Cauchemez et al., 2006; Pitman et al., 2005; Riley et al., 2003; Wallinga and Teunis, 2004) and novel influenza strains for example (Ferguson et al., 2006; Germann et al., 2006; Longini et al., 2005). The 2003 SARS epidemic highlighted how a lapse in surveillance and response in a single country can compromise global public health security (Heymann and Rodier, 2004; Peiris et al., 2004; Skowronski et al., 2005). Understanding how these infectious diseases spread would inform control measures and may thereby prevent transmission.

Mixing patterns and contact-transmissible infectious diseases

Contact-transmissible infectious diseases—such as influenza, SARS, and TB— percolate through a population through close contact between infected and susceptible individuals. For instance, human-to-human transmissible influenza can spread when a susceptible individual comes into close proximity with an infectious person, thereby being exposed to the infectious agent, and subsequently succumbing to the infection (Cox and Subbarao, 2000). Hence, studying human behaviours such as social mixing patterns is critical to understanding the spread of these diseases. Mathematical models which simply assume homogeneous mixing of individuals may not realistically capture patterns of disease transmission (Edmunds et al., 1997; Ferguson et al., 2003; Wallinga et al., 1999, 2006). This may lead to mischaracterisation of epidemic features, such as the potential of a pathogen to mutate to ever-greater virulence (Read and Keeling, 2003). Individuals’ mixing patterns are non-random as they are conditional on the physical presence of the contacted persons, and such contact rates influence the transmission dynamics of communicable diseases. Although difficult to do well, several studies have been conducted to measure social contact or mixing patterns of populations (Ajelli and Litvinova, 2017; Cattuto et al., 2010; Fu et al., 2012; Grijalva et al., 2015; Horby et al., 2011; Isella et al., 2011; Johnstone-Robertson et al., 2011; Kiti et al., 2014; Melegaro et al., 2017; Mossong et al., 2008; Read et al., 2014; Smieszek et al., 2016; Stehlé et al., 2011b). Paper diaries (Ajelli and Litvinova, 2017; Fu et al., 2012; Grijalva et al., 2015; Horby et al., 2011; Johnstone-Robertson et al., 2011; Kiti et al., 2014; Melegaro et al., 2017; Mossong et al., 2008; Read et al., 2014) and radio frequency identification tags (Cattuto et al., 2010; Isella et al., 2011; Stehlé et al., 2011b) measuring individuals’ mixing patterns have been fielded in several settings and the heterogeneities elicited through these studies used to inform mathematical models (Baguelin et al., 2010; Chao et al., 2010; de Greeff et al., 2010; Medlock and Galvani, 2009; Rohani et al., 2010; Salathé and Jones, 2010; Tamerius et al., 2011). Human mobility and mosquito-borne diseases

In addition to heterogeneities in social mixing that may drive transmission dynamics of contact-transmissible infectious diseases, there may also be heterogeneities in exposure to vectors for diseases like dengue that spread between humans and mosquitoes. Such exposures may also be spatially structured. Mosquito-borne diseases, like dengue, remain a public health issue with an estimated 390 million dengue infections yearly (Bhatt et al., 2013). Such diseases have a strong geographic component because of the role of vectors in their transmission, vectors which are in turn strongly influenced by environmental factors. While new vector control strategies are being developed, such as biocontrol through infection of the mosquito population with the Wolbachia bacterium (Hoffmann et al., 2011), the release of genetically modified sterile mosquitoes (Alphey et al., 2013; Thomas et al., 2000), and recent developments in partially effective vaccines (Capeding et al., 2014; Durbin et al., 2013, 2011; Flasche et al., 2016; Kirkpatrick et al., 2015; Osorio et al., 2011, 2014; Schwartz et al., 2015; Villar et al., 2015), the effectiveness of these interventions is limited by our understanding of the drivers of disease transmission and the spatial dynamics between the host and vectors (Stoddard et al., 2013). Mosquitoes do not traverse far (Getis et al., 2003; Harrington et al., 2005) but humans do—thus allowing for the spread of mosquito-borne diseases to distant sites (Wesolowski et al., 2012). Several studies have emphasised the role of human mobility in the propagation of dengue (Barmak et al., 2016; Cosner et al., 2009; Getis et al., 2003; Stoddard et al., 2013, 2009), and it is anticipated that other vector-borne diseases, such as Zika, have similar properties.

Modelling infectious diseases and their geography

Statistical and epidemiological models calibrated on data from past epidemics allows us to identify and assess, from a plethora of scenarios and interventions, the best strategies to mitigate the future transmission of an infectious agent (Ferguson et al., 2005, 2006, 2003; Haggett, 2000; Tatem et al., 2006). However, the vast range of possible scenarios complicates the development of such models, limiting the models’ capability to capture complete biological realism (Ferguson et al., 2003; Tatem et al., 2006).

The choice of prior distributions is one of the several challenges in Bayesian inference and there are several approaches to select suitable prior distributions of the parameters. If the parameter of a model has the same family of distributions a priori and a posteriori, the prior distribution can be selected to be conjugate to the likelihood distribution, and in such instances the posterior distribution can be derived analytically. However often there are no suitable conjugate prior distributions for the likelihood and we may have to define prior distributions for the parameters by other means. As the data used in the ensuing chapters of the thesis were rich with information, we were often able to use flat or non-informative prior distributions for our parameters. A flat (or almost flat) prior has parameter values equiprobably on an interval around high likelihood parameter values. In such cases, the posterior is proportional (or almost so) to the likelihood and as such the prior has negligible effect on the posterior. MCMC is one of several of algorithms used to generate random samples from a posterior distribution that is difficult to sample from directly. In MCMC, we sample iteratively from a distribution which eventually (and hopefully) converges to the desired probability distribution. In this thesis, we use both the Gibbs sampler (Geman and Geman, 1984) and the more general Metropolis-Hastings algorithm (Hastings, 1970; Metropolis et al., 1953) to update the parameters and simulate from the target posterior distribution.

Specific aims of this thesis

In this thesis, we develop statistical methods to incorporate spatial data and locationstratified social contact data to study the geographical-related exposures for infectious disease transmission. The thesis aims to evaluate and inform location-specific interventions for contact-transmissible infectious disease such as TB and pandemic influenza, and vector-borne diseases such as Zika. These location-specific interventions include for example school closure, workplace distancing, and intensive

national vector control programmes. We endeavour to understand (i) how prevalence of an infectious disease may vary across geographical regions of a country with differences in demographic structure; (ii) how social contacts which may lead to infection with a contacttransmissible infectious disease vary across locations and between countries at different stages of demographic development; and finally (iii) how human mobility across homes and workplaces can advance the transmission of a geographically structured infectious disease. Specifically, through modelling geographical heterogeneities in Chapter 2, we seek to understand how the prevalence of TB varies across geographical regions, using Cambodia, a high burden TB country, as a case study. This work provides a lens to guide policymakers and public health programmes, specifically the TB control unit in Cambodia, when developing and evaluating possible public health interventions to combat TB. To understand how location-specific (i.e. home, workplace, and school) interventions to mitigate a contact-transmissible pandemic vary in their effects across countries at different stages of development, we developed a modelling framework in Chapter 3 to combine social contact data from past studies of contact patterns within eight countries in Europe with data from multiple data sources. In this work, we provide validated estimates of age-and-location-specific contact rates for 152 countries and perform simulations to evaluate the effectiveness of location-specific interventions to curtail the transmission of a pandemic. Using spatio-temporal modelling and data from the 2016 Zika outbreak in Singapore, in Chapter 4 we demonstrate empirically the effectiveness of locationspecific interventions in containing the Zika outbreak. We seek to understand the relevance of location types such as workplaces in the transmission of this geographically structured infectious disease and describe a method to infer possible infection trees, which importantly accounts for primary and secondary locations of infected individuals. The Cambodia healthcare system has an established network of public and private providers, but while the private healthcare sector provides 61% of healthcare services (World Health Organization Regional Office for the Western Pacific, 2016), TB care—including control, prevention, and treatment—is predominantly provided by the government sector. Conclusion

In collaboration with CENAT under the purview of the Ministry of Health, Cambodia, the work in this chapter investigated the changes in TB prevalence across the geographically regions in Cambodia through evidence synthesis of health and geographic data. The Bayesian hierarchical model developed endeavours to project the future burden of active TB across the country to 2030. Future TB projections under alternative scenarios were also derived by interfacing these estimates with an individual-based demographic model. Public health actions and interventions can be further developed in the model to inform efficient allocation of resources. Through modelling the geographical heterogeneities in this chapter, we explored how the prevalence of a contact-transmissible infectious diseases like TB differs across the large-scaled geographical regions in Cambodia. The risk of infectious disease transmission also varies in different settings on a smaller scale, for instance at home, at work or in the community, as a result of the different social structures and mixing patterns in those locations. Social contact patterns have been measured in a small number of countries, but in large swathes of the world, contact patterns are unmeasured, which makes it challenging to build mathematical or computer models of disease spread and control. In the next chapter, we describe a modelling framework developed to combine social contact data from the past studies of contact patterns within eight countries in the Europe with data from multiple data sources including the Demographic Household Surveys (DHS), World Bank and United Nations (UN) Statistics Division, to provide validated approximations to age-and-location-specific contact rates for 152 countries covering 95.9% of the world’s population. We will investigate how location-specific interventions to mitigate a contact-transmissible pandemic, like school closure and workplace distancing, vary in their effects across 152 countries at different stages of development. For instance, we believe that the interventions to contain the spread of TB in Singapore will probably be different from that of Cambodia because social structures vary across these countries which are in different stages of development and with different demographics.

Heterogeneities in contact networks have a major effect in determining whether a pathogen can become epidemic or persist at endemic levels. Epidemic models that determine which interventions can successfully prevent an outbreak need to account for social structure and mixing patterns. Contact patterns vary across age and locations (e.g. home, work, and school), and including them as predictors in transmission dynamic models of pathogens that spread socially will improve the models’ realism. Household level data from the Demographic and Health Surveys for nine lower-income countries and socio-demographic factors from several on-line databases for 152 countries were used to quantify similarity of countries to estimate contact patterns in the home, work, school and other locations for countries for which no contact data are available, accounting for demographic structure, household structure where known, and a variety of metrics including workforce participation and school enrolment. This study provides estimates of mixing patterns for societies for which contact data such as POLYMOD are not yet available.

Introduction

Events over the last decade have highlighted the threat posed internationally by contact-transmissible infectious diseases such as hand, foot and mouth disease (Koh et al., 2016; Sabanathan et al., 2014), MERS-CoV (Penttinen et al., 2013), Ebola (Gomes et al., 2014), influenza (Khan et al., 2009), and Tuberculosis (Reichler, 2002), putting pressure on governments and public health institutes (Reichler, 2002) to ensure countries are pandemic-prepared. Research in social networks has shown that transmissibility, and hence the effectiveness of many interventions, is determined by the intensity of human-to-human interactions (Eubank et al., 2004). erogeneities in contact networks—in the sense of clustering of contacts within triadic structures and the existence of individuals or groups with many more contacts than average—has been shown in modelling studies (i) to have an effect on determining whether a pathogen can become epidemic (Hu et al., 2013; Jacquez et al., 1988; Meyers et al., 2005) or can persist at endemic levels (van den Driessche and Watmough, 2002), (ii) to exert selective pressure for low virulence (Dieckmann and International Institute for Applied Systems Analysis, 2002), and (iii) to determine which interventions can possibly mitigate an outbreak (Eubank et al., 2004; Ferguson et al., 2005; Meyers et al., 2005; Worby et al., 2015) or even eradicate a disease from the population (Ferguson et al., 2005). While epidemic models that do not account for contact structure suffice for some research questions, such as prediction (Andrews and Basu, 2011) or determining minimum vaccine coverage if there are no pockets of high transmission intensity or low coverage (Ball et al., 2004; Gay et al., 1995; Mukandavire et al., 2013; Nokes and Swinton, 1997), assessing the effectiveness of interventions that specifically target social networks, such as school closure (Cauchemez et al., 2008; Hens et al., 2009a; Stehlé et al., 2011b), requires models that explicitly account for such social structure. Determining contact patterns within and between different segments of the population is therefore vital to evidence-based prediction and planning with sufficient realism to inform good policy making.

To this end, a seminal study by Mossong et al. in 2008 (Mossong et al., 2008) measured the social structure of ∼ 100 000 contacts across eight European countries using paper diaries as part of the POLYMOD project. The study illuminated the strong assortativity of social contacts with age, with children in particular driving the early period of an epidemic, a finding that has been reproduced in other studies (Glass and Glass, 2008). Similar studies to measure the assortativity of contacts have been conducted in a few other locations: Viet Nam (Horby et al., 2011), Taiwan (Fu et al., 2012), southern China (Read et al., 2014), Peru (Grijalva et al., 2015), South Africa (Johnstone-Robertson et al., 2011), Kenya (Kiti et al., 2014),

Russia (Ajelli and Litvinova, 2017) and Thailand (Stein et al., 2014). However, the age and social structure of countries with different levels of socioeconomic development, family structure and at different stages of demographic transition differ substantially and contact patterns concomitantly vary across countries. The findings from the POLYMOD and limited number of other countries cannot therefore be directly applied to models of socially-transmitted infections, such as influenza, in other settings (Stein et al., 2014). The lack of reliable contact studies in most of the world almost a decade after the original POLYMOD study highlights the logistical challenges of conducting such studies, particularly in low income countries which account for most of the infectious disease burden globally (Murray and Lopez, 1997). Although it is preferable for models in such a country to be based on empirical contact data from that country directly, in the absence of such data, modelers have to decide how to modify estimates from another country to fill the gap. One approach to tailor the POLYMOD estimates to another setting would be to adjust them based on the differences between the age-profiles of that country and the eight countries from the European study that contributed to POLYMOD, inflating the projected number of contacts with younger individuals, for instance, for a country with a young age-profile, and concurrently reducing the projected number of contacts with the elderly. However, because the POLYMOD survey collected information on where each contact occurred, a more refined projection can be obtained by incorporating data on household structures, labour-force participation rates, and school enrolment, and separately projecting contacts in different locations. This has the added benefit of providing a means to model location-specific interventions. The objective of this chapter is to provide projected age-specific contact rates for countries in different stages of development and with different demographic structures to those studied in POLYMOD, which provide validated approximations to social contact patterns when directly measured data are not available. To this end, we combined data from POLYMOD, from the large-scale DHS, from the UN

population division and from various international indicators, to project household structures and school and labour force participation rates for most countries of the world, and thereby to provide baseline projections of age-specific contact patterns in settings where contact surveys have yet to be conducted, until empirical estimates become available.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

The POLYMOD data collection was approved by national institutional review boards, as previously described (Mossong et al., 2008). As no identifying information was provided, institutional review was not required for reanalysis.

Overview of the methodology

The overall approach is to use data fusion to combine various data sources to project ageand location-specific contact rates for a spectrum of countries in different stages of development and with different demographic structures. Data

The POLYMOD study design has been fully described elsewhere (Mossong et al., 2008). In brief, cross-sectional surveys were conducted across eight countries then in the European Union (listed in Table 3.1: Belgium (BE), Germany (DE), Finland (FI), the United Kingdom (UK), Italy (IT), Luxembourg (LU), the Netherlands (NL), and Poland (PL)) by commercial survey companies and public health institutes, between May 2005 and September 2006. 7 290 participants documented their contacts as they occurred on a randomly assigned 24 hour period in a paper diary, including age and gender of contacted person, and the type (physical or nonphysical), duration, location and frequency of the contact. Participant demographics were also recorded. Diaries of young children were completed by a parent or guardian. The study collected information of 97 904 contacts. In the analysis, 52 participants (0.7% of the total; 36 reported no contacts and 16 reported contacts with missing ages) and 148 contacts (0.2% of total; 120 with missing age and 28 with missing location) were excluded for data quality reasons. Because participants and contacts were mostly from the general community, the resulting contact patterns are suitable for models of diseases that are socially spread, such as influenza, but not directly usable for diseases with other routes of transmission, such as nosocomial infections. Validation

Several forms of validation were conducted.

HAM Validation for POLYMOD and DHS Countries. Household structure data was made available for eight POLYMOD (Belgium, Germany, Finland, the United Kingdom, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, and Poland) (Mossong et al., 2008) and nine DHS countries (Bangladesh, Bolivia, Ghana, Indonesia, India, the Philippines, Sierra Leone, Uganda and Zambia) (Demographic and Health Surveys, 2015). HAM is needed to estimate the age-specific contacts at home. While this can be easily computed for the 17 POLYMOD and DHS countries, it is not straightforward for ROW countries. In the earlier section, we highlight the methods we developed to estimate the HAM of the ROW countries which is the weighted mean of the population ratio matrices of the 17 POLYMOD/DHS countries. Leaveone-out validation was performed to reconstruct the HAM of one POLYMOD or DHS country at a time. This involved projecting that country’s household age structure as if it were unknown and comparing against the actual structure to assess the method’s performance.

Hierarchical Model Validation

In this study, individual-level contact data were available for the 8 POLYMOD countries, allowing us to validate the model’s performance. We validated (i) the Bayesian hierarchical model and (ii) the ROW projection procedure with the POLYMOD contact data. To validate the ROW procedure, we treated the POLYMOD countries as an ROW country to obtain the projected contact matrices. These projections were compared to the empirical POLYMOD data.

External Validation

We also externally validated the projected contact matrices by comparing the projected matrices to recently conducted contact surveys in five low and middle income countries in three continents (Kenya, Peru, Russia, South Africa, and Viet Nam (Ajelli and Litvinova, 2017; Grijalva et al., 2015; Horby et al., 2011; JohnstoneRobertson et al., 2011; Kiti et al., 2014)). Numerical estimates or data were avail74

able for four of the five countries (all but Peru). For Peru, we digitized age-specific contact matrices (aggregated over location) and to get the estimated contact matrices. As ageand location-specific contact rates were available for Russia, we were able to validate our ageand location-specific estimates with theirs. For both the survey and our projected estimates, we computed the mean number of locationspecific contacts across the age of the individuals. For the other countries where only age-specific contact rates aggregated over all locations, we could only compare our projected matrices aggregated over location. We compared the mean number of location-specific contacts across the age of the individuals. For most of the projections or data from these countries, we computed the mean contact rates by age of individuals and contrasted the estimates and the projected contact matrices generated using the method described in the earlier sections. For 25 European countries, the projected contact matrices were compared with contact matrices derived by Fumanelli et al. (Fumanelli et al., 2012). Virtual populations were constructed using parameters from census and demographic data by Fumanelli et al. to estimate contact matrices.

Non-household contacts

Modelled projections of contact patterns in different locations—home, work, school, other and all—for Bolivia, Germany and South Africa, are plotted in Figure 3.11. In Germany, for which it was explicitly measured, the age-specific contact patterns (panels d–f) at the workplace show wide clusters of contacts among working ages (20–60), indicating relatively homogenous mixing in this setting. Adapting this finding to Bolivia and South Africa, accounting for the age structure of their labour forces, led to similar homogeneity in workforce contacts there. Unlike in households, the presence of more diverse age structures in workplaces could provide a ready channel for transmission between distinct age groups, separated in the main from each other within the home setting.

Intense mixing, indicated by the pronounced central diagonal, is present in the age-specific school contact pattern (panels g–i), suggesting the importance of this milieu for transmission potential within this age group. Within schools, the assortativity of mixing patterns is more pronounced in younger individuals (below the age of 25), while those of working-age (teachers and support staff aged 30–60) have more moderate contact rates between themselves and younger individuals, the latter characterizing student-teacher interactions. This pattern was present across other countries (Appendix A). Similar to the home and school contact patterns, a strong central diagonal band and at times weak secondary diagonals can be observed in the projected contacts made at other (non-home, non-work, non-school) locations. This assortativity contrasts with the work environment. However, apart from the leading diagonal, the contact patterns within this other grouping vary across countries in a non-systematic way. Aggregating contact rates from the four locations (home, work, school and others) indicates that the strong central diagonal due to school and home contacts dominates overall contact rates.

Discussion

A decade after POLYMOD, age-structured contact matrices based on random populationbased samples have been published for only a limited spectrum of countries: eight European countries (Mossong et al., 2008), a handful in Asia (Ajelli and Litvinova, 2017; Fu et al., 2012; Horby et al., 2011; Read et al., 2014; Stein et al., 2014), one in Latin America (Grijalva et al., 2015) and two in Sub-Saharan Africa (JohnstoneRobertson et al., 2011; Kiti et al., 2014). The risk of emerging diseases spreading from other animals to humans is not restricted to these areas (Song et al., 2005) (as the recent MERS and Ebola events illustrate (Gomes et al., 2014; Penttinen et al., 2013)), and there is a pressing need for contact matrices representing more diverse countries around the world for models of socially-spread diseases to be built. This study presents data-driven contact matrices for 152 countries of the world for the first time. Individuals’ interactions are non-random and, because they are contingent on the physical presence of the individual and contact, vary by location. As in the original analysis of these data (Mossong et al., 2008), we found that household, workplace and school structures across the world are consistent with age-specific contacts made by individuals, in that they are highly assortative, with much more frequent interactions with others of a similar age group. However, these precise age-dependent patterns differed by location and across the countries we studied. Interactions between school-going children and the elderly have important public health implications, as the former may have high infection rates while the latter are vulnerable to complications from infections, such as pneumonia (Cate, 1987; Falsey et al., 2003). Evidence for the effect of transmission from children to the elderly comes from Japan, where the cessation of the school influenza vaccination program led to a rise in mortality among the elderly (Reichert et al., 2001). Synthetic contact matrices have been generated by individual-based model simulations (Xia et al., 2013) or derived from socio-demographic variables (Fumanelli et al., 2012; Iozzi et al., 2010) and validated on serological data of H1N1 Influenza (Fumanelli et al., 2012; Xia et al., 2013), varicella and parvo-virus (Iozzi et al., 2010) using age-structured SIR models. Some of these synthetic contact matrices were created for only one country (Hong Kong (Xia et al., 2013) and Italy (Iozzi et al., 2010)), while Ref. (Fumanelli et al., 2012) estimated contact matrices for 26 European countries. Despite the lack of social contact surveys, synthetic contact matrices have only developed for higher-incomed countries (Del Valle et al., 2007; Fumanelli et al., 2012; Iozzi et al., 2010; Xia et al., 2013), with large proportions lower-and-middle-income countries unrepresented.

There are several assumptions underlying our study. We assumed the number

Discussion

of contacts in each location was Poisson, with over-dispersal accounted for using an individual-level random effect term that was assumed to govern contacts in all four locations, but in principle additional heterogeneities might be present leading to mischaracterisation of correlations, dispersion (Efron, 1992), and the number of zeros. As in the original POLYMOD study, we only quantified age-specific dyadic contacts, as eliciting higher order contacts in a survey is challenging both cognitively and practically. Triadic contacts—where A contacts B contacts C contacts A—are important for disease propagation in contact network models (Eubank et al., 2004; Stehlé et al., 2011a) and other methods to measure these, such as radio frequency tagging (Cattuto et al., 2010; Stehlé et al., 2011a), or more sophisticated structural models accounting temporal presence within the household (Potter et al., 2011), may be needed to characterize higher degrees of contact. However, it remains to be demonstrated empirically that the differences in the patterns of transmission of an infection between two countries can be explained by differences in their contact patterns, although our simulation study in three countries suggested that the effect of interventions can vary substantially based solely on changes in contacts driven by age structure. The short simulation study in this chapter made simplistic assumptions about how social distancing measures would translate into a reduction in contact rates, as well as assuming that heterogeneities in contact patterns could be well-described by an age-structured but otherwise mean-field model. In actual usage, the translation of a policy into a specific change in contact rates should be supported by evidence, while individual-based simulation models (Chao et al., 2010) provide a more flexible framework to capture shared contacts between individuals and allows policies such as reactive school closure to be assessed (Fumanelli et al., 2016).

The primary limitation of the chapter is that the projected contact matrices are derived from assumptions about the social structure in countries for which contact data are unavailable, so that the projected contact patterns cannot in most cases be directly validated. involved two main modelling steps with inherent assumptions, elaborated below.

The first main modelling step was the creation of household structures and the demographics of workforce/school participation for countries for which this was not measured. For households, this involved identifying similarity to countries for which household data were available (using POLYMOD or DHS), using a mixture of indicators on the economy and demography of each country, and applying a mapping from the age pyramids of countries to their household structure. The variables used gave more weight to pairs of countries that for the most part make intuitive sense (for instance, Germany (POLYMOD) is assigned high weight in constructing Austria’s (ROW) contact matrices). This approach did, however, mean excluding several small countries with missing information, covering 4.1% of the world’s population. The variables were arbitrarily selected to span measures of health and social structure (fertility, mortality, growth, and health expenditure), as well as development (income, internet penetration and urbanisation). The scree plot of the eigenvalue decomposition of these variables suggested that beyond 4 or 5 dimensions, additional variables did not add much new information, but alternative measures of distance would have resulted in some differences in the projected matrices. On validation, although there were some systematic discrepancies (for instance, the slightly off-centre leading diagonal for some Asian countries, which we suspect might be corrected by accounting for gender in future work), this approach reconstructed the empirical household structures with otherwise high accuracy for both the POLYMOD and DHS countries. We had substantially less data on potential school and work contacts, because direct samples from these environments were not available, even in POLYMOD, and so the approach extrapolated from indirect measures (workforce and school participation rates, teacher to student ratios). This means that the number and age-profile of contacts outside the home are inherently less well projected by our approach than those within the home. It is not clear how this approach could be improved for these location types, or for ‘other’ locations, however, without collecting substantial amounts of new data. The original

Conclusion

POLYMOD study found highly assortative mixing in ’other’ locations, and while the study in Russia (Ajelli and Litvinova, 2017) found a similar pattern, it is not clear whether this would be observed in other, non-European or Eurasian settings. The second major modelling step was to project from POLYMOD to non-POLYMOD countries the relationship between (i) household structure or workforce/school participation and (ii) contact rates within those locations. This approach implicitly assumes that given a potential contact in a given location (for instance, the existence of a cohabitant), the chance of an actual interaction is the same in POLYMOD and non-POLYMOD countries. The approximate validity of this assumption is supported by the broad similarities between our projections and estimates from the limited number of contact studies conducted outside Europe (Ajelli and Litvinova, 2017; Fu et al., 2012; Grijalva et al., 2015; Horby et al., 2011; JohnstoneRobertson et al., 2011; Kiti et al., 2014; Read et al., 2014), but some discrepancies were found in this validation. It is unclear to what extent these are due to violations of the assumptions made in this chapter, due to differences in study design—such as interviewerversus self-administered questionnaires, or translation effects—or due to differences between study populations and the country as a whole due to recruitment in a limited geographical area. Further empirical studies in other countries would provide additional validation. Those limitations notwithstanding, the projected contact matrices outlined in this chapter provide a basis for model-based analyses to inform public health policy making around the world, until comprehensive studies can be carried out that cover a greater fraction of the world’s population.

Conclusion

Findings from this chapter limned the difference in the social mixing patterns across populations at various stages of development and with different demographics. The simple simulation study suggested that the effectiveness of the location-specific interventions can vary substantially based solely on changes in social mixing patterns.

Conclusion

While it remains to be demonstrated empirically, differences in contact patterns between populations should lead to differences in the pattern of transmission of an infection. In the next chapter, we will empirically demonstrate how the effectiveness of interventions varies across location types using data documented during the 2016 Zika outbreak in Singapore. The transmission risk of vector-borne diseases, like Zika, also varies across location types implying that interventions designed to curtail spread may need to be location-specific. The 2016 Zika outbreak in Singapore evinced the contribution of human movement and transmission around workplaces to the spread of this vector-borne disease. Moreover, the effects of human mobility in the transmission of vector-borne diseases have been observed in several other urban studies (Barmak et al., 2016; Cosner et al., 2009; Stoddard et al., 2013, 2009). In an urban outbreak, home and work addresses may be geographically distant relative to the spatial scale of transmission; hence ambulant infected persons may infect distant parts of the country. Zika had and has the potential to become endemic in Singapore due to the presence of the vector, Aedes aegypti. Within weeks of the first documented case of autochthonous transmission in 2016, Zika virus (ZIKV) infected individuals across the city-state. However, much remains unknown about the risk of ZIKV infection to the immunologically naïve population of the highly urbanized island. In the next chapter, we examine via spatio-temporal modelling how transmission at workplaces played a vital role in perpetuating the ZIKV across Singapore and thus should not be ignored when targeting control efforts.

Introduction

The tropical city-state of Singapore is home to the Aedes mosquitoes, Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus, with local transmission of arboviruses such as dengue (Ang et al., 2015; Hapuarachchi et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2012; Ng et al., 2009a; Stephenson, 2005) and Chikungunya (Leo et al., 2009; Ng et al., 2009a,b), and putting it at risk of outbreaks of Zika virus (ZIKV) (Ho et al., 2017). The anthropophilic Ae. aegypti in particular is found—at low densities—in close proximity to the human population, despite sustained eradication efforts, including source reduction, traps that remove gravid mosquitoes, outbreak investigation around clusters of cases, and public health education campaigns (Ng et al., 2016; Ooi et al., 2006). The continued presence of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus has led to dengue being endemic in Singapore, with substantial impacts on health and the economy (Carrasco et al., 2011).

Introduction

As in many other metropolises, it is commonplace for residents to live and work in different parts of the city. As a result, although vector borne diseases are geographically clustered, because of the localized presence of infected mosquitoes, humans may act as a conduit to longer distance spread to seed new foci of infection (Barmak et al., 2016; Cosner et al., 2009; Stoddard et al., 2009). It is therefore vital to understand the contribution of both human mobility and the length of mosquito ranges to arbovirus dissemination. The Zika outbreak that was first identified in Singapore in August 2016 provided a unique opportunity to illuminate the geographical spread of a nascent arbovirus outbreak across the city-state. Within several weeks of its first identification within and around a construction site in the Southeast of the country, the outbreak had spread to multiple secondary loci across many parts of the island (Ho et al., 2017). Over the next month, there were ∼ 100 confirmed cases a week, of the same order of magnitude as the four serotypes of dengue (Ministry of Health, 2016). Like the dengue viruses, Zika is transmitted primarily by Ae. albopictus, and in consequence there was, and remains, the potential that the ZIKV could become similarly endemic. Autochthonous ZIKV transmission is concerning, because of the risk of severe complications including Guillain-Barré syndrome and microcephaly (CaoLormeau et al., 2016; Mlakar et al., 2016; Parra et al., 2016), which, although not reported to date in relation with ZIKV infection in Singapore, make successful control imperative. As a legally notifiable disease in Singapore, the residential and workplace addresses of confirmed cases of ZIKV disease were collected by the Ministry of Health under the proviso of the Infectious Diseases Act (Ho et al., 2017). Despite the ongoing baseline vector control program that historically targets Aedes mosquitoes in residential areas, the outbreak demonstrates the possibility that human movement may allow the virus to break the “cordon” of vector control and lead to cross-island transmission. In contrast to dengue, which has been endemic in Singapore since at least the 1960s (Ooi et al., 2006; Rajarethinam et al., 2018), the 2016 Zika outbreak

spread from one initial locus and across an immunologically naïve population; like dengue, it is expected that there be relatively little seasonal forcing on transmission risk (Chen et al., 2015). Together, these characteristics make Singapore an ideal model system to understand Zika transmission. Using spatio-temporal models built using Bayesian methods, we comprehensively analyze the autochthonous spread of Zika during the first wave in Singapore, exploiting the home and work addresses and date of symptom onset of 323 Zikaconfirmed cases. To understand the relevance of workplaces to the transmission of ZIKV infections away from home, we explored the space of possible infection trees (i.e. who-infected-whom-where) to infer how much infection, and how much transmission, occurs around the home and workplace of cases, which provides valuable information to guide vector control efforts.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

Ethical approval was obtained from the National University of Singapore Institutional Review Board.

Zika Case Data

The Zika outbreak in Singapore resulted in 323 Zika confirmed cases between July and September 2016 (Ho et al., 2017). As Zika had been made a notifiable disease, attending physicians were required to notify the Ministry of Health for all cases. Under the auspices of the Infectious Diseases Act, the residential and workplace addresses, and date of symptom onset were recorded for these confirmed cases. The data for residential address and date of symptom onset are complete, 44 (13.6%) Zika confirmed cases reported no workplace address—the majority of whom were retired, homemakers, or unemployed, and for these individuals their ‘workplace’ address was replaced with their residential address. We have no alternative but to

assume that they are likely to be around in the vicinity of their home during working hours.

Public Transport Data

Public transportation is the most common mode of transport of the residents of Singapore (Department of Statistics Singapore, 2010). The majority of the population uses public transport to commute to work or school. From the Singapore census in 2010 Department of Statistics Singapore (2010), 87% of the population relies on some means of transport to get to work or school, of whom about 57% regularly use the public transport to get to school/work. There are three main modes of public transportation in Singapore; the Mass Rapid Transit (MRT), the Light Rail Transit (LRT), and public buses. To pay the public transport fares, most commuters utilize an EZ-link contactless card: a smart card that is read when entering or leaving the MRT or LRT network or when boarding or alighting a bus. Using data on all users of EZ-link cards over one month (August 2013) from the Land Transport Authority of Singapore, which captures the time and location of commuters’ intersecting with the public health system, we identified individuals who visited within a 1 kilometre square (km2 ) centered on the initial outbreak focus, and for each inferred a plausible home and work address. For each card (which we treated as equivalent to a unique individual: although cards are transferable, commonly individuals retain a single card for years) in this reduced dataset, we inferred primary and secondary addresses based on the following algorithm, which we labeled their home and work location, respectively. To impute a home location for each card, we identified the first and last trips made per day. We then applied a two-dimensional Gaussian kernel with arbitrary bandwidth of 0.001 degrees of latitude or longitude around each starting or ending point, primarily to accommodate individuals who board and alight from buses on opposite sides of the street on the way to and from work. We then identified the point among these with the highest summed kernel to be the imputed home location.

Bus stops are typically located quite close to one another and to high density urban neighborhoods where most Singaporeans live, so the error between the actual home and imputed home is likely to be of the order of a few hundred metres. To impute a work (or school) location, we followed a similar approach, but using the last trip made before noon and the first trip made after noon. We then counted the number of days in which the imputed work address was visited and excluded those with fewer than 15 visits over the one month period. Those removed were suspected of having multiple work sites or of the trips not being to a workplace. We then discarded those with neither an inferred home nor work address within that 1 km2 , and summarized the distribution of distances between the addresses of those remaining.

Spatio-temporal model

We developed a spatio-temporal model using the residential and workplace addresses, and date of symptom onset (as a proxy for time of infection), of the 323 Zika confirmed cases between July and September 2016 to estimate the source of infection for each case using Bayesian data augmentation. While contact tracing is possible for diseases like severe acute respiratory syndrome (Leo et al., 2003), it is a challenge for vector borne disease as we are, obviously, unable to contact trace the mosquitoes, and as a result we do not know the source of infection for each case, i.e. the case infecting the mosquito infecting that case. The location where the individual is infected is unobserved and may often be their home or other places they spend significant amounts of time, such as their place of work. In addition, there is very little information available on those individuals who escape infection altogether. Calculating the likelihood of the parameters becomes challenging without knowing the extra information. To address this uncertainty, we developed a Bayesian data augmentation framework which considers the source of infection as augmented data or nuisance parameters (Gelman, 2014; Gibson and Renshaw, 1998; Gilks et al., 1998). Results

The spatio-temporal evolution of the outbreak depicted in Figure 4.2 shows how the initial focus within and around a construction site in the Aljunied neighborhood (in the Southeast of the country and on the periphery of the downtown core of Singapore) rapidly proliferated across the island over four weeks (Ho et al., 2017). This rapid dissemination coincided with the relatively large distances between the homes and workplaces of cases, with 25.4% of cases working at least 10km from their home (on an island that spans 50km at its greatest extent); this mirrors the relatively long distances travelled by commuters working or living in the Aljunied area (Figure 4.3), of whom 14.0% travel more than 10km to get to and from work. Cases on average travelled further than the reference group of people living or working in Aljunied (permutation test of median distances, p = 0.01). However, the distributions are qualitatively similar, indicating that even if the initial focus had not been at a construction site—leading to a substantial number of cases being construction workers mostly living in foreign worker dormitories on the other side of the island—there would still have been considerable risk of secondary foci being seeded far from the initial outbreak. The posterior mean half-life distances between infections—which represents a combination of human movement in the vicinity of their home or workplace and of mosquito movement over the incubation period—of the different models fitted ranged from 430m to 560m (Figure 4.4). Discussion

ZIKV infections, in common with other arboviruses such as dengue, are predominantly clinically mild (Ho et al., 2017), infected hosts may not be isolated from the vector, and through their continued mobility may disseminate infection far from their home, or from the location they were infected. The potentially high fraction of such ambulant, subclinical infections may lead to delays in the identification of new foci of infection. Moreover, in an urban outbreak like this, the distance from home to work may be large relative to the scale of transmission and, as a result, ambulant infected persons may act as the ‘vector’ infecting distant parts of the country (Barmak et al., 2016; Cosner et al., 2009; Stoddard et al., 2009), further complicating control. In this analysis, we identified some individuals who could be super-dispersers—or long distance human vectors—for ZIKV infection, and a key priority in future outbreaks should be to identify such individuals early and ideally isolate or protect them from being bitten by the mosquitoes. Such individuals are not unusual, as seen in the long tail for commuting distances for people living or working near the initial focus of the outbreak.

Discussion

Conventionally, the residential address is the usual proxy for location of infection in spatial analyses (Stoddard et al., 2013), but this study identified workplaces as a substantial source location for transmission. In the past, vector control measures concentrated on residential areas, although the national vector control program now considers both types of location in defining clusters for interventions. Substantial amounts of infection occurring at workplaces will make control that focuses on the home less effective, and as a result, the method developed in this study may potentially better inform vector control measures. Given the endemicity of dengue in Singapore, it would be of some interest to investigate the degree to which dengue infections occur away from the home, especially in the light of previous research which showed infection risk varying over a weekly cycle (Cook et al., 2012).

The methods described in this study met with several challenges. Neither the exact location nor the time of infection of the cases were known, and to overcome this we used the residential and workplace addresses and date of symptom onset as proxies of place and time of infection. Transmission in the neighborhood of the home or workplace is implicitly captured through the dispersal kernel, but it is a limitation that other locations frequented by cases (such as eating houses or places of worship (Cook et al., 2018)) could not be recorded. Learning more about human movement from mobile companies would be able to give us a deeper understanding to characterise the spread of Zika. Due to the non-negligible asymptomatic rate of ZIKV infections, we may have incomplete information on potentially infectious individuals despite the good surveillance system in Singapore. A previous analysis of this outbreak sought to infer the phylogeny of a fraction of the cases, but the low mutation rate led to a lack of variation in the genetic data (Ho et al., 2017), and as such, the epidemiological data provides greater information on the transmission dynamics. Over a longer time period, more mutations would be expected, and a marriage of field and molecular epidemiological information may be warranted for a longer outbreak (Lau et al., 2015; Morelli et al., 2012; Worby et al., 2016).

Conclusion

Conclusion

This work in this chapter is both important epidemiologically—indicating the importance of considering both residential and workplace addresses to inform the vector-control measures for a disease like Zika and dengue—and involved new methodological development to tease out the multiple spatial patterns driving this urban outbreak. Incorporating spatial data in the model allows us to study the geographical-related exposures for these vector-borne diseases. Singapore’s outbreak appears qualitatively different from those in less urbanized environments (Duffy et al., 2009; Musso et al., 2015; Pettersson et al., 2018), in that infected Aedes mosquitoes played a less significant role in the long-distance transmission of Zika than did ambulant cases, which may have implications for control of subsequent arbovirus outbreaks in urban settings like Singapore.

Concluding remarks and

recommendations for future work As this thesis has illustrated, the risk of infectious disease transmission varies in different settings, for instance at home, at work or in the community, as a result of the different social structures and mixing patterns in those locations. This thesis described statistical modelling methods developed to incorporate location-stratified social contact and spatial data to study geographical-related exposures for contacttransmissible infectious diseases and vector-borne diseases which are able to evaluate and inform location-specific interventions. Although the applications of these methods span different diseases found in Singapore and the region—TB, Zika, and respiratory infections—in principle these methods are applicable more broadly to other settings and other infections.

Several studies have attempted to measure social mixing patterns of populations using contact surveys (Ajelli and Litvinova, 2017; Fu et al., 2012; Grijalva et al., 2015; Horby et al., 2011; Johnstone-Robertson et al., 2011; Kiti et al., 2014; Mossong et al., 2008; Read et al., 2014). As social contact patterns remain unmeasured for many countries in the world, the country-specific contact matrices projected in Chapter 3 provide a basis for model-based analyses to inform public health policy making around the world, until comprehensive empirical studies to measure social contacts can be performed. These projections have started to be used to inform modelling (Chang et al., 2018; Fonnesbeck et al., 2018; Ozella et al., 2018). Having said that, as the projected contact matrices are derived from assumptions about the social structure in countries for which contact data are unavailable, these projected contact patterns cannot in most cases be directly validated. Moreover, future studies should be conducted to study the changes in social structures of countries that have experienced significant demographic changes; we would expect that as a country, like Cambodia, would have different mixing patterns as it continues to develop. In addition, interactions between individuals are also influenced by the day of the week—weekday versus weekend—and the professions of the individuals— due to exposure to the public. The surveillance data collected during the Zika outbreak provided the ability to study how ZIKV transmitted over long distances which is important for other diseases in particular those involving vectors. Moreover, several studies have shown the movements of cases have increased the spread and reach of mosquito-borne diseases ((Barmak et al., 2016; Cosner et al., 2009; Stoddard et al., 2013, 2009) for dengue and (Wesolowski et al., 2012) for malaria). Concluding remarks

The chapters in this thesis describe statistical methodologies developed to incorporate location-stratified social contact and spatial data to study the geographicalrelated exposures for infectious disease transmission. I hope that it will have the potential to contribute meaningfully to utilisation of spatial techniques when understanding the transmission of infectious diseases and supplement epidemiological analyses to inform location-specific interventions for infectious diseases.
