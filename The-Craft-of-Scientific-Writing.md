# The Craft of Scientific Writing
### A working guide for building a compelling research narrative

*Written as a companion for someone who knows their science deeply but freezes at the blank page. Read it once end to end, then keep it nearby and return to the sections you need. Annotate it. Argue with it. It is meant to be used, not admired.*

---

## 0. Read this first: why your mind goes numb

You are not bad at writing. You are experiencing the single most common failure mode of expert writers, and it has a name.

Steven Pinker calls it the **curse of knowledge** (*The Sense of Style*, 2014): once you understand something deeply, you can no longer imagine *not* understanding it. Everything in your head is connected to everything else. When you sit down to write, your mind offers you the whole web at once — every caveat, every dependency, every "but it depends" — and asks you to serialise it into a single line of prose. Of course it shuts down. You are trying to pour an ocean through a straw, and you are furious at the straw.

The paralysis is therefore not a sign that you have nothing to say. It is a sign that you have *too much*, all of it arriving simultaneously, none of it ordered. The skill of writing is not the skill of *knowing*. It is the skill of **choosing a path through what you know** and walking the reader down it one step at a time.

Three reframes to carry through everything below:

1. **Writing is thinking, not transcription.** You are not copying finished thoughts out of your head. The thoughts become finished *by* being written. Expect the first attempt to be confused — that is the drafting doing its job, not a verdict on your ability.

2. **The reader interprets; you do not control.** Gopen and Swan's foundational point (*The Science of Scientific Writing*, 1990) is that the meaning of a sentence is not what you *intend* but what the reader *reconstructs*. Any passage can mean many things to many readers. Your craft is to narrow that gap by putting information where readers already expect to find it.

3. **Your job is to make the reader's job easy.** This is Joshua Schimel's refrain (*Writing Science*, 2012). Every sentence either reduces or increases the reader's mental load. Good writing is a long series of small mercies extended to a tired, distracted, sceptical reader.

Hold those three ideas and most of the rest is technique.

## 1. The one mental model that changes everything

Before any structure or rule, install this: **you are managing a reader's expectations, moment by moment.**

Readers do not absorb prose passively. They are constantly predicting what comes next, and interpreting what they read in the light of those predictions. When you meet their expectations, they spend their energy on your *ideas*. When you violate them, they burn that energy untangling your *structure* instead — and they have less left over to be persuaded.

This is why the same facts, in a different order, can read as brilliant or as impenetrable. Gopen and Swan showed that the difficulty of scientific prose is usually **structural, not conceptual**: complexity of thought need not produce impenetrability of expression. That is genuinely good news for you. It means your writing problem is largely a *placement* problem, and placement can be learned.

Everything that follows is really one instruction applied at four scales — whole paper, section, paragraph, sentence:

> **Put old/familiar information before new information. Put the point where the reader expects the point.**

## 2. Structure from the top down: the paper is a story

### 2.1 IMRaD is a rule; OCAR is the principle

You already know IMRaD (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion). But IMRaD is just a *container*. What makes a paper compelling is the **story** inside the container, and stories have a deeper shape.

Schimel's framework is **OCAR**:

| Element | What it is | Where it lives in IMRaD |
|---|---|---|
| **O** — Opening | The larger problem, the "characters," why anyone should care | First part of the Introduction |
| **C** — Challenge | The specific unresolved question this paper attacks | End of the Introduction |
| **A** — Action | What you did and what you found | Methods + Results |
| **R** — Resolution | How the challenge is answered and how our understanding has changed | Discussion + Conclusion |

Schimel's line is worth memorising: **OCAR is principle, IMRaD is rule.** Journals impose IMRaD; good writers impose OCAR on top of it. A reader should be able to read only your Opening, Challenge, and Resolution and still grasp the whole point of the paper.

### 2.2 The hourglass

OCAR has a shape: an **hourglass** (some call it a diamond stacked on its reflection).

```
   \        broad: the big problem, the field        /     ← Opening (general)
    \                                               /
     \        narrowing to your specific gap       /       ← Challenge (specific)
      \___________________________________________/
      /            your study: methods,            \
     /              results — the neck               \      ← Action (most specific)
    /                                                 \
   /        widening back out: what it means,          \   ← Resolution (general again)
  /          how the field has changed                  \
```

Two rules fall out of this picture, both from Schimel, both easy to violate:

- **The Resolution should return to the Opening.** A good story is circular: you end where you began, but the world has changed. If your Introduction opened on dengue burden in Southeast Asia, your Discussion should close by showing how your work changes what we can say about dengue burden in Southeast Asia — not drift off into some unrelated methodological cul-de-sac.
- **The width of O and R should match.** If your Opening promises to transform the field (wide O) and your Resolution offers a modest technical tweak (narrow R), you have **overpromised and underdelivered** — the reviewer's least favourite experience. If O is narrow and R is wide, you are *underselling* genuine contribution. Calibrate the two ends to each other.

### 2.3 Why your advisor says "Methods and Results first"

Your boss is giving you excellent advice, and here is *why* it works, so you can trust it rather than merely obey it:

- **The Action is the part you know cold.** You did the work. Writing Methods and Results is mostly reporting, not arguing. It is the lowest-activation-energy way to get words on the page and break the blank-page spell.
- **You cannot frame a Challenge until you know your Resolution.** The Introduction's job is to set up exactly the question your results answer. But you only *know* what question they answer once you have written the results down and seen their shape. Writing the Introduction first is trying to build a doorway before you know the size of the room.
- **The Introduction is the hardest section and should be written when you are strongest**, not when you are staring at nothing. Write it late, when the paper has told you what it is about.

A drafting order that reliably works: **Figures/Tables → Results → Methods → Discussion → Introduction → Abstract → Title.** (Yes, the Abstract and Title come near the end. They are distillations; you cannot distil what does not yet exist.)

## 3. The Introduction, move by move (the funnel you described)

You described the funnel intuitively — general topic, then the gap, then your work filling it. There is a precise, teachable formalisation of exactly this: **John Swales' CARS model** — *Create A Research Space* (Swales, *Genre Analysis*, 1990; and Swales & Feak, *Academic Writing for Graduate Students*). Nearly every well-written Introduction in your field is doing these three "moves," whether or not the author could name them.

### Move 1 — Establish the territory *(why this area matters)*
You claim that the general area is important, relevant, or unresolved, and you summarise what is already known.
- **Step 1 — Claim centrality:** signal that this matters. *"Dengue is the most rapidly spreading mosquito-borne viral disease…"*
- **Step 2 — Generalise about the topic:** the settled background the reader needs.
- **Step 3 — Review prior research:** who found what. Not a catalogue — a *curated* story that leans toward your gap.

### Move 2 — Establish the niche *(what is missing)*
This is the hinge of the whole Introduction, and the move weak writers skip. You argue that a *space* exists that needs filling. Swales identifies four ways to do it:
- **Counter-claim:** prior work is flawed. *"Existing contact matrices assume homogeneous mixing within age bands, which…"*
- **Indicate a gap:** something has not been done. *"However, income-related heterogeneity in contact patterns remains largely unquantified in Southeast Asian settings."*
- **Raise a question:** a puzzle prior work leaves open.
- **Continue a tradition:** extend a promising line of work.

The niche is signalled by a **contrastive turn** — *however, yet, despite, remains unclear, has not been addressed, few studies*. That turn is the emotional pivot of your Introduction: it creates the tension the rest of the paper will relieve.

### Move 3 — Occupy the niche *(your work fills it)*
Now, and only now, do you enter.
- **Announce the present work:** *"Here, we develop income-stratified contact matrices and embed them in an age-structured SEIR framework to…"*
- **State principal findings** (in high-level venues) or purpose.
- **Signpost the structure**, if the venue expects it.

**The craft point:** Move 2 must set up Move 3 like a lock sets up a key. The gap you name should be *precisely* the gap your study fills — no wider (or you overpromise), no narrower (or your contribution looks trivial). When your Introduction feels weak, it is almost always because Move 2 is vague or missing, so Move 3 has nothing to answer.

> **Exercise:** Take three papers you admire in your field. For each Introduction, write "M1 / M2 / M3" in the margin where each move begins, and underline the single sentence that is the niche (Move 2). You will start to see the machinery, and then you will be unable to un-see it. This is the reading-for-structure skill of §7 in miniature.

## 4. Warranting your arguments

You asked specifically about *warranting* arguments — a precise term, and the heart of persuasion. The best tool here is the **Toulmin model** (Stephen Toulmin, *The Uses of Argument*, 1958). It breaks any argument into six parts. The first three are essential; the last three make arguments *rigorous*, which is exactly the register science demands.

| Part | Question it answers | In a modelling paper |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | What do you want me to believe? | *"Income-stratified mixing materially changes projected attack rates."* |
| **Grounds / Data** | What is the evidence? | Your simulation outputs, fit statistics, empirical contact data. |
| **Warrant** | Why does that evidence support that claim? | The (often unstated) assumption linking data to claim: *that the contact structure you estimated is a valid proxy for transmission-relevant contact.* |
| **Backing** | Why should I trust the warrant itself? | Prior validation of contact-survey methods; the mechanistic basis of the next-generation matrix. |
| **Qualifier** | How strongly does the claim hold? | *"under the assumption of density-dependent transmission," "in high-transmission settings."* |
| **Rebuttal** | When might it *not* hold? | *"If reporting rates differ systematically by income, the estimated effect may be…"* |

Three lessons that will immediately sharpen your Discussion:

1. **The warrant is usually the weakest, least-examined link — so make it explicit.** Grounds ("here is my data") and claims ("here is my conclusion") are visible. The warrant — the *assumption that lets the data speak to the claim* — is where reviewers attack and where honest scientists lose sleep. Structural non-identifiability, a proxy standing in for an unobservable, a modelling assumption doing quiet load-bearing work: these are all warrant problems. State them. A stated warrant reads as rigour; an unstated one, once spotted, reads as sleight of hand.

2. **Qualifiers are strength, not weakness.** Novice writers fear hedges make them look unsure. In science the opposite is true: an unqualified claim reads as naïve, and a well-placed qualifier reads as command of the evidence. The skill is *calibration* — matching the confidence of your language to the strength of your grounds. Reserve strong verbs (*demonstrates, establishes*) for strong evidence; use *suggests, is consistent with, indicates* when the grounds are partial. Over-hedging (*it might possibly be tentatively suggested that…*) is as much a failure as over-claiming.

3. **Pre-empt the rebuttal; do not wait to be ambushed.** The Limitations paragraph is not a confession extracted under duress — it is you performing the Toulmin rebuttal *on your own behalf*, which demonstrates that you understand your argument better than your critic does. Name the threat, then bound it: *"Although X could bias the estimate, the direction of bias is conservative because…"* Schimel's advice on limitations is apt — address them with enough confidence that the reader trusts your judgement, without so much hand-wringing that you talk yourself out of your own result.

The Discussion is where argument lives. A clean pattern for each Discussion paragraph: **restate a finding (claim + grounds) → interpret it (warrant) → connect it to prior work and the big picture → bound it (qualifier/rebuttal).**

## 5. Paragraphs and sentences: the engine room

This is where "my writing feels clunky" is actually solved. The principles below are mostly Gopen & Swan's, and they are the most immediately *actionable* thing in this whole guide.

### 5.1 The paragraph

- **One paragraph, one idea.** If you cannot write a one-sentence summary of a paragraph, it is doing two jobs; split it.
- **Topic sentence first.** The reader should know the paragraph's point from its first sentence, then watch you support it. Do not save the point for the end like a punchline — this is science, not a mystery novel (Schimel).
- **Old-to-new across sentences (the "known–new contract").** Begin each sentence with something the reader already has (a link back), and end it with the new thing you are adding. The new thing then becomes the "old" hook the next sentence links to. This is what *flow* actually is, mechanically. Choppy prose is usually prose where every sentence starts with new information and the reader keeps losing the thread.

### 5.2 The sentence — Gopen & Swan's reader-expectation principles

These are not arbitrary style rules; each one exploits a fixed habit of how readers process English.

1. **Keep subject and verb close together.** Readers hold their breath from the subject until the verb arrives; anything long jammed between them reads as an interruption and drains emphasis. *"The model, which incorporates income quintiles, seasonal forcing, and age structure, and was fitted using cmdstanR, predicts…"* makes the reader wait 12 words for the verb. Move the machinery out: *"The model predicts… It incorporates income quintiles, seasonal forcing, and age structure, and was fitted using cmdstanR."*

2. **Put the emphasis where the reader expects emphasis — at the end.** The end of a sentence is the **stress position**, the point of natural reading emphasis (it coincides with the moment the sentence resolves). Put the word or idea you most want remembered *there*, not buried in the middle. Weak: *"Attack rates rose sharply in the lowest income quintile, according to our simulations."* Stronger: *"Our simulations show attack rates rising sharply — most sharply in the lowest income quintile."*

3. **Put the "whose-story-is-this" element at the start — the topic position.** The reader assumes the grammatical subject at the front tells them whose story the sentence is. If you keep switching what sits in that slot, the reader keeps re-orienting. Decide who your characters are (the pathogen? the population? the model? the estimate?) and keep them in the topic position across a passage so the story has a consistent protagonist.

4. **Put the action in the verb.** English readers look for the action of a sentence in its verb. When you bury the action in a noun (*"we performed an estimation of R₀"* instead of *"we estimated R₀"*), the sentence goes limp. These buried-action nouns are called **nominalisations** — *-tion, -ment, -ance, -ing* words that used to be verbs. Hunt them: *"the identification of," "the implementation of," "a comparison was made."* Turn them back into verbs and the prose comes alive and shortens by a third.

5. **Context before novelty.** Never ask the reader to hold a new fact before you have told them where to put it. Give the frame, then the picture.

6. **Match structural emphasis to substantive importance.** The most important idea should sit in the most emphatic *place*, not just be asserted to be important. Structure carries meaning; use it deliberately.

Gopen and Swan are careful, and so should you be: **these are not laws.** Any of them can be broken to good effect. They are defaults — where to place things *unless you have a reason not to*. The value is that once they are conscious, you can *choose*, instead of guessing and feeling that the result is "clunky" without knowing why.

## 6. Language and tone

- **Prefer plain words to grand ones.** *Use*, not *utilise*. *Before*, not *prior to*. Complexity should live in your ideas, never in your vocabulary. Grand words are camouflage, and experienced readers read them as camouflage.
- **Be concrete and credible.** Schimel ties credibility to concreteness: facts, figures, mechanisms. Buzzwords and hype ("novel," "cutting-edge," "paradigm-shifting") do the opposite — they signal that the substance may be thin. Let the numbers carry the weight.
- **Active and passive both have jobs.** The old "always use active voice" rule is too blunt. Use **active** when the *doer* matters or you want energy (*"We fitted the model…"*). Use **passive** when the *doer* is irrelevant or the *object* is the true topic of the passage (*"Samples were incubated at 37 °C"* — nobody cares who incubated them). Choose by what belongs in the topic position (principle 3 above), not by superstition.
- **Cut ruthlessly, but second.** First get the idea down; *then* condense. Schimel treats condensing as its own pass. On a revision pass, try to remove 10% of the words without losing meaning — you almost always can, and the prose tightens every time.
- **Register: confident, precise, honest.** Scientific tone is not stiff or self-effacing. It is the voice of someone who knows exactly what they found, exactly how far it generalises, and is telling you both without drama. Calibrated confidence (see §4) *is* the tone.
- **One term, one meaning.** In science, do not chase "elegant variation." If it is the *incidence rate*, call it the incidence rate every time — not "the rate," then "the frequency," then "the level." Synonyms that read as sophisticated in an essay read as ambiguous in a model.

## 7. Reading like a writer: how to mine a paper for what you need

You asked for this explicitly, and it is a genuinely separable skill from reading for *content*. Most researchers read only for findings. To *learn to write*, you must also read for **moves** — the rhetorical machinery underneath the prose. Here is a concrete protocol.

### 7.1 Read the skeleton before the flesh
On a first pass, read only: Title → Abstract → all section headings → first sentence of every paragraph → figures and their captions. This alone reveals the paper's OCAR arc and often tells you 80% of what you need. If the first-sentences-only pass reads as a coherent summary, the author has strong topic sentences — study them.

### 7.2 Reverse-outline it
Go paragraph by paragraph and write, in the margin, the *function* of each — not what it says, but what it *does*: "establishes territory," "names the gap," "justifies the estimator," "reports main result," "pre-empts the reporting-bias objection." A reverse outline exposes the load-bearing structure. Do this to good papers to learn; do it to *your own drafts* to find where the argument sags. (When your own reverse outline reads as a jumble, that — not the sentences — is what is broken.)

### 7.3 Tag the three funnel moves and the argument spine
In the Introduction, mark M1/M2/M3 (§3). In the Discussion, for the main claims, mark claim / grounds / warrant / qualifier (§4). You are training yourself to *see* structure so you can *build* it.

### 7.4 Keep a swipe file
Steal *sentence frames*, never content. When you find a sentence that does a job cleanly — naming a gap, hedging a claim, transitioning from method to result — copy the *skeleton* into a running note in your vault:
- *"Although prior work has established ___, it remains unclear whether ___."* (niche)
- *"Taken together, these results suggest ___, though ___ cannot be ruled out."* (calibrated conclusion)
- *"To our knowledge, this is the first study to ___."* (contribution — use sparingly and truthfully)

Over a year this becomes a personal phrasebook of the *rhetoric of your field*, and blank-page paralysis loses much of its power because you are never truly starting from nothing.

### 7.5 Read one paper as a writer for every ten you read as a scientist
You do not have time to do this for everything. Pick the papers whose *writing* you envy, and dissect those. Model the ones who write the way you want to write.

## 8. A practical workflow to beat the blank page

The paralysis is real, so here is a sequence engineered to defeat it. The governing principle: **separate the jobs.** Trying to generate ideas, order them, phrase them elegantly, and correct them *all at once* is what overloads you. Do them one at a time.

1. **Talk before you write.** Explain the paper's point to a colleague, or out loud to yourself, or into a voice memo, as if to an intelligent friend outside your subfield. Speech bypasses the paralysis because you already know how to talk. Transcribe the good bits — you now have raw material.

2. **Build the skeleton first.** Before prose, write the paper as a bulleted reverse outline: one line per paragraph stating that paragraph's *job* and its single point. Argue with the skeleton until the *logic* is right. Fixing structure is cheap in bullet form and expensive in prose.

3. **Draft ugly, on purpose.** Anne Lamott's "shitty first drafts": the first draft exists only to *exist*. Lower the bar to the floor — write badly, fast, without editing, following the skeleton. You cannot revise a blank page; you can always revise a bad one. The internal editor that freezes you is *useful later and poisonous now* — send it out of the room.

4. **Draft in the low-activation order** (§2.3): figures → results → methods → discussion → intro → abstract → title.

5. **Revise in passes, each with one job:**
   - **Structure pass:** Is the OCAR arc intact? Does each paragraph do one job (reverse-outline your own draft)? Move whole blocks.
   - **Paragraph pass:** Topic sentences first? Old-to-new flow between sentences?
   - **Sentence pass:** Apply the Gopen & Swan principles (§5.2) — subjects near verbs, actions in verbs, points in stress positions.
   - **Word pass:** Kill nominalisations, jargon, and 10% of the words. Check calibration of every claim.

6. **Read it aloud.** Your ear catches what your eye forgives — the run-on, the missing link, the clause that makes you gasp for breath. If you stumble reading it, the reader will stumble too.

7. **Let it rest, then return cold.** A day away restores enough distance to read your own prose as a stranger would — which is the only way to see it honestly.

Do not expect any single draft to be good. Expect the *process* to be good. Quality is what revision deposits, layer by layer.

## 9. Field-specific notes: writing at the crossroads of maths, stats, computer science, epidemiology, and public health

Your field is unusually demanding to write *for*, because it draws on several literatures whose readers do not overlap. A statistician wants your likelihood; an epidemiologist wants the transmission mechanism; a public-health reader wants the decision implication; a reviewer wants to reproduce you. Writing well here is partly writing for *all of them at once* without boring or losing any of them.

### 9.1 Write for a reader who lacks *one* of your fields
Your reader is expert — but rarely in every discipline you are combining. Define terms that are standard in one field but not another (a "next-generation matrix," a "posterior predictive check," a "WAIFW matrix" each mystifies half your audience). The rule: **define a term the first time you use it, once, briefly, then use it consistently.** Do not condescend, but do not assume the union of all expertises in one head.

### 9.2 Equations are sentences — make them grammatical
- Introduce, then display, then interpret every equation. Never let an equation float in unannounced.
- **Define every symbol** at first use, with units. Undefined notation is the single most common reason a methods section becomes unreadable to a reader from a neighbouring field.
- Integrate the equation into the grammar of the surrounding sentence (it should read aloud as a clause), and put the *interpretation* — what the equation buys you — in the stress position after it.
- Keep notation consistent across the whole paper and supplement. If \(\beta\) is the transmission rate on page 3, it is not the shape parameter on page 9.

### 9.3 Uncertainty is not a caveat — it is the finding
In transmission modelling, a point estimate without its uncertainty is not a partial result; it is a misleading one. Reporting guidelines for the field (e.g. **EPIFORGE 2020**, Pollett et al., *PLOS Medicine*) put presentation *and explanation* of uncertainty at the centre of good reporting. Show intervals, show how they were obtained, and — crucially — say *what drives* them (parameter uncertainty, structural uncertainty, stochasticity). Distinguish these sources in words, because they have different meanings for a decision-maker. Forecasts that break down over longer horizons should be presented as such, not quietly truncated.

### 9.4 A model is an argument, not an oracle
Write in the register of "all models are wrong, some are useful." Be explicit about:
- **Structure and assumptions:** what the model includes, excludes, and *why*. The exclusions are often where the reviewer's interest lives.
- **Identifiability:** if parameters trade off (the R₀/ascertainment or collinearity problems you know well), *say so* — this is a warrant-level disclosure (§4), and stating it is a mark of seriousness, not weakness.
- **What the model can and cannot support.** Separate cleanly: "the model implies X" (a statement about your equations) from "the world will do X" (a claim about reality). Reviewers and, worse, journalists conflate these; your prose should make the conflation impossible.

### 9.5 Reproducibility is part of the writing
For computational and Bayesian work, the paper is not complete without the means to rerun it. State software and versions (cmdstanR/CmdStan pins matter and drift), priors and their justification, convergence diagnostics (R-hat, ESS, divergences), and data/code availability. Frameworks like EPIFORGE and the MInD-Healthcare reporting framework exist precisely because reproducibility, assumption transparency, and uncertainty handling were found to vary wildly across published models. Treat the relevant checklist for your paper type as a *drafting aid*, not a submission chore — running through it early tells you what your Methods must contain.

### 9.6 The equity dimension (your thesis) demands extra care
Because your work introduces heterogeneity and equity framing, your writing carries interpretive responsibility others' may not:
- **Justify each stratification.** Why *income* quintiles? Why *these* boundaries? A stratifying variable is a modelling choice with ethical weight; name the rationale, not just the mechanics.
- **Be precise about what a disparity in your output *means*.** A model can generate an inequitable projection from biased inputs (e.g. differential ascertainment). Distinguish "the population experiences unequal burden" from "our data-generating assumptions produce unequal estimates." Conflating them can misdirect policy.
- **Write so results cannot be weaponised.** Findings about heterogeneous groups can be misread as blaming those groups. Frame mechanisms (exposure, contact structure, access) rather than attributes, and state the framing explicitly.

### 9.7 Figures are arguments too
In modelling papers the central figure often *is* the result. Design each figure to make one point, legible without the caption; then write a caption that states that point (topic sentence for the figure), defines every element, and gives the reader what they need to interpret it unaided. A reader should be able to reconstruct your paper's spine from its figures alone.

### 9.8 Translate for the decision-maker
Much modelling exists to inform action. If the venue or the work calls for it, include a plain, quantified statement of the practical implication — what a public-health reader could *do* differently — separated clearly from the technical claims, and honestly qualified. This is the top of the hourglass returning to the ground where the problem started.

## 10. A curated shelf (the erudite sources, annotated)

Read these in roughly this order. The first two will change your writing within a week.

- **George Gopen & Judith Swan — "The Science of Scientific Writing"** (*American Scientist*, 1990). ~9 pages, free online. The single highest-return thing you can read. The reader-expectation principles of §5 in full, with worked before/after examples. Read it three times a year.
- **Joshua Schimel — *Writing Science*** (Oxford, 2012). The book to own. Story structure (OCAR/LDR), the funnel, making writing "sticky" (his SUCCES heuristic: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories), condensing, and — valuable for you — a whole chapter on writing limitations and one on writing "global science" for non-native-English contexts.
- **John Swales & Christine Feak — *Academic Writing for Graduate Students*** (Michigan). The CARS model of §3 and the genre-analysis mindset of §7, as a practical course. The reference for reading papers *as genre*.
- **Steven Pinker — *The Sense of Style*** (2014). The curse of knowledge (§0), and a linguist's case for "classic style" — writing as showing the reader something in the world, not performing your own cleverness.
- **Joseph Williams & Joseph Bizup — *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*.** The deepest treatment of the sentence: cohesion, the known–new contract, nominalisations, and how "clarity" is a property of reader experience, not a mystical gift.
- **William Zinsser — *On Writing Well*.** Not about science, but the finest sustained argument for clarity, warmth, and cutting clutter. Read for the *ear*.
- **Strunk & White — *The Elements of Style*.** Dated in places, but "omit needless words" earns its fame. A short annual refresher.
- **Helen Sword — *Stylish Academic Writing* / *The Writer's Diet*.** Evidence that academic prose need not be turgid, plus a diagnostic for flabby sentences (heavy on nominalisations and prepositions).
- **Field-specific:** **EPIFORGE 2020** (Pollett et al., *PLOS Medicine* 2021) for forecasting/prediction reporting; the **MInD-Healthcare** framework and the **ODD protocol** (Grimm et al.) for describing models transparently. Read these for *what must be reported*; read the general books for *how to report it well*.

## 11. One-page cheat sheet (pin this above your desk)

**When you freeze:** you have too much to say, not too little. Pick one path. Talk it out. Draft ugly.

**Order of drafting:** figures → results → methods → discussion → intro → abstract → title.

**Whole paper (OCAR / hourglass):** Opening (broad, why care) → Challenge (the specific gap) → Action (what you did/found) → Resolution (return to the opening, world changed). Match the width of O and R.

**Introduction (CARS):** M1 establish territory → M2 name the gap (*however…*) → M3 your work fills *exactly* that gap.

**Every argument (Toulmin):** claim + grounds + **make the warrant explicit** + qualifier calibrated to the evidence + pre-empt the rebuttal yourself.

**Paragraph:** one idea; point in the first sentence; old-to-new between sentences.

**Sentence (Gopen & Swan):** subject next to verb · action in the verb (kill nominalisations) · new/important info at the end (stress position) · consistent topic at the start · context before novelty.

**Words:** plain over grand · concrete over hype · one term one meaning · cut 10%.

**Field:** define cross-discipline terms once · every symbol defined with units · uncertainty *is* the finding · model-implies ≠ world-does · justify every stratification · report enough to reproduce.

**Revise in single-job passes, read aloud, sleep on it.**

**The whole guide in one line:** *make the reader's job easy — put old before new, and put the point where the reader expects the point.*

*You already have the rarest ingredient: something true and non-obvious to say. Everything above is just a set of reliable ways to get it out of your head and into someone else's with the least loss along the way. The craft is learnable, it compounds, and it will become — as you hoped — your long-term edge. Write badly, then revise well. Begin.*
