Summary

Aedes-borne diseases are a major contributor to human mortality and morbidity worldwide. The past 50 years have witnessed an unprecedented expansion of Aedes-borne diseases, most notably dengue, Zika, yellow fever, and chikungunya. Due to the ongoing climate change and urban growth, it is anticipated that both Aedes aegypti and Ae. albopictus will continue to expand their geographical range, thereby further increasing the global health burden. In this thesis, I aim to use mathematical and statistical modelling to generate new insights and knowledge about the epidemiology and ecology of Aedes-borne diseases as well as the impact of Aedes control measures on disease transmission at global and metropolitan scales. In my first data chapter, I developed a mathematical modelling framework integrating travel, demographic, climate, entomological, and human case data and identified a total of 24 locations at a high risk of cryptic Zika virus spread during the 2015—2016 global epidemic. Notably, some of these locations have recently reported their first Zika outbreaks in history, which add to the credibility of our model results and highlight the importance of continued investments in Zika virus research and surveillance systems to prepare for future epidemics. Next, I examined how different environmental and anthropogenic variables influence the spatio-temporal variation of Aedes abundance in Singapore’s high-rise public residential zones. My results showed building age to be the strongest predictor, and contrary to prior expectations, none of the weather variables were found to have a substantial impact on Aedes abundance. The evidence generated from this study can be used to guide the refinement of Aedes control strategies in Singapore. In my final data chapter, I developed an agent-based model to study the short-term and long-term impacts of sustained Aedes control in a wide range of dengue endemic settings. I showed here that in the absence of a highly effective intervention technology that is able to reduce the basic reproduction number to below one, a fixed level of reduction in the Aedes abundance would only cause temporary reduction in dengue transmission. Furthermore, the time point at which interventions cease to be effective is strongly influenced not only by the intensity of vector control and the pre-intervention transmission intensity, but also by the individual-level heterogeneity in biting risk. The insights obtained will be useful to inform future model-based studies that seek to predict the impact of dengue vector control in their local contexts.

Epidemiology of Aedes-borne diseases

Dengue

Dengue virus (DENV) is a single-stranded RNA virus belonging to the Flaviviridae family [1]. The virus is mainly transmitted by Aedes aegypti, and to a lesser extent, Ae. albopictus [2]. There are five antigenically distinct serotypes (DENV-1 to DENV-5) discovered to date, with DENV-5 being a new serotype that is maintained in a sylvatic transmission cycle and has yet to be found circulating among humans [3]. Most dengue infections are clinically inapparent (i.e., either asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic), and these hard-to-detect infections have been estimated to contribute to over 80% of DENV transmission [4]. Individuals with apparent symptomatic infections experience high fever, accompanied by other symptoms such as severe headache, vomiting, muscle and joint pains, and rash [2]. Some patients may further develop potentially fatal complications upon defervescence, such as thrombocytopenia, hemorrhage, shock, and major organ failure [2]. No specific treatment is available for dengue, and standardized clinical management is vital for reducing the case fatality rate [5].

Global burden

Globally, an estimated 105 (95% CI: 95—114) million dengue infections occur each year, of which over half are concentrated in South and Southeast Asia [6]. Both of these regions play an important role in seeding viral lineages to the other regions of the world such as Latin America and the Caribbean [7,8], where dengue burden is also high [9]. Sporadic autochthonous cases have been reported in southern Europe [10,11], southern United States [12], and northern Queensland in Australia [13], although sustained local transmission is not currently present. In addition, the frequency of dengue outbreaks has been rising in the Eastern Mediterranean Region [2], and recently the first autochthonous dengue cases in Afghanistan have been detected [14]. To date, little is known about the dengue burden in sub-Saharan Africa due to the lack of diagnostic capacities, but returning traveler reports and other supplementary evidence suggest that DENV transmission is present in most countries within the region [15].

Immune interactions between dengue serotypes

Primary dengue infection is generally thought to create strong immunity against the infecting serotype and temporary cross-immunity against the other serotypes [16–18]. Despite being uncommon, homologous reinfection can occur and may result in symptomatic disease [19,20]. Upon subsequent infection with a heterologous serotype, disease symptoms tend to be more severe than primary infection [21–23]. This phenomenon has been hypothesized to be predominantly caused by antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE), whereby cross-reactive antibodies are unable to neutralize the virus of a different serotype but instead assist with viral entry into Fc receptor-bearing cells such as dendritic cells, macrophages, and monocytes, thus increasing the severity of infection [24,25]. ADE has also been argued to be responsible for the development of dengue hemorrhagic fever in infants who had previously received anti-DENV antibodies from their mothers [26]. Besides ADE, other proposed mechanisms underlying the enhanced disease severity in secondary infections include the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines by cross-reactive memory T cells that ultimately leads to plasma leakage [27]. Previous studies have also shown that certain sequences of infections (e.g., DENV-1 followed by DENV-2) can result in a higher risk of clinical illness [28], and moderate antigenic variation exists within each serotype, which may play a role in determining disease severity [29]. After a secondary dengue infection, the antibody response becomes broadly neutralizing and has been shown to significantly decrease the risk of disease during tertiary and quaternary infections [30]. However, it is still unclear to what extent secondary infection can prevent subsequent infections from occurring instead of solely protecting against clinical disease [31]. Likewise, uncertainty remains with regard to the nature of transient cross-immunity obtained following a primary infection [31]. While there is general agreement on the clinical significance of immunological serotype interactions, little consensus has been reached as to how these interactions affect the incidence patterns of dengue [31]. Both ADE and cross-immunity have been put forward in earlier studies as important drivers of dengue’s complex transmission dynamics [32–34]. However, results of Recker et al. suggest that ADE alone can produce the interepidemic periods and desynchronized oscillations of the four dengue serotypes observed in real-world settings, provided its effects are decomposed into two forms of enhancement: increased transmissibility during secondary infection and increased susceptibility to secondary infection [35]. More recently, Lourenco & Recker used a spatially explicit individual-based model to demonstrate that stochasticity underlying the interactions between hosts and vectors alone can desynchronize the serotypes and produce multi-annual cycles of dengue

outbreaks even in the absence of immune interactions, suggesting that ADE and cross-immunity are unlikely to be the sole mechanisms underlying the epidemiological dynamics of dengue [36]. Estimating the strengths of these immune interactions, however, can be highly challenging, and more research is required to address this knowledge gap, which has important implications for vaccine impact predictions [37].

Zika

Zika virus (ZIKV) was first isolated from a rhesus monkey in the Zika Forest of Uganda in 1947 [38], and the first human infections were detected five years later [39]. The virus is primarily transmitted by the Aedes mosquitoes, but can also spread from mother to fetus or through sexual contacts [40–43]. Although ZIKV has been shown by early serological studies to be endemic in a number of African and Asian countries, it had received little attention for 60 years since its discovery owing to the paucity of reported cases and the selflimiting nature of most infections [40]. In 2007, ZIKV expanded to Yap Island in the Federated States of Micronesia and caused the first recorded Zika outbreak in history [44]. Thereafter, another outbreak was reported in French Polynesia during 2013—2014, where severe complications such as Guillain-Barré syndrome in adults and central nervous system malformations in neonates were first documented [45]. In May 2015, a large Zika outbreak was reported in Brazil, and a few months later the virus quickly spread across Latin America and the Caribbean region [40]. In February 2016, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared the association of ZIKV infection with cases of microcephaly and Guillain-Barré syndrome to constitute a Public Health Emergency of International Concern [40]. Although the number of Zika cases has dramatically declined after 2016 [46], much remains to be understood concerning the virus’s epidemiology. To date, a lot of progress has been made in understanding ZIKV’s phylogenetic history [47], the association between microcephaly and ZIKV infection [48], neurodevelopment in infants with prenatal ZIKV exposure [49], as well as the asymptomatic proportion of ZIKV infections [50]. Previous studies have also generated important insights into the potential epidemiological impact of cross-reactivity between ZIKV and DENV. For example, using surveillance data from Brazil and Colombia, Borchering et al. demonstrated that ZIKV infection may have conferred cross-protection against DENV and thus resulted in the unusually low dengue incidence in 2017 in both countries [51]. By contrast, Katzelnick et al. studied a long-standing pediatric dengue cohort in Nicaragua and found intermediate anti-ZIKV antibody titers to increase the future risk of dengue disease [52]. It has also been hypothesized that cross-reactive anti-DENV antibodies may play an important role in enhancing the

pathogenicity of ZIKV infections [53]. Although enhancement of ZIKV by anti-DENV antibodies has been observed in vitro, convincing evidence in vivo is not yet available [53]. Besides immunological interactions between ZIKV and DENV, recent studies have also found rapid waning of anti-ZIKV antibodies following the epidemic [54–56], suggesting that ZIKV infection may not confer lifelong immunity. The duration of ZIKV immunity, however, is still unclear and requires further research. Another important yet unresolved question is in which locations ZIKV may have circulated without being detected during the recent global epidemic. Addressing this research gap will have important implications for improving outbreak preparedness and response.

Other Aedes-borne diseases

In addition to dengue and Zika, other Aedes-borne diseases include yellow fever, chikungunya, and Mayaro fever. Like DENV and ZIKV, yellow fever virus (YFV) is a flavivirus [57], in comparison to chikungunya virus (CHIKV) and Mayaro virus (MAYV), which are alphaviruses belonging to the Togaviridae family [58]. The clinical symptoms of the diseases caused by these arboviruses are highly similar especially in the early phase of infection [59], and hence misdiagnosis can often occur in the absence of laboratory testing. A small proportion of patients with yellow fever may develop jaundice [57], a distinct clinical symptom not shared by the other Aedes-borne diseases, and both CHIKV and MAYV infections may cause long-term arthralgia that severely impairs the quality of life [58,60]. YFV is endemic in over 40 countries in Africa and Central and South America [57], but has yet to be found circulating in Asia [61]. By contrast, the global burden of chikungunya is heavily concentrated in Asia and the Americas [62]. Most of the Mayaro fever cases reported to date have been confined to the Amazon region and were caused by spillover from the virus’s sylvatic transmission cycle involving non-human primates and the Haemagogus mosquitoes [63]. However, both Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus have been found to be able to transmit MAYV in laboratory settings [64], suggesting that the virus may one day become urbanized and pose a serious threat to global health.

Ecology of Aedes-borne diseases

Vector behavior

As the primary vector of dengue, Zika, yellow fever, and chikungunya, Ae. aegypti preferentially resides in shady areas inside human dwellings and feeds almost exclusively on humans [65,66]. The mosquito is active during the daytime [67], rendering insecticide-treated bed nets ineffective in preventing infections. Moreover, daytime biting increases the likelihood of long-distance viral transmission caused by host movement, despite the limited mosquito flight distance [68,69]. The Ae. aegypti mosquitoes are also known to oviposit in a wide range of containers, especially artificial ones such as pails, vases, and roof gutters, and their breeding sites can often be cryptically located and hard to reach [70]. In addition, studies have revealed a high incidence of insecticide resistance in the Ae. aegypti population, which creates additional challenges for vector control [71]. In contrast to Ae. aegypti, Ae. albopictus is predominantly found in rural and suburban areas, feeds on a variety of hosts, and has an overall lower level of insecticide resistance [72,73]. Nonetheless, both mosquito species are capable of migrating with humans (e.g., their eggs are drought-resistant and can travel in dry containers), which is believed to be an important mechanism for their rapid expansion worldwide [70,74].

Climatic influences on disease transmission

There are numerous pathways whereby climate can influence the transmission of Aedes-borne diseases [75]: Temperature directly affects the viral replication rate within the Aedes mosquitoes and has been demonstrated in laboratory studies to have a strong impact on the extrinsic incubation periods of DENV [76], ZIKV [77], and YFV [78]. Furthermore, a decrease in diurnal temperature range can increase the susceptibility of Ae. aegypti to DENV infection given the same mean temperature [79]. Temperature also has a significant influence on the development and survival of immature Ae. aegypti and hence the rate of adult mosquito emergence [80]. The mortality rate of adult Aedes mosquitoes is temperature-dependent as well, which not only affects mosquito abundance but also determines the proportion of infected mosquitoes that can survive long enough to become infectious [75]. In view of the important influence of adult vector survival time on arboviral transmission, Brady et al. used data collected from 351 laboratory and 59 field experiments to estimate the mortality rate of adult female Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus respectively as a function of temperature and mosquito age [81]. The result of their study can be used in a dynamic transmission model to study the spatio-temporal variation in transmission potential for different Aedes-borne diseases on a global scale [82]. Unlike temperature, precipitation mainly influences Aedes-borne disease transmission via creating vector breeding habitats [75], although excessive rainfall can lead to breeding site flushing and hence may reduce the

risk of disease outbreaks [83]. A recent study conducted in Brazil has also found extreme drought to increase the risk of dengue transmission in areas with water supply shortages, which is likely due to changes in household water storage practices in response to water scarcity [84,85]. Moreover, both precipitation and temperature can affect humidity, which in turn influences mosquito survival and frequency of biting [86]. In recent years, there has been a growing interest in developing statistical models to evaluate the added value of weather data to dengue epidemic forecasting, with mixed results obtained by previous studies [85,87–90], suggesting that the strength of association between weather and dengue incidence can be highly heterogeneous across locations [75,89]. Previous work has also developed mechanistic models to predict the Aedes population dynamics under perturbed conditions such as severe weather fluctuations and vector control [91,92]. These models are highly complex, with a lot of assumptions made about the development of Ae. aegypti and its relation to the environmental conditions [93]. Comparison of multiple modelling approaches can be useful for identifying the subsystem of each model that needs further improvement, and high-quality entomological field data are required for model calibration and validation [93]. 1.2.3 Mapping environmental suitability for Aedes-borne disease transmission Another important area of research is mapping the environmental suitability for Aedes mosquitoes and the viruses they transmit. In their seminal work, Kraemer et al. compiled the most comprehensive Aedes occurrence database currently available [94], which was then combined with a set of environmental and land-cover variables to map the global distribution of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus [95]. A similar approach was also employed by Messina et al. to estimate the global environmental suitability for ZIKV [96]. More recently, Kraemer et al. produced forecasts of the possible geographic expansion of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus within the next 60 years due to human travel, urban growth, and climate change [97], which can be used to predict the future population at risk of dengue and other Aedes-borne diseases, and to guide entomological surveillance and vector control [98]. Despite the significant progress made in predicting the global distribution of Aedes mosquitoes, the spatiotemporal dynamics of the Aedes population remains poorly understood in urbanized local settings. Longitudinal and fine-scale measurements of Aedes abundance are seldom available [99], with few studies, if any, having successfully identified key variables that explain the geographic variation of adult Aedes abundance within a

single city. Addressing this research gap will have important implications for the improvement of local vector control strategies.

Control of Aedes-borne diseases

Existing Aedes control measures

The existing Aedes control measures can be broadly classified into two categories: those affecting the aquatic stages of the mosquitoes (e.g., container larvicide treatment, source reduction, public education, and law enforcement) and those targeting the adult mosquito stages (e.g., space spraying, indoor residual spraying, and personal protection) [100]. Regrettably, there have been very few vector control programs that have successfully suppressed dengue transmission: in Singapore, sustained larval source reduction and community engagement have substantially reduced dengue incidence during 1975—1990 [101]. Although the resulting lowered herd immunity subsequently led to the resurgence of dengue transmission, the annual force of infection has remained at a low level to date [101,102]. In Cuba, an intensive Aedes eradication campaign was launched in 1981, which caused dengue transmission to be nearly eliminated throughout the country during 1982—1996 [103]. Apart from the success stories mentioned earlier, the evidence base for the effectiveness of the existing dengue vector control measures remains poor [104]. In particular, no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) examining the epidemiological impact of outdoor fogging have been found [104,105], although this approach has been widely adopted due to its high visibility [100]. While an RCT conducted in Mexico and Nicaragua has used serological data to demonstrate a significant impact of community engagement on dengue transmission [106], most trials assessing the effectiveness of community-based environmental management focused exclusively on entomological outcomes and did not generate any evidence for the reduction in dengue incidence [104]. Besides, an RCT conducted in Santiago de Cuba reported an insignificant increase in dengue incidence due to the addition of indoor residual spraying to the routine Aedes control activities [107], and similar results were also obtained by a case-control study in Taiwan [108]. These results were in contrast to the findings from an observational study in Australia, which showed a significant reduction in the odds of secondary dengue infections due to the high coverage of indoor residual spraying [13]. Taken all together, the paucity of high-

quality evidence for the effectiveness of dengue vector control [109] highlights the need for more research to be conducted to assess the epidemiological impact of different Aedes control measures in various dengue transmission settings. To date, there have been even fewer studies evaluating the effectiveness of Aedes control for the reduction of Zika or chikungunya transmission [110]. This can be primarily explained by the longer inter-epidemic period and more unpredictable epidemic timing of these diseases, which pose additional challenges to the design and implementation of intervention trials [111]. Hence, during a Zika or chikungunya epidemic, it may be reasonable to adopt Aedes control approaches that have already been proven effective at reducing dengue transmission in each particular setting. It should be noted, however, that there is one class of Aedes control measures whose epidemiological impact can only be evaluated in a Zika epidemic, namely personal protection (e.g., mosquito repellent) targeting pregnant women in the first trimester of pregnancy with the aim of reducing the incidence of congenital Zika syndrome instead of the overall level of viral transmission within the community [111]. Rigorously designed RCTs will be required in future Zika epidemics to assess the effectiveness of this approach.

Novel Aedes control measures

Recently, there has been an increasing number of novel Aedes control measures under development. One of the most promising approaches involves releasing mosquitoes carrying the wMel strain of Wolbachia pipientis to replace the wild population, which has been shown to reduce the incidence of virologically confirmed dengue (VCD) by 77% (95% CI: 65%—85%) in Yogyakarta, Indonesia in a cluster randomized trial [112]. The same approach has also been tested in other countries such as Australia, Vietnam, Brazil, and Colombia, with encouraging results reported by preliminary studies [113]. Besides blocking DENV transmission [114], the wMel strain has been demonstrated to lower the vector competence of Ae. aegypti for ZIKV, CHIKV, YFV, and MAYV as well [115–118], and more research is needed to understand the exact cellular mechanisms underlying this viral blocking effect [119]. Similar to the aforementioned Wolbachia approach, gene-drive technologies (e.g., CRISPR-Cas9) has the potential to enable prolonged suppression of pathogen transmission to be achieved in the absence of frequent releases of engineered mosquitoes [120]. In early 2020, Li et al. developed the first gene-drive system in Ae. aegypti [121], and during the same year, Buchman et al. successfully engineered the first transgene that

protects Ae. aegypti against all four dengue serotypes [122]. These studies have laid an important foundation for the future development of technologies to spread anti-dengue genes into the wild Aedes population. In addition to vector competence reduction, gene-drive systems can be used for sex ratio distortion to reduce the mosquito population, although such a transgenic method can be much more challenging to develop for Ae. aegypti in comparison to Anopheles since it does not have a distinct sex chromosome [100]. In view of the rapid advances in the development of genetically modified mosquitoes (GMMs), the WHO has recently updated its guidance framework for the testing of GMMs, where quality standards for efficacy and safety evaluations were provided and the relevant ethical and regulatory issues were discussed [123]. Besides mosquito population replacement, other promising Aedes control measures include incompatible insect technique and sterile insect technique, the combination of which has led to a 88% (95% CI: 57%—99%) reduction in dengue incidence within the core release zones compared to the control areas in a field trial conducted in Singapore [124]. A common drawback of all the Aedes control measures involving releases of engineered mosquitoes is their delayed impact on the adult vector populations [100]. Hence, the implementation of these novel approaches alone could be inadequate for reducing disease transmission in an outbreak setting, where swift deployment of reactive interventions such as indoor residual spraying may still be required [100]. 1.3.3 Design of large-scale field trials to assess Aedes control measures Given the importance of evidence-based policymaking for Aedes control, a number of studies have provided key considerations on the design of large-scale vector control trials [109,125–127]. Recommended study designs include randomized controlled before-and-after study, randomized interrupted time series, and randomized step-wedge study, and epidemiological endpoints are required to demonstrate the impact of vector control on disease transmission [109]. A longitudinal pediatric cohort may be useful to maximize transmission detection and minimize movement of trial participants between intervention and control areas, and cross-reactivity should be accounted for in estimating seroconversion rates in settings where multiple closely-related arboviruses co-circulate [125]. Besides, the determination of cluster size and cluster spacing needs to take into account the movement range of the study participants as well as the dispersal range of the engineered mosquitoes (provided they are released) to reduce spillover effects [126], and the period of follow-up needs to be sufficiently long (e.g., two or more transmission seasons) given that the incidence of Aedes-borne diseases

can be highly variable over time [109]. It should also be noted that the magnitude of reduction in disease transmission observed in a vector control trial conducted for only several years should not be equated with the long-term intervention effectiveness [111]. Unfortunately, conducting large-scale field trials over a decade or even longer is unlikely to be economically feasible especially for lowand middle-income countries. While mechanistic models can be utilized to predict the long-term effectiveness of sustained Aedes control, such studies are rare [128,129] and the current understanding of how the time-varying epidemiological impact of Aedes control changes across different transmission settings remains poor.

Vaccines

The only dengue vaccine commercially available to date is CYD-TDV, developed by Sanofi-Pasteur. The vaccine is tetravalent and has been shown to protect against severe VCD in seropositive vaccine recipients in phase-III trials [130]. However, an increased risk of severe VCD has been observed in participants who had not experienced any dengue infections prior to vaccination [130], and the WHO recommends that serological testing be conducted before the vaccine is administered [131]. One particular challenge in pre-vaccination screening is the poor sensitivity of the Immunoglobulin G (IgG) enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) test in those with a monotypic dengue profile: the benefit of the vaccine is the largest among this sub-population, and yet the cutoff value of the test cannot be further lowered to increase sensitivity without causing a sizeable number of false positives [132,133]. A more accurate, rapid, and reasonably cheap pre-vaccination screening test is urgently needed to maximize the vaccine’s public health impact [131]. Previous work has also shown that the effect of the test accuracy on the population-level benefits of vaccination depends on the transmission intensity, with specificity being more important in low-transmission settings and sensitivity more important in high-transmission settings [134]. In addition, projections of the CYDTDV’s public health impact need to account for the waning vaccine effectiveness [135] as well as the uncertainties about breakthrough infections and immune interactions between serotypes [37,136]. More recently, Biswal et al. reported data from the first part of a phase-III RCT that assesses the efficacy of a second dengue vaccine, TAK-003, developed by Takeda [137]. The vaccine was efficacious against VCD among children aged between 4 and 16 years regardless of the serostatus at baseline, although the efficacy was estimated to be slightly higher among seropositive participants [137]. Furthermore, the efficacy estimates

varied by dengue serotype, and long-term data will be critical to shed more light on this vaccine’s efficacy and safety profile [137]. The first yellow fever vaccine was developed in the 1930s [138], and to date four have been prequalified by the WHO [139]. No vaccine protecting against ZIKV or CHIKV infections is commercially available at the time of writing, but a few candidates have entered early-phase clinical trials and shown promising results [140,141].

Aims of thesis

Although significant progress has been made in understanding the epidemiology and ecology of Aedes-borne diseases as well as developing new vector control tools and vaccines, a lot of critical knowledge gaps still remain. In particular, little is known about the true geographical extent of ZIKV, which is believed to be still circulating in many locations in the tropics and subtropics without being detected. The identification of these locations has important implications for the enhancement of global Zika outbreak preparedness and response capacity. To achieve this, however, requires knowledge about the geographical areas that should be prioritized for ZIKV surveillance strengthening. In chapter 2, I aim to develop a mathematical modeling framework integrating diverse sources of information to identify geographical areas at a high risk of undetected ZIKV circulation during the 2015—2016 global epidemic. It is hoped that this work will provide valuable evidence to guide the allocation of finite public health resources to mitigate the risk of another large-scale Zika outbreak in the future. To date, numerous studies have been conducted to map the distribution of Aedes mosquitoes at the global, continental, and national scales. While substantial spatial variation in Aedes abundance also exists within the confines of a single city, its associated determinants have rarely been studied in detail. This knowledge gap hinders the development of spatially targeted interventions to reduce the Aedes population at a city scale. In addition, it remains unclear to what extent weather influences the temporal fluctuation of Aedes abundance inside and nearby residential zones in cities with a tropical climate such as Singapore, although vector control is typically intensified during the wet season. In chapter 3, I aim to build regression models using longitudinal data generated from Singapore’s Gravitrap surveillance system to assess a wide range of environmental and anthropogenic variables in their contribution to explaining the spatio-temporal variation of Ae. aegypti and Ae.

albopictus abundance in Singapore’s high-rise public residential zones. The results generated from this study can be used to guide the refinement of Aedes control strategies in Singapore. In recent years, a lot of novel technologies for Aedes control have been developed, with the potential to achieve long-term dengue elimination as demonstrated by small-scale field trials. However, large-scale implementation of these novel interventions remains extremely challenging, and it is still essential to understand how the sustained and intensive application of the currently available tools for Aedes control would impact dengue transmission both in the short and long-run. Furthermore, few studies, if any, have been conducted to identify the main drivers for the variation in vector control effectiveness across different dengue endemic settings. In chapter 4, I aim to develop an agent-based model to simulate the time-varying impact of sustained Aedes control in diverse dengue endemic settings over a 30-year time period. It is hoped that this work will generate useful insights to guide the development of Aedes control strategic plans to achieve sustained reduction in the burden of dengue.

Chapter 2: Mapping the cryptic spread of the 2015–2016 global Zika virus epidemic

This work has been accepted for publication as: Sun, H., Dickens, B.L., Jit, M. et al. Mapping the cryptic spread of the 2015–2016 global Zika virus epidemic. BMC Med 18, 399 (2020).

Abstract

Background

Zika virus (ZIKV) emerged as a global epidemic in 2015–2016 from Latin America with its true geographical extent remaining unclear due to widely presumed underreporting. The identification of locations with potential and unknown spread of ZIKV is a key yet understudied component for outbreak preparedness. Here, we aim to identify locations at a high risk of cryptic ZIKV spread during 2015–2016 to further the understanding of the global ZIKV epidemiology, which is critical for the mitigation of the risk of future epidemics.

Methods

We developed an importation simulation model to estimate the weekly number of ZIKV infections imported in each susceptible spatial unit (i.e., location that did not report any autochthonous Zika cases during 2015– 2016), integrating epidemiological, demographic, and travel data as model inputs. Thereafter, a global risk model was applied to estimate the weekly ZIKV transmissibility during 2015–2016 for each location. Finally, we assessed the risk of onward ZIKV spread following importation in each susceptible spatial unit to identify locations with a high potential for cryptic ZIKV spread during 2015–2016.

Results

We have found 24 susceptible spatial units that were likely to have experienced cryptic ZIKV spread during 2015–2016, of which 10 continue to have a high risk estimate within a highly conservative scenario, namely, Luanda in Angola, Banten in Indonesia, Maharashtra in India, Lagos in Nigeria, Taiwan and Guangdong in China, Dakar in Senegal, Maputo in Mozambique, Kinshasa in Congo DRC, and Pool in Congo. Notably, among the 24 susceptible spatial units identified, some have reported their first ZIKV outbreaks since 2017, thus adding to the credibility of our results (derived using 2015–2016 data only).

Conclusion

Our study has provided valuable insights into the potentially high-risk locations for cryptic ZIKV circulation during the 2015–2016 pandemic and has also laid a foundation for future studies that attempt to further narrow this key knowledge gap. Our modelling framework can be adapted to identify areas with likely unknown spread of other emerging vector-borne diseases, which has important implications for public health readiness especially in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Zika virus (ZIKV) is a flavivirus that is mainly transmitted by the Aedes mosquitoes [142]. Since its first isolation in a Ugandan forest in 1947 [143], the virus has until recent years only caused regional outbreaks [144,145]. In February 2016, a Public Health Emergency of International Concern was declared by the World Health Organization, as ZIKV swept through the majority of the countries in Latin America and the Caribbean, coinciding with an unusual increase in the number of microcephaly cases and other neurological disorders [40]. The reported ZIKV incidence declined substantially after 2016 [46], but in the meantime, new scientific evidence continued to emerge and reveal new locations where ZIKV circulation had never been identified before [146]. More recently, the first autochthonous Zika case in Europe was reported in October 2019 with its source remaining unknown at the time of writing [147], which underscores our limited understanding of the virus’ epidemiology. Although ZIKV is no longer a public health emergency, the potential reoccurrence of future largescale epidemics remains a concern, which necessitates continued investments in ZIKV research and surveillance in preparation for such an event [148]. To date, modelling studies have yielded important insights into the virus’ transmission dynamics [149–153], ecological niche [96,154,155], and at-risk population size [156], but little has been done to understand the gap between where cases may have already occurred and where they have been reported [157]. Due to the high proportion of ZIKV infections that are asymptomatic and its similarity in clinical presentation to other diseases such as dengue fever [144], undetected or unreported spread of ZIKV was widely presumed, especially in countries with limited public health resources [155]. The identification of these locations is critical, as their healthcare systems may be overwhelmed during possible future waves of epidemics if ill prepared, and due to the risk of onward dissemination via air travel. Whilst a recent study used travel surveillance and viral genomes data to detect an unreported ZIKV outbreak in Cuba [158], our understanding of the geographical areas that are likely to have experienced cryptic ZIKV spread is still seriously lacking at a global scale. To narrow this knowledge gap requires a novel modelling framework integrating a wide range of factors that determine the worldwide introduction of the virus and the subsequent autochthonous transmission. In this study, we aim to answer the following key questions, focusing on all countries or first-level subdivisions where no indigenous Zika cases were reported during the 2015–2016 global epidemic: (i) At least how many ZIKV infections were imported in each country or subdivision during 2015–2016 and to what extent were

imported ZIKV infections underreported? (ii) Which countries or subdivisions were most likely to experience cryptic ZIKV spread during the 2015–2016 global epidemic based on currently available data?

Methods

Data

Epidemiological data

The weekly number of reported autochthonous Zika cases during 2015–2016 for each country in the Americas was published as bar charts by the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) [159]. These data were digitized by the Andersen Lab for a genomic epidemiological study [160], and made publicly available [161]. We used the Web Plot Digitizer to extract the weekly number of notified Zika cases in Brazil during 2015 published by Lourenço et al. [162], since these data were not published by the PAHO. To achieve a higher spatial resolution, we also obtained weekly cumulative case counts for each first-level subdivision of Colombia between Eweek 38 and Eweek 52 of 2016 from the Colombian National Institute of Health’s website [163]. Each time series was then differenced to derive the weekly case counts and combined with data compiled by Siraj et al. [164], which included weekly-notified case data prior to Eweek 38 of 2016. For weekly autochthonous Zika cases reported by countries outside the Americas, we manually reviewed information compiled by HealthMap alongside additional data sources such as ReliefWeb [165,166] (refer to the Appendix A [167–171] for more details). Each reported case was located to a first-level country subdivision whenever possible, and both confirmed and suspected autochthonous cases were included in our study (Appendix A: Data S2.1). In addition to autochthonous case data, we obtained the reported total number of imported Zika cases during 2015–2016 for each US state, which was published by the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (collection of imported case data for Florida and Texas was not needed for the study, as discussed in later sections) [172]. For all the other countries or subdivisions that did not report any autochthonous cases during 2015–2016, the imported case data were collected using HealthMap [165].

Travel data

We requested the yearly average length of stay (LOS) on inbound tourism trips up to 2016 from the United Nations World Tourism Organization (UNWTO) [173]. For each country, we took the most recently available estimate for the analyses. In most cases, estimates were derived based on the check-in dates from arrival and departure cards or border survey data, but if such data were unavailable for a country, the estimate based on the Hotel Occupancy Survey was used instead [173]. In the rare event that the data were completely missing for a given country, we took the conservative approach and assumed the average LOS to be equal to the minimum value among all the countries under study, to avoid overestimating the number of imported ZIKV infections (refer to section 2.3.2.2 for more details). The monthly number of air ticket bookings during 2015–2016 was obtained from the Official Airline Guide (OAG) for every origin-destination route with up to two connections. This was used to derive the weekly number of bookings assuming the daily number of bookings was uniform within each month for each route. For each first-level country subdivision with no incoming air passengers during 2015–2016, we performed an estimation of the most likely airport (within the same country) that the population would rely on when they returned home (details shown in Appendix A [174]). The subdivision was subsequently merged with the one where the identified airport was located, and they were modelled as a single unit from then on. For each country where autochthonous cases could not be located to the subdivision level, the entire country was treated as a single unit of analysis. Hence, the spatial unit of analysis in our study can be a single first-level country subdivision, a combination of subdivisions, or an entire country (hereinafter referred to as “spatial unit”). Countries with zero incoming air passenger during 2015–2016 were excluded from the analysis (refer to the Appendix A for more details).

Demographic and ecological data

We obtained the global estimated 2015-population counts at a ~1km×1km resolution from the Socioeconomic Data and Applications Centre (SEDAC) [175], which was used to derive the total population within each spatial unit. The daily temperature at 2 meters between 2015 and 2016 was obtained from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts at ~30km×30km resolution [176]. Each temperature map was resampled using bilinear interpolation based on the cell size of the SEDAC population map. Subsequently, the resampled temperature pixel values were averaged within each spatial unit for each day, using the corresponding 2015population counts as weights. We did not calculate raw average values because the population distribution can

be highly uneven for some spatial units such as the Sichuan province of China, where the majority reside within the basin instead of the mountain or plateau regions. The methods employed to quantify the global environmental suitability of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus, as well as the associated uncertainties, were described in Dickens et al. [177]. For each species, we obtained 250 vector suitability maps at a ~5km×5km resolution via bootstrapping. Our models performed reasonably well based on the out-of-sample prediction accuracy, with a median true skill statistic of 0.84 (0.76–0.86) for Ae. aegypti and 0.71 (0.66–0.78) for Ae. albopictus [177]. Following the same procedure as above, each vector suitability map was resampled according to the resolution of the SEDAC population map to derive the average suitability value weighted by the human population counts for each spatial unit.

Statistical analyses

Overview

To map the cryptic spread of ZIKV during the 2015–2016 global epidemic, we first performed simulations to estimate the weekly number of ZIKV infections imported into each spatial unit that did not report any autochthonous cases during 2015–2016. Hereinafter, we will refer to these spatial units as susceptible spatial units, and the rest as donor spatial units. As a by-product of the simulation, a reporting index was derived to quantify the probability of reporting a case per importation for each susceptible spatial unit. Next, we estimated the virus’ weekly basic reproduction number (𝑅0 ) for each susceptible spatial unit, which was then used to compute the probability that no onward spread following ZIKV importation occurred during 2015–2016. These probability estimates derived from our model, together with the local evidence of Aedes-borne disease transmission potential based on the existing literature, were used to identify susceptible spatial units with a high chance of cryptic ZIKV spread during 2015–2016. Simulation of the number of imported infections

To simulate the weekly number of imported ZIKV infections for each susceptible spatial unit, we first made the following definitions and assumptions. First, the local population of a spatial unit was defined as all who live in that spatial unit regardless of citizenship status. At any point in time, the size of a spatial unit 𝑖’s local population who were visiting other spatial units was assumed to be approximately equal to the total number of spatial unit 𝑖’s visitors. Hence, the total number of individuals located in a spatial unit at any point in time can be approximated by the total local population size, which was given by the SEDAC population estimates. Second, we assumed that air travellers with origin airport located in spatial unit 𝑖 and destination airport located in spatial unit 𝑗 were only made up of people belonging to the local population of spatial unit 𝑖 or those of spatial unit 𝑗. Third, people who acquired ZIKV infection while visiting a spatial unit were assumed to remain asymptomatic by the end of their visits, so that all the autochthonous cases reported by a spatial unit can be assumed to come from its local population. Fourth, for any individual who belonged to the local population of a susceptible spatial unit and visited a donor spatial unit during 2015–2016, no immunity against ZIKV developed prior to his/her visit. Any remaining susceptible spatial units with evidence of historical ZIKV transmission but no outbreaks were intentionally retained, since the population-level immune landscape at the beginning of 2015 in these spatial units was still poorly understood in general due to incomplete or outdated serological data.

Model validation results

We observed a high correlation between the estimated total number of imported ZIKV infections and the reported case count during 2015–2016 for each susceptible spatial unit in the US (Pearson 𝑟 = 0.913 for log10 transformed data). Except two data points, where either the reported or the estimated value was zero, the rest of the observations were close to a straight line with a slope of one (Figure 2.2A), suggesting our simulation results were reasonably accurate provided the reporting rates were not drastically different among these susceptible spatial units within the same country. In addition, there was overall a good agreement between the median 𝑅0 estimates derived from the global risk model and those obtained from the literature, with the majority falling within the ±0.3 error band (Figure 2.2B). Discussion

This study unveils the potential cryptic spread of ZIKV during 2015–2016 to further our understanding of the virus’ circulation worldwide. This was achieved via integrating heterogeneous data sources to model ZIKV’s transmissibility, importation, and onward spread, combined with the evidence of Aedes-borne disease transmission potential from the existing literature. Although ZIKV has received much less media attention ever since the epidemic waned, our results highlight the geographical areas where future studies may be needed to investigate if past or ongoing ZIKV circulation is present, in preparation for possible epidemics in the future. Overall, the global distribution of the number of imported ZIKV infections based on our simulations was consistent with the importation risk estimates derived by Nah et al. [193], where countries in Western Europe and the Americas experienced a higher risk of importation in contrast to the majority of Asian and African countries. Several spatial units in Asia and Africa, however, were estimated to have a large number of imported ZIKV infections, such as Shanghai in China and Luanda in Angola, in agreement with the findings of Bogoch et al. [155]. Different from the previous studies, we directly estimated the absolute number of imported infections at a reasonably high spatial resolution, and our results were found to correlate very well with the imported case counts reported by different susceptible spatial units in the US.

To date, new evidence of ZIKV transmission continues to accumulate, which enables our estimated geographical extent of cryptic ZIKV spread to be partially validated. On 26 December 2016, the first Zika case in Angola was identified in a 14-year-old boy residing in Luanda, immediately after the initiation of ZIKV RT-PCR testing in the country [194]. Correspondingly, our model successfully detected autochthonous ZIKV transmission in Luanda, with the estimated probability of no indigenous cases occurring during 2015–2016 being the lowest (8.53×10-31) among all the 24 susceptible spatial units listed in Table 2.2. Note that we classified Luanda as a susceptible spatial unit in our study, since the first detected case was not announced until the early January of 2017 [195]. Besides Luanda, Tamil Nadu in India reported its first autochthonous Zika case in July 2017 [196]. Based on our model, 31 (19–46) ZIKV infections were imported in Tamil Nadu during 2015–2016, with the chance of no onward spread following these imported infections being low (0.0003). Thus, it was possible that ZIKV had already circulated silently in Tamil Nadu by the end of 2016, which later gave rise to the first reported case in 2017. Moreover, in a recent study published in October 2019, low ZIKV seroprevalence was observed in serum samples collected from Southern Taiwan at the end of 2015, providing evidence of possible undetected autochthonous transmission [197]. Overall, the abovementioned epidemiological evidence adds to the credibility of our results, and underscores the necessity of local investigation in the rest of the susceptible spatial units that were found to have a high potential of cryptic ZIKV spread during 2015–2016. In Africa, information on the ZIKV seroprevalence and incidence remains limited and largely outdated, but recent evidence suggests that the virus has been silently spreading in the continent for at least two decades [191]. According to Herrera et al., of the 387 serum samples collected from febrile patients in Nigeria and Senegal during 1992–2016, 6.2% were positive for IgM to ZIKV and negative for dengue reactivity, with four samples from which ZIKV envelope was amplified [191]. Thus, there are at least two plausible interpretations for the 9 susceptible spatial units in Africa listed in Table 2.2. These spatial units could have indeed experienced ZIKV spread during 2015–2016, which was undetected due to limited surveillance capacity. Alternatively, the level of herd immunity prior to 2015 may have been higher than currently known (due to paucity of serological data), thereby successfully preventing ZIKV transmission during 2015–2016. In either case, a significant knowledge gap remains to be filled, and this is particularly critical in light of the past or current spread of dengue, Chikungunya, or yellow fever viruses in these spatial units, which shows that the future risk of ZIKV epidemics should not be ignored. For example, a dengue outbreak occurred in Abidjan city, Côte d'Ivoire in

2019, where a total of 1,776 suspected dengue cases were reported between 1 January and 25 June [198]. Recent entomological investigations carried out in Abidjan also revealed a very high larval index of Ae. aegypti, the primary vector species of ZIKV [199]. Elsewhere, a cross-sectional study conducted between November 2015 and June 2016 in Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of Congo, found that 30.2% and 26.4% of the participants had experienced past dengue and Chikungunya infections respectively [200]. Although no acute ZIKV infections were found, of note is that much fewer blood samples (n=80) were tested for ZIKV, with no urine samples collected and only RT-PCR performed [200]. Thus, the possibility of low-level cryptic transmission of ZIKV in Kinshasa remains to be investigated. In South Asia, the presence of ZIKV antibodies in humans was first discovered in India in 1952 [189]. More recently, India reported its first three laboratory-confirmed indigenous Zika cases in Gujarat on 15 May 2017, months after they were detected [201]. Subsequently, another case was found in Tamil Nadu during the same year [196]. After testing over 35,000 febrile illness serum samples, the Indian government had only identified the aforementioned four cases by 2017, indicating a very low level of ZIKV circulation [202]. Interestingly, we have found seven spatial units in the country with a high potential of cryptic ZIKV spread during 2015–2016, and these included Tamil Nadu but not Gujarat. Based on the representative partial sequences, the virus from Gujarat seems to belong to an old Asian strain, in contrast to the ZIKV in Tamil Nadu, which was classified as the then-current Asian outbreak strain [203]. It is thus possible that the unreported cases in Gujarat were not imported from any of the donor spatial units in our study, and hence were not detected by our model. The four Zika cases reported in 2017, together with the following 2018 outbreaks in Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh [203] demonstrates the importance of strengthening ZIKV surveillance in other states as well, especially the ones where we found a high potential for cryptic spread of ZIKV. Given the considerably high intensity of dengue transmission in India [204], any low-level spread of ZIKV could be easily undetected due to misdiagnosis, until the virus causes a major outbreak possibly in the future. In Southeast Asia, data on ZIKV epidemiology is scarce despite the long-term circulation of the virus [146]. Our study has identified two provinces in Indonesia—Bali and Banten—where cryptic ZIKV spread was likely to have occurred during 2015–2016. The earliest serological evidence of ZIKV transmission within the country was obtained in Central Java and Lombok around the 1970s [205,206], and the virus was found to remain circulating in the early 2010s [188]. Specifically, among the 662 samples collected from healthy children aged 1–4 years in

2014, 9.1% were ZIKV-seropositive, who came from 11 provinces including Banten but not Bali [188]. The total number of serum samples obtained from these two provinces was limited (67 combined) [188], and the level of ZIKV immunity among the general population was still unclear. Besides Southeast Asia, we also found two spatial units in East Asia that may require future risk assessment, namely Guangdong and Taiwan. Both spatial units have reported dengue outbreaks within the past few years [207,208], as well as imported ZIKV infections as recently as the early 2020 [209,210]. These, combined with the latest discovery of low ZIKV seroprevalence in southern Taiwan and a neighbouring province of Guangdong [197,211], suggest that the potential risk of future ZIKV outbreaks should not be overlooked. In West Asia, our results highlight only one spatial unit that requires special attention, namely Makkah. In 2009, a dengue epidemic occurred in the city, with a 20-fold increase in the incidence rate compared with the year before, and the disease became endemic since then [212], suggesting the local environment could also be suitable for ZIKV transmission. We estimated that at least 22 (12–36) ZIKV infections were imported in Makkah during 2015–2016, with a very low chance of no onward transmission (0.012). Notably, the city receives ~200,000 pilgrims from Indonesia annually [213], where ZIKV is presumed to be endemic [188], thus facing a potentially ongoing risk of ZIKV importation every year. To date, no indigenous Zika cases in Makkah have been reported, but serological data indicate that the virus may have circulated elsewhere within the country. In a recent study involving 410 asymptomatic pregnant women in Najran, 24 tested positive for anti-ZIKV IgM and 52 were anti-ZIKV IgG positive, none of whom had travelled abroad [214]. Although no acute infections were detected using RT-PCR and cross-reactivity with dengue virus could not be excluded, the study points to the possibility of silent ZIKV spread in Saudi Arabia, which needs to be further assessed by larger studies at the nationwide level [214]. Outside of Africa and Asia, we found high potential for cryptic ZIKV spread during 2015–2016 in the following spatial units: Canelones in Uruguay (which covers the vast majority of the country), Hawaii in the United States, and Queensland in Australia. Being one of the very few countries in the Americas without any ZIKV transmission reported to date [146], Uruguay also did not find any autochthonous dengue cases until an outbreak which occurred in the early 2016 [215]. The high estimated risk of cryptic ZIKV spread in the Canelones spatial unit during 2015–2016 was likely to be attributed to the very large number of imported ZIKV infections, which we estimated to be 2,043 (1,537–2,913). In contrast, both Hawaii and Queensland were estimated to have much

fewer imported ZIKV infections but meanwhile higher environmental suitability for Ae. aegypti, with multiple historical dengue epidemics reported to date [216,217], and hence may face a higher risk of local ZIKV outbreak in the future. Zika has not gone away. It is thought that low-level transmission is still ongoing in many countries in the world [146]. The identification of locations with likely cryptic spread of ZIKV during the 2015–2016 global epidemic can have significant public health implications, as evidenced by the previous silent circulation of the virus in northeast Brazil that eventually caused a huge epidemic nationally and internationally [218]. Even in spatial units where winter months exist (e.g., Guangdong), ZIKV can be transmitted vertically in Ae. aegypti as a means of survival under adverse conditions, and thus the epidemiological importance of cryptic ZIKV spread in these areas should not be discounted [219]. In addition, improving knowledge about global ZIKV spread could also contribute to a better understanding of dengue epidemiology, given the immune interactions between these closely-related flaviviruses as demonstrated by a recent study [52]. Our study must be interpreted in light of the following limitations. For each susceptible spatial unit, we were unable to quantify the population-level immunity immediately prior to the 2015–2016 global ZIKV epidemic, largely owing to the paucity of updated serological data, which also include antibody titre data that are needed to account for the effect of pre-existing dengue immunity on the susceptibility to ZIKV infection in each location [220]. This is compounded by the complex effect of vector-virus genetic interactions on ZIKV transmissibility [221], which is still poorly understood in general and outside the scope of our study. In addition, we did not account for land or sea transport in our analysis, and assumed their impact on our estimates to be limited given that most ZIKV infections that were imported from donor to susceptible spatial units involved air travel, although short-range commuting flows could play a more important role in the epidemic invasion path once the virus was introduced to a new country or continent [222]. We also did not consider importation caused by infected passengers in transit who entered a country as short-term visitors before reaching their final destinations, due to the generally short LOS presumed for these relatively small number of individuals. Besides, our study did not account for the impact of weather variables such as rainfall on the seasonal variation in vector-to-host ratios, which is challenging to model on a global scale and can be further explored in future work. Finally, we relied on only the locations with reported indigenous Zika cases as sources of importation, and hence both the estimated number of imported infections and the geographical extent of cryptic ZIKV spread during

2015–2016 were conservative. In other words, it is not possible to reveal all the locations at a high risk of cryptic ZIKV spread in a single modelling study, and the findings generated in this study were conditional on the currently available data only. For instance, it is thought that ZIKV may be endemic in many parts of Southeast Asia, but epidemiological data are seriously lacking [146], thereby hindering a more comprehensive analysis of ZIKV spread within the region. Nonetheless, this study has made an important contribution to narrowing the knowledge gap of the global ZIKV epidemiology and has also laid a foundation for future studies that attempt to further explore this important topic. For example, once previously-undetected circulation of ZIKV is confirmed by serological / RT-PCR testing at locations that we estimated as having a high potential of cryptic ZIKV spread, future modelling studies can then aim to identify geographical areas with (1) high connectivity to the aforementioned locations, and (2) local evidence of Aedes-borne disease transmission potential, to further narrow this key knowledge gap.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study has provided valuable insights into the potentially high-risk locations for cryptic ZIKV circulation during the 2015–2016 pandemic. Enhanced surveillance is recommended in these geographical areas to mitigate the risk of future epidemics—locally, nationally, and even globally—given the world is increasingly vulnerable to pandemic threats due to the expansion of air traffic networks. In the context of the growing importance of enhanced vigilance and epidemic preparedness in today’s world, our modelling framework can also be adapted to identify areas with likely unknown spread of other emerging vector-borne diseases, which has important implications for public health readiness especially in resource-limited settings. In addition to surveillance for emerging infections in human populations, entomological surveillance can provide an indicator of the transmissibility of viruses once they are introduced into a country or region. Singapore has been lauded for its vector control, one element of which is to identify areas with high Aedes mosquito abundance to be targeted for measures. In the next chapter, we will develop regression models using Singapore’s island-wide vector surveillance data, to identify factors underlying the spatio-temporal variation of adult Aedes abundance in the country’s high-rise public residential zones.

Chapter 3: Spatio-temporal analysis of the main dengue vector populations in Singapore

This work has been accepted for publication as: Sun, H., Dickens, B.L., Richards, D. et al. Spatio-temporal analysis of the main dengue vector populations in Singapore. Parasites Vectors 14, 41 (2021).

Abstract

Background

Despite the licensure of the world’s first dengue vaccine and the current development of additional vaccine candidates, successful Aedes control remains critical to the reduction of dengue virus transmission. To date, there is still limited literature that attempts to explain the spatio-temporal population dynamics of Aedes mosquitoes within a single city, which hinders the development of more effective citywide vector control strategies. Narrowing this knowledge gap requires consistent and longitudinal measurement of Aedes abundance across the city, as well as examination of relationships between variables on a much finer scale.

Methods

We utilized a high-resolution longitudinal dataset generated from Singapore's islandwide Gravitrap surveillance system over a 2-year period, and built a Bayesian hierarchical model to explain the spatio-temporal dynamics of Aedes aegypti and Ae. albopictus in relation to a wide range of environmental and anthropogenic variables. We also created a baseline during our model assessment to serve as a benchmark to be compared with the model’s out-of-sample prediction / forecast accuracy as measured by the mean absolute error.

Results

For both Aedes species, building age and nearby managed vegetation cover were found to have a significant positive association with the mean mosquito abundance, with the former being the strongest predictor. We also observed substantial evidence of a nonlinear effect of weekly maximum temperature on the Aedes abundance. Our models generally yielded modest but statistically significant reductions in the out-of-sample prediction / forecast error relative to the baseline.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that public residential estates with older buildings and more nearby managed vegetation should be prioritized for vector control inspections and community advocacy to reduce the abundance of Aedes mosquitoes and the risk of dengue transmission.

Introduction

Dengue fever is a rapidly emerging vector-borne disease mainly transmitted by Aedes aegypti and Ae. albopictus [2], causing an estimated number of 105 million infections per year worldwide [6]. Clinical manifestations of dengue infection range from mild fever, to potentially lethal complications such as dengue shock syndrome [2]. Despite the licensure of the world’s first dengue vaccine and the current development of additional vaccine candidates [223], successful vector control remains critical to the reduction of dengue virus transmission [224]. Moreover, the benefits of Aedes population control extend beyond dengue infection prevention alone, given the multiple diseases that can be transmitted by these mosquito species, such as Zika, chikungunya, and yellow fever. Previous work has yielded important insights into the behaviors and ecology of the main dengue vectors. Both Aedes species could easily disperse throughout areas with radius ~300 meters to seek oviposition sites [225]. The Ae. aegypti mosquitoes, in particular, have become a highly efficient vector for dengue transmission owing to its skip oviposition behavior (i.e. deposit eggs from the same batch in multiple sites), desiccation-resistant eggs, preference for human biting, multiple feeds per gonotrophic cycle, and adaptation to reside and breed in human habitats, among other factors [68]. The Ae. albopictus mosquitoes were found to have a relatively lower contribution to the reported dengue cases overall despite their high competence for dengue transmission, which is primarily attributed to aspects of their ecology [82]. Both environmental and anthropogenic factors can exert an important influence on the distribution of Aedes mosquitoes [75,95,97,177,226], and modelling studies have been carried out to map the suitability and distribution of the main dengue vectors at a global scale [95,97,177]. However, there is still very limited literature that attempts to explain the spatio-temporal population dynamics of Aedes mosquitoes within a single city [99], which hinders the development of more effective citywide vector control strategies. To bridge this knowledge gap requires consistent and longitudinal measurement of Aedes abundance across the city [99], as well as examination of relationships between variables at a much finer scale. As an island city-state lying one-degree north of the equator, Singapore faces regular dengue outbreaks with the four dengue virus serotypes co-circulating all year round [227]. The low herd immunity [102], coupled with the tropical climate and highly urbanized environment, poses challenges to the nation’s dengue control program [228]. As part of Singapore’s vector control program, the National Environment Agency conducted regular

inspections of homes and surrounding areas all year round to remove mosquito-breeding habitats, and mobilized the community and stakeholders to minimize instances of stagnant water [229]. Vector control activities were also ramped up in dengue cluster areas, with space sprays used for adulticiding. To monitor the spatio-temporal trend of the adult Aedes abundance in Singapore, the National Environment Agency also established an islandwide Gravitrap surveillance system since 2017, with over 50,000 Gravitraps deployed in the public housing estates across the island [230,231], which accommodate ~80% of the resident population [232]. The weekly mean catch per trap for each species provides an indication of the Aedes abundance around each specific residential location and each time point, which is presumed to be closely associated with an individual’s risk of exposure to mosquito bites inside or around homes and also much less susceptible to the measurement bias encountered in non-systematic breeding sites inspection [233]. To facilitate resource planning for Singapore’s vector control, we used the longitudinal dataset generated from the islandwide Gravitrap surveillance system during 2017–8 as well as a wide range of environmental and anthropogenic variables acquired from various sources to (1) explain the spatio-temporal dynamics of the Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus population in Singapore’s high-rise residential zones and (2) assess our model’s ability to predict Aedes abundance across space and generate forecasts up to three weeks ahead.

Methods

Data

Aedes mosquito data were collected fortnightly for each of the 552 sites from 2017–8 [231], with oddnumbered blocks inspected one week and even-numbered blocks the next. Fortnightly collections were then halved to obtain the weekly numbers of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus caught at each site respectively, which contained roughly equal numbers of odd and even-numbered blocks. We created a 300m buffer around each block based on Liew et al [225], and for each site, all buffers were merged into a single polygon, to be used for deriving zonal statistics for the environmental and anthropogenic variables. The Singapore land classification map was generated at a resolution of 10m using seven separate images from the Sentinel-2 satellite of the European Space Agency [234]. The collected images were taken on different dates to ensure the existence of cloud-free pixels for the whole of mainland Singapore based on a cloud cover

classification algorithm [234]. With 309 labelled data points obtained manually using Google Earth, a random forest algorithm was used to produce seven land cover maps, excluding cloudy areas for each of the collected images [234]. The final classified land cover for each pixel was set to be the majority vote out of all the predictions, with an out-of-bag classification accuracy of 81% [234]. For each site, we derived the percentage of the buffer area covered by water, grass, forest, and managed vegetation (i.e., trees and shrubs with structure dominated by human management) respectively, setting “urban” as the reference level. Data on waterbodies were extracted from OpenStreetMap [235]. We measured the distance to the nearest waterway from each block using ArcMap 10.6, which was then averaged within each site (similarly for the distance to the nearest water area). We also obtained Singapore’s drain line map from the Public Utilities Board. The total drain line density for each site was defined to be the total length of the drain lines falling within the corresponding buffer divided by the buffer area. In addition, the average age of buildings for each site was computed using lease commencement year data collected from the Singapore Land Authority [236]. Weekly mean, maximum, and minimum temperature and mean relative humidity were obtained from a total of 21 weather stations installed by the National Environment Agency. For each climatic variable and each week, we fitted a thin plate spline surface to produce an interpolated value for each site. Weekly raster maps of total precipitation were obtained from the Meteorological Service Singapore at a ~ 500m × 500m resolution, and for each site and each week, all the pixel values within the corresponding buffer were averaged. All the aforementioned explanatory variables were standardized to zero mean and unit variance, and a quadratic term for each of the standardized temperature variables was created to examine nonlinear effects [81].

Statistical analyses

To understand the direction and strength of associations between Aedes abundance and different environmental and anthropogenic variables, we first computed the pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients for the full set of covariates and removed redundant variables using a threshold of ±0.6 to avoid collinearity. A Bayesian spatio-temporal model was created, where we assumed that the number of Ae. aegypti or Ae. albopictus caught at site 𝑖 during week 𝑡 (𝑦𝑖𝑡 ) followed a negative binomial distribution with mean 𝜇𝑖𝑡 and dispersion parameter 𝑟, namely 𝑦𝑖𝑡 ~𝑁𝐵(𝜇𝑖𝑡 , 𝑟), where

log(𝜇𝑖𝑡 ) = log(𝐸𝑖𝑡 ) + 𝑏0 + ∑ 𝛽𝑗 𝑥𝑖𝑗 + ∑ 𝛾𝑘 𝑤𝑖𝑡𝑘 + 𝑢𝑖 + 𝑣𝑃𝐴𝑖 + 𝜙𝑡 . 𝑗

In the equation above, 𝐸𝑖𝑡 denotes the number of Gravitraps present at site 𝑖 during week 𝑡, 𝑏0 the intercept, 𝑥𝑖𝑗 the spatial variables of site 𝑖, and 𝑤𝑖𝑡𝑘 all the weekly weather measurements of site 𝑖 between one and three weeks prior to week 𝑡. We included an unstructured spatial effect 𝑢𝑖 for each site and an extra term 𝑣𝑃𝐴𝑖 for the corresponding planning area (each containing 20 sites on average, with an interquartile range of 8–26) to account for additional spatial dependence. The temporally structured effect 𝜙𝑡 was assumed to follow a random walk with a maximum order of two. Throughout this paper, we used 𝑡 = 1 to denote the first epidemiological week of 2017, and 𝑡 = 104 the last epidemiological week of 2018. All the model parameters were assigned a minimally informative prior (refer to Table 3.2 caption), and parameter estimation was performed using Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation [237,238], with 95% credible intervals (CrI) computed to summarize the uncertainty in each model parameter. For each species, both the optimal order of the random walk for the temporally structured effect and the time lag of the weather variables were selected based on the deviance information criterion. Further variable subset selection was not implemented at this stage to avoid biased parameter estimates resulting from sequential comparisons, since the primary aim of our Bayesian spatio-temporal model was to infer the associations between Aedes abundance and all the different environmental and anthropogenic variables considered in this study. Next, we used cross-validation to assess how accurately one can predict Aedes abundance across space. Here we treated the total number of Ae. aegypti or Ae. albopictus caught at each site during 2017─8 as the response variable, with the log transformation of the total number of trap-week units as an offset. Only spatial fixed effects and the planning area random effect were included in the model, and a backwards elimination procedure was implemented for fixed effects variable selection using Akaike information criterion during model training. Two forms of cross-validations were performed, namely, leave-one-site-out and leave-one-planningarea-out, where in each case a baseline prediction of the mean catch per trap per week was generated for each site, to be used as a benchmark to assess whether the model can indeed yield a higher prediction accuracy. In the former case, each time a site 𝑖 was left out for testing, its baseline prediction was defined as the observed mean catch per trap per week of the site that was geographically the closest to site 𝑖. In the latter case, the

baseline prediction for each site 𝑖 was defined as the observed mean catch per trap per week averaged across all the sites within the planning area that was geographically the closest to site 𝑖 but did not contain site 𝑖. Finally, we evaluated the contribution of weather variables to the improvement of the weekly Aedes abundance forecast accuracy up to three weeks ahead. Using 𝑡𝐶 to denote the current time point, we treated the mean catch per trap of each site at week (𝑡𝐶 + ∆𝑡) (∆𝑡 = 1,2, or 3) as the response variable, and each was regressed upon all the weather and/or entomological (i.e. Aedes abundance) covariates at weeks 𝑡𝐶 , (𝑡𝐶 − 1) and (𝑡𝐶 − 2). Here, the entomological covariates were always included as autoregressive terms, with the additional inclusion of weather covariates in an alternative model to assess the resulting change in the out-ofsample forecast errors. Due to the large number of lagged weather variables included in the alternative model, we used the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator to perform variable selection during model training. For each site and each species, we derived the out-of-sample Aedes abundance forecast accuracy at week (𝑡 ∗ + ∆𝑡) for all combinations of 𝑡 ∗ ∈ {55, 60, … , 95, 100} and ∆𝑡 ∈ {1,2,3} respectively, with the model being trained on all the historical data points of that site with 𝑡𝐶 < 𝑡 ∗ . The corresponding baseline forecast was defined as the observed Aedes abundance of that site at week 𝑡 ∗ , and hence we will also refer to 𝑡 ∗ as the baseline time point.

Results

In total, 4,923,456 trap-week units of observation were obtained, with 495,638 Ae. aegypti and 132,533 Ae. albopictus caught during the entire study period. For both species, we observed a marked difference in the Aedes abundance across space, with the mean catch per trap per week at some sites exceeding 5-fold that at some other sites (Figure 3.1). For example, only an average of 0.05 Ae. aegypti mosquitoes were caught per trap per week at a site within Clementi area during the study period, in contrast to 0.28 at a site in Tampines. Operationally, these data are updated on a weekly basis to provide policy makers with an indication of which areas may require more vector control to mitigate the risk of dengue transmission. The mean and standard deviation of the site-level mean catch per trap per week were 0.102 and 0.074 for Ae. aegypti, and 0.027 and 0.019 for Ae. albopictus (Table 3.1 contains the summary statistics of all the variables in this study, and visualizations of selected variables across space or time can be found in Appendix B).

Similarly, the turning points were 31.9°C, 31.6°C and 31.9°C for Ae. albopictus. In both leave-one-site-out and leave-one-planning-area-out cross validation, which were performed to assess predictive accuracy across space, there was an overall increasing trend in the observed site-level Aedes abundance as we moved from the lowest to the highest quintile based on the out-of-sample model predictions (Figure 3.4). Except for the leave-one-site-out cross validation of the model for Ae. aegypti, there was a modest and statistically significant reduction in the mean absolute prediction error of the model compared with the baseline prediction (Table 3.3). forecast error was observed for models forecasting 2or 3-week ahead Aedes abundance, regardless of species or whether we included weather covariates as additional predictors (Table 3.4). Our model, however, did not outperform the 1-week ahead baseline forecast (Table 3.4), owing to the fortnightly mosquito collection and the subsequent conversion to weekly data (details described in Methods), which caused data points at adjacent weeks to share 50% of the information in common. Notably, we found that in all cases, the additional inclusion of lagged weather covariates did not improve the out-of-sample forecast accuracy compared with a simple model that only included autoregressive terms as predictors (Table 3.4).

Discussion

This study examined the spatial and temporal variation of the main dengue vectors in Singapore’s high-rise public residential zones in relation to a wide range of environmental and anthropogenic variables. The insights derived from this study further add to previous work that aimed to understand Aedes ecology in the local context, and can facilitate the formulation of more effective vector control strategies in the future. Our model performance also suggests the potential use of spatio-temporal mapping as a tool to improve the understanding of the Aedes distribution in other cities or countries, where intensive entomological surveillance may be harder to achieve. We found that the majority of our spatial covariates had an at least borderline significant association with the Aedes abundance (i.e., the 95% credible intervals did not / barely overlap zero). In particular, building age was shown to be the strongest predictor. This might be due to a combination of factors, including infrastructural degradation and the water storing practices associated with the sociodemographic profile of residents that result in more instances of water stagnation that can breed mosquitoes. For both species, we estimated that an

increase in the managed vegetation cover within the buffer area was associated with a substantial rise in the mean vector abundance, likely owing to the increased availability of water in leaf axils, leaf litter, and discarded receptacles hidden in foliage and tree holes which supports mosquito breeding. Unlike managed vegetation cover, both forest and grass cover were found to be negatively associated with the abundance of Ae. aegypti. This was not unexpected given that Ae. aegypti prefers highly urbanized areas where it can breed in artificial containers. On the other hand, there was a positive association between forest cover and Ae. albopictus abundance based on the point estimate, which is consistent with the existing knowledge of the vector’s ecology [239]. Our analysis showed a borderline significant negative association between drain line density and the abundance of Ae. aegypti in contrast to the positive correlation reported by Seidahmed et al. [240] This discrepancy can be due to a number of reasons: for example, the study conducted by Seidahmed et al. was restricted to a small area of Singapore, and results may be confounded by the different housing types with different demography [240], whereas this study used two-year data collected from the same type of housing areas across the island. Moreover, the number of Aedes mosquitoes caught inside the high-rise residential buildings and the number of outdoor breeding habitats can be impacted by drain line density in different ways. Since perimeter drains are known to be the most common breeding habitats of Aedes in Singapore’s public areas according to routine inspections [241], their abundance could simultaneously decrease the per-mosquito probability of looking for oviposition sites inside residential buildings and increase the total number of Aedes mosquitoes in the nearby public area. Thus, our estimate is likely to be a reflection of the resulting net effect, and similarly for the estimated effects of other spatial covariates such as nearby managed vegetation cover. Previous work has highlighted the challenges of mapping the spatial distribution of Aedes mosquitoes for operational dengue vector control [242]. In particular, predictors that can be informative across an entire continent or a sufficiently large country may lose predictive power within the confines of a single city [242]. While we did observe substantial evidence of a non-zero association between many spatial covariates and Aedes abundance in our analysis, the estimated standard deviations of the spatial random effects remained large, suggesting substantial unexplained heterogeneity across space. Hence, entomological surveillance remains critical to generating knowledge of Aedes abundance in the field to inform vector control. On the other hand, we found that in most cases the model performed significantly better than the baseline at predicting

mosquito abundance at new locations, based on the spatial variables at those locations, suggesting that statistical modelling can still serve as a complementary tool to refine our understanding of the Aedes abundance at locations where entomological data are unavailable and hence to identify additional locations that may require enhanced vector control. It should be noted that the model’s improvement in prediction accuracy over the baseline was found to be smaller for Ae. aegypti than Ae. albopictus, despite the former being the most important dengue vector in Singapore. This finding may be explained by the ecology of Ae. aegypti, which is a container breeder that is subject to the vagaries of human behavior; adherence to household practices to prevent breeding is hard to measure and may vary spatially, rendering spatial modelling much more challenging. There is abundant literature on how different weather variables regulate the population dynamics of Aedes via influencing mosquito habitat availability, development, survival, and reproduction [75]. In this study, however, we estimated the effects of all the lagged weather variables on the observed Aedes abundance to be very minimal, which can be owing to the restricted range of the weather variables in the context of Singapore as well as vector control activities that were typically ramped up during higher breeding seasons. The assessment of the out-of-sample forecast errors, too, shows that the additional inclusion of weather covariates did not improve the accuracy of the Aedes abundance forecasts, and a simple model with autoregressive terms alone could yield a modest and statistically significant improvement in the 2-week and 3-week ahead forecasts over the baseline. While these results may suggest that weather had a negligible impact on Aedes abundance in Singapore, it should be noted that this study was conducted in public housing estates and detected mosquitoes that may have hatched nearby, and the relationship between weather and outdoor breeding habitats may be more complex than was identified herein. A longitudinal entomological study in the Geylang neighbourhood of Singapore found that the outdoor Aedes population was likely to be shaped by rainfall through a monsoondriven sequence of flushing, drying, and return of breeding habitats [243]. Taken together, these results suggest the differential impacts of weather on the Aedes population dynamics and hence the potential risk of exposure to mosquito bites in different settings, i.e., Aedes abundance inside and nearby public housing estates may be less sensitive to changes in weather compared with outdoor abundance. Our results need to be interpreted in the light of the following limitations. First, we were unable to account for the effects of vector control programs on the observed Aedes abundance across space and time. Regulatory inspections and community efforts aimed at removing larval habitats, as well as chemical control to reduce

adult mosquito populations, usually peak during higher vector breeding / dengue transmission seasons, and this could to some extent mask the true association between different weather variables and the Aedes abundance, with the confounding bias difficult to quantify or adjust for. Besides, there could be residual spatial dependence structure in our data due to factors such as potential ongoing expansion of Aedes mosquitoes, which may have been coincidentally absorbed by certain spatial covariates because of confounding. Nonetheless, this issue was assessed via a leave-one-planning-area-out cross validation framework with baseline predictions created to serve as a benchmark for model performance comparison, and results suggest that the spatial covariates could indeed enhance the out-of-sample predictive accuracy. In addition, our parameter estimates were derived based on mosquito data collected from Singapore’s high-rise public residential zones, and thus should not be used for extrapolation to low-rise houses. Previous work has suggested that the risks of indoor breeding of Aedes mosquitoes could be highly dependent on the accommodation type in Singapore [240]. In early 2020, the National Environment Agency extended the deployment of Gravitraps to private landed residential estates [244], and as more data are being generated, this will shed further light on how Aedes abundance differs between high-rise and low-rise residential zones across the island.

Conclusion

Our study has demonstrated the potential and challenges of spatio-temporal modelling for improving the understanding of the main dengue vectors’ ecology, and provided empirical evidence to guide the refinement of vector control strategies in the context of Singapore. Our findings suggest that public residential estates with older buildings and more nearby managed vegetation should be prioritized for vector control inspections and community advocacy to reduce the abundance of Aedes mosquitoes and the risk of dengue transmission. The insights obtained from this study could also be helpful to inspire future studies that attempt to understand the spatio-temporal dynamics of the dengue vector population at a city scale, particularly in settings where vector surveillance data are less abundant and thus require modelling to further narrow the knowledge gap. In the next chapter, I will aim to expand what is currently known about the impact of Aedes control on dengue transmission. Specifically, I will develop an agent-based model to simulate the time-varying epidemiological impact of sustained Aedes control in diverse dengue endemic settings over a 30-year time period. It is hoped that the insights generated from this work will be useful to guide the development of Aedes control strategic plans to achieve sustained reduction in the burden of dengue.

Chapter 4: Short-term and long-term epidemiological impacts of sustained vector control in various dengue endemic settings: A modelling study

Abstract

Background

As the most widespread viral infection transmitted by the Aedes mosquitoes, dengue has been estimated to cause 51 million febrile disease cases globally each year. Although sustained vector control remains key to reducing the burden of dengue, current understanding of the key factors that explain the observed variation in the shortand long-term vector control effectiveness across different transmission settings remains limited.

Methods

We used a detailed individual-based model to simulate dengue transmission with and without sustained vector control over a 30-year time frame, under a wide range of transmission scenarios. Vector control effectiveness was derived for different time windows within the 30-year intervention period. We then used the extreme gradient boosting algorithm to predict the effectiveness of vector control given the simulation parameters, and the resulting machine learning model was interpreted using Shapley Additive Explanations.

Results

According to our simulation outputs, dengue transmission would be nearly eliminated during the early stage of intensive interventions, but over time incidence would gradually bounce back to the pre-intervention level. The time point at which intervention ceases to be effective is strongly influenced not only by the intensity of vector control, but also by the pre-intervention transmission intensity and the individual-level heterogeneity in biting risk. Moreover, the impact of many transmission model parameters on the intervention effectiveness is shown to be modified by the intensity of vector control, as well as to vary over time.

Conclusion

Our study has identified the main drivers for the difference in the time-varying effectiveness of sustained vector control across different dengue endemic settings, and the insights obtained will be useful to inform future model-based studies that seek to predict the impact of dengue vector control in their local contexts.

Introduction

As the most widespread viral infection transmitted by the Aedes mosquitoes, dengue has been estimated to cause 51 million febrile disease cases globally each year, of whom 3.6 million may require hospitalization [6]. Unfortunately, the only licensed dengue vaccine to date, CYD-TDV, has been found to enhance the future risk of severe dengue infections for seronegative recipients and hence cannot be used to provide protection for all individuals [245]. Even under the very optimistic assumption that 100% of the CYD-TDV vaccine recipients are seropositive, sustained vector control remains key to reducing the burden of dengue [224]. Recently, there has been a growing interest in applying novel vector control technologies to reduce dengue transmission. For instance, combining incompatible and sterile insect techniques has been demonstrated to dramatically reduce the Aedes population size by over 90% under field conditions [124,246,247], and the application of attractive toxic sugar baits has also shown impressive results in both laboratory and small-scale field experiments [248,249]. Other examples include introducing the wMel Wolbachia strain to the Aedes population with the aim of reducing vector competence instead of suppressing the mosquito population [250]. Although the novel technologies described above have the potential to achieve dengue elimination even in high-transmission settings [111], it is still important to assess the shortand long-term impacts of the current vector control measures before more powerful vector control tools become available to be rolled out on a large scale. At present, evidence supporting the epidemiological impact of existing dengue vector control measures remains limited, due to the lack of surveillance data and the challenge in deploying rigorous study designs to assess intervention effectiveness, as well as insufficient investment in sustained mosquito suppression [111]. In particular, assessing the long-term effectiveness of dengue vector control can be extremely challenging, as this would require substantial investment in monitoring and evaluation. Moreover, long-term dengue vector control trials may violate the principle of equipoise once an intervention under evaluation has been shown to reduce disease incidence in the short to medium term, even though there may be genuine uncertainty regarding its epidemiological impact in the long run. Although not a replacement for surveillance data, mathematical modelling can serve as a useful tool to compare the predicted dengue incidence with and without interventions and thus to minimize the confounding bias commonly encountered in studies without an appropriate control group. So far, very few studies have been carried out to model the impact of dengue vector control in both the short and long terms [111,128], and to the

best of our knowledge, none has examined how the time-varying impact of dengue vector control differs across a wide range of transmission settings. Narrowing this knowledge gap will yield useful insights into the important details of a transmission context that must be accounted for to predict the shortand long-term epidemiological impacts of a sustained dengue vector control intervention. In this study, we aim to use mathematical modelling to (i) simulate the change in dengue incidence within a 30year time period following a sustained reduction in the Aedes population size by 30%–70%, and (ii) identify key factors that explain the variation in the shortand long-term vector control effectiveness across different dengue endemic settings.

Methods

Individual-based model

Overview

We developed an individual-based model to simulate dengue transmission within a spatially explicit setting while accounting for the individual-level variation in the risk of exposure to mosquito bites. The flexibility of the modeling framework also allowed us to examine the sensitivity of our vector control effectiveness estimate with respect to changes in assumptions about serotype immune-interactions [36]. Specifically, we first simulated the introduction and autochthonous transmission of dengue virus in the absence of vector control for 100 years (“warm-up period”) to establish the baseline immunity in the population. Thereafter, we continued the simulation for another 30 years (“intervention period”) with vector control being implemented to maintain the vector density at a substantially reduced level. The discrete time step used to simulate dengue transmission was equal to the length of the mosquito feeding cycle (𝑙𝑓 ), during which a mosquito took a blood meal, looked for breeding sites, laid eggs, and then searched for the next blood meal [251]. We created a wide range of scenarios in our simulations by varying the intensity of vector control, pre-intervention mosquito density, dengue importation rate, rate of change in the population age structure, host mobility rate, and the degree of individual-level heterogeneity in the risk of being bitten by the Aedes mosquitoes. In addition, we also varied the assumptions regarding the degree of antibody-dependent enhancement and duration of temporary cross-

immunity across different simulation runs to assess their influence on the model output (refer to Figure 4.1 for the schematic overview of the individual-based model).

Dengue infections in humans

Each human was allowed to have at most four sequential infections, with life-long protection against homotypic reinfection and temporary cross-protection against heterotypic infections. Upon receiving an infectious bite, (𝑘)

each human had a probability 𝜏𝑀→𝐻 of becoming infected in the absence of immunity. Here, the vector-to-host transmission probability depended on the number of historical dengue infections an individual had previously (𝑘)

experienced (𝑘 = 0,1,2,3), where we set 𝜏𝑀→𝐻 to be 0.5 for 𝑘 ≠ 1, and 0.5 ∙ (1 + 𝜙𝐴𝐷𝐸 ) for 𝑘 = 1 to account for the enhanced susceptibility to secondary infections. On average, each infected human took 𝑙𝐿𝑃 days to become infectious and another 𝑙𝑅 days to achieve viral clearance, followed by a cross-protection period lasting an average of 𝑙𝐶 days (or lifelong immunity against all serotypes after recovery from a quaternary infection); we assumed the length of each of these time intervals to be exponentially distributed. Similar to the model implemented by Hladish et al. to simulate dengue introductions [252], each human had a time-invariant probability 𝜋 of being exposed to dengue virus from an external source in each feeding cycle, and

the exposure may be resisted by immunity. To simulate the serotype of the imported dengue virus for each exposed individual, we obtained a random draw from a categorical distribution with the probability parameters equal to the relative frequencies of the four dengue serotypes in Southeast Asia estimated by Bell et al. [29]. Specifically, we first averaged Bell et al.’s estimates across the 45-year study period to be used for the simulation of dengue introductions throughout the 100-year warm-up period [29]. Here, we used a timeinvariant probability vector since the length of the warm-up period far exceeded the length of the time period for which data were available. Next, we used linear interpolation to transform Bell et al.’s quarterly estimates for the period of 1970–2000 into daily estimates [29]. These estimates were then used to simulate the introduced dengue serotypes at each feeding cycle during the 30 years of the intervention period.

Dengue infections in mosquitoes

Each susceptible mosquito had a probability 𝜏𝐻→𝑀 of becoming infected after biting an infectious human who (𝑘)

had historically been infected 𝑘 times prior to the current infection. We set 𝜏𝐻→𝑀 to be 0.5 for 𝑘 ≠ 1, and 0.5 ∙ (1 + 𝜙𝐴𝐷𝐸 ) for 𝑘 = 1 to account for the enhanced transmissibility from humans undergoing secondary infections; we assumed equal degrees of enhancement in transmissibility and susceptibility. Note that in actuality the transmission probabilities between humans and mosquitoes in the absence of enhancement could differ from 0.5. However, the uncertainty in these values can be absorbed by adjusting the vector-to-host ratio to achieve a certain level of transmission intensity. We assumed the extrinsic incubation period to follow an exponential distribution with mean 𝑙𝐸𝐼𝑃 (𝑇), which was calculated as a function of temperature (𝑇) using a thermal response model estimated by Mordecai et al. [76]. Here, we accounted for the seasonal variation in dengue transmission intensity by allowing the temperature to fluctuate between 25°C and 31°C (i.e., similar to the range of the monthly mean temperature in Bangkok or Phnom Penh) within each year using a sine function. We did not explore alternative temperature ranges in this study, since temperature amplitudes were presumed to be reasonably small in most settings with endemic dengue transmission, and any changes in the transmission intensity due to changes in the mean temperature can be reproduced by adjusting the average vector-to-host ratio. Finally, once a mosquito had survived beyond the extrinsic incubation period of the virus, she was assumed to remain infectious until death.

Spatial structure and heterogeneous biting

We created a set of communities forming on a squared lattice of size 20×20, following the spatial structure specified by Lourenço & Recker [36]. At the start of the simulation, a total of 𝑁𝐻 fully susceptible humans and 𝑁𝐵𝑆 mosquito breeding sites were uniformly distributed over space. Within each feeding cycle 𝑡, each adult female mosquito 𝑚 randomly bit a human ℎ with probability 𝑝𝐵𝑖𝑡𝑒 (𝑚, ℎ), which was calculated as follows:

In the equation above, we used 𝐶[ℎ] to denote the community where human ℎ lived (similarly for 𝐶[𝑚]). The value of the indicator function 𝛿(𝐶[𝑚], 𝐶[ℎ]) was defined to be one if the two communities 𝐶[𝑚] and 𝐶[ℎ] were identical or neighbors to each other, and zero otherwise. Given the limited flight range of Aedes aegypti, we followed Lourenço & Recker and assumed each mosquito 𝑚 was only able to bite humans present in her own community 𝐶[𝑚] or the neighboring communities [36]. Long-distance transmission occurred due to human movement, and we allowed each mosquito to randomly bite a human living in a distant community with probability 𝜔 [36]. The numerator 𝜐ℎ𝑡 denoted the “biting risk score” of individual ℎ at time 𝑡, which was normalized according to the equation above to obtain 𝑝𝐵𝑖𝑡𝑒 (𝑚, ℎ). We computed the biting risk score as the

product of the following two variables: 𝜐ℎ𝑡 = 𝜐ℎ(1) ∙ 𝜐ℎ(2) .

The first variable 𝜐ℎ(1) denoted the relative attractiveness to mosquitoes, which was estimated as a function of

an individual’s body surface area according to results from Liebman et al.’s field study [253], with the growth in body surface area from birth to adulthood simulated based on the age and sex of that individual following Perkins et al. [136]. The second variable 𝜐ℎ(2) accounted for the residual variation in the biting risk score due to other factors (e.g., differential duration each person spent near biting mosquitoes). For simplicity, we assumed 𝜐ℎ(2) to be independent of age and generated its value from a gamma distribution with mean one and standard deviation 𝜎𝜐ℎ(2) , and we varied the value of 𝜎𝜐ℎ(2) across different transmission settings to study its impact on the shortand long-term effectiveness of vector control (refer to section 4.3.1.7 for more details).

Human age structure

We simulated the birth, ageing, and death of humans at a time step of 28 days. The maximum human lifespan was set to be 100 years, and each human’s age was treated as an integer variable measured in the unit of 28 days, so that every time step we simulated the demographic changes in the population, each human either had their age increased by one or was removed from the population due to death. We used the United Nations model life table (General Pattern with life expectancy at birth being 75 years) [254] and assumed a constant mortality rate within each 5-year age interval in the life table. This allowed us to convert the age interval length of the life table to 28 days. Prior to the start of the warm-up period, we initialized the age of each human such that the proportion of individuals falling within each 28-day age interval tallied with the stationary age distribution informed by the life table. Throughout the warm-up period, the number of incident deaths occurring at each age every 28 days was fixed at the corresponding expected value according to the life table, so that the stationary age structure can be maintained. Each death was replaced by a newborn whose sex was randomly generated assuming equal probabilities of male and female births. For the 30 years of the intervention period, we modelled different rates of changes in the population age structure to examine the associated impact on the vector control effectiveness. Let the annual mortality rate at age 𝑎 in year 𝑦 be 𝜆𝑎,𝑦 , with 𝑦 = 0 referring to the first year of the intervention period. We assumed 𝜆𝑎,𝑦 to decline over time according to the following equation: log(𝜆𝑎,𝑦 ) = log(𝜆𝑎,0 ) + 𝜌 ∙ 𝛽𝑎 ∙ 𝑦 (𝜌 ≥ 0, 𝛽𝑎 < 0). In the equation above, the constant 𝛽𝑎 was defined such that (1 − 𝑒 𝛽𝑎 ) was equal to the average annual percentage reduction in mortality rate at age 𝑎 in Singapore across the period of 1990–2020 [255]. Hence, the rate of mortality decline can be varied by changing the value of the relative rate of ageing 𝜌, with 𝜌 = 1 indicating that the mortality rate declined at the same speed as observed in Singapore. Once we calculated 𝜆𝑎,𝑦 based on the values of 𝑎, 𝑦 and 𝜌, the age-specific probability of death within a 28-day time interval can be derived to simulate mortality events using a binomial distribution. The same method was applied to create different rates of fertility decline, where we used the same relative rate of ageing 𝜌 described earlier. Note that previously we did not specify the age-specific fertility rates since each death was instantaneously replaced by a newborn during the warm-up period. Here, we derived the baseline age-specific fertility rates (i.e., when 𝑦 was equal to zero) such that both of the following conditions were

satisfied: (1) the ratios of the baseline fertility rates between different ages tallied with the corresponding ratios observed in Singapore in 1990 [255]; (2) the expected total number of newborns in each 28-day time interval, calculated under the baseline age-specific fertility rates and the stationary age distribution mentioned earlier, tallied with the total number of newborns every 28 days during the warm-up period.

Development of mosquitoes

We modelled the development of mosquitoes using a Markov process with four states: eggs, larvae, pupae, and adults. Within each feeding cycle, each adult female mosquito 𝑚 randomly chose a breeding site located in either her own community 𝐶[𝑚] (i.e., the community where she was born) or one of the neighboring communities to lay eggs. The number of eggs laid in one oviposition followed a Poisson distribution with mean 𝜃𝐸 . Male and female adult emergence was assumed to be equally likely, and male mosquitoes were not explicitly modelled upon adult emergence. The relationship between the mosquito developmental rates and temperature was specified using Schoolfield et al.’s model [256]. Following Otero et al., the mortality rates of eggs and adult female mosquitoes were both treated as constant, and the larval mortality rate was decomposed into the sum of the natural mortality rate under optimal conditions and the density-dependent mortality rate [257]. We modelled the natural larval mortality rate using an exponential decay function of temperature [257] and assumed the density-dependent larval mortality rate to be proportional to the number of larvae present in a breeding habitat; we denoted the proportionality coefficient by 𝛼. The pupal mortality rate was also assumed to vary with temperature according to an exponential decay function, with an additional “emergence factor” to account for unsuccessful emergence of adult mosquitoes [257]. The discrete time step used to simulate the development of mosquitoes during the aquatic stage was taken to be one tenth of the mosquito feeding cycle length, to keep the probabilities of multiple transitions (i.e., from eggs to pupae, from eggs to adults, or from larvae to adults) low (<0.05). At the start of the simulation, the number of eggs in each breeding site was randomly drawn from a Poisson distribution with mean 𝑛𝐸 , with no larvae, pupae, or adults present. To vary the pre-intervention vector-to-host ratio across different simulation runs, we varied the parameter 𝛼 which was used to calculate the densitydependent larval mortality rate as described earlier. Here, we chose to vary the productivity instead of the total number of breeding sites for the sake of computational efficiency, since the simulation run-time increased with

the number of breeding sites. Finally, at the end of the 100-year warm-up period, we randomly selected a proportion (𝑝𝑅𝑀 ) of the breeding sites to be permanently removed to reduce the mosquito population size by roughly the same proportion. Although here we implemented vector control via source reduction, the same effect can be achieved using other strategies such as the incompatible insect technique or the sterile insect technique used in real-world settings.

Simulation model parameters

We used Latin hypercube sampling [258] to create 1,000 combinations of the following parameters: 𝜋, 𝜔, 𝜌, 𝜎𝜐ℎ(2) , 𝛼, 𝜙𝐴𝐷𝐸 , and 𝑙𝐶 , to study the impact of dengue importation, human mobility, population ageing, individual-level heterogeneity in the risk of being bitten, mosquito abundance, degree of antibody-dependent enhancement, and the average duration of cross-protection upon vector control effectiveness. Note that although both the values of 𝜙𝐴𝐷𝐸 and 𝑙𝐶 are likely to be relatively stable across different transmission settings, they were jointly varied with the other five parameters to test the sensitivity of our results with respect to changes in the strengths of immune interactions assumed in the simulation model. For each combination of the seven parameters mentioned earlier, dengue transmission during the warm-up period was simulated only once. Thereafter, we simulated dengue transmission during the intervention period ten times, each time with a different level of vector control intensity quantified by 𝑝𝑅𝑀 . The set of values taken by 𝑝𝑅𝑀 contained zero, which represented the counterfactual scenario to be used for the calculation of vector control effectiveness as described in the next section (Refer to Table 4.1 for the list of parameter values). The simulation model was implemented using Python 3.9.

Analyses of the simulation output

To measure the overall dengue transmission intensity prior to the implementation of vector control in each simulation run, we computed the proportion of nine-year olds who had prior dengue exposure at the end of the warm-up period (“baseline PE9”). We also calculated the increase in the median age of the entire population during the 30-year intervention period (∆age ). We extracted the monthly number of dengue infections from each simulation run and defined the vector control effectiveness (provided 𝑝𝑅𝑀 > 0) within a time window (𝑡𝑦 , 𝑡𝑦 + ∆𝑡 ] as follows:

Due to the multi-annual incidence patterns of dengue, we set the length of the time window to be five years in the equation above to reduce noise in the estimated underlying trend in the vector control effectiveness. In the rare event that right censoring occurred (i.e., eff(𝑡𝑦,𝑡𝑦 +5] ≥ 0, ∀ 𝑡𝑦 ), we set the value of the critical time point to be 25. Hereafter, the intervention effectiveness calculated for each of the seven time windows mentioned earlier, as well as the critical time point, will be referred to as the “outcome variables”, and we will use 𝒚𝒋 = (𝑦1 𝑗 , … , 𝑦9000 𝑗 )𝑇 to denote the vector containing all the values of an outcome variable 𝑗 (𝑗 = 1, … ,8) obtained from the 9,000 simulation runs where vector control was implemented (i.e., 𝑝𝑅𝑀 > 0). Similarly, the predictor variable matrix will be denoted by 𝐗 = (𝑥𝑠𝑖 ) (𝑠 = 1, … ,9000, 𝑖 = 1, … ,8).

We used the extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) algorithm to capture the nonlinear and interaction effects of the predictor variables on each of the eight outcome variables [263]. To train the XGBoost model for each outcome variable 𝑗, we first split our data (𝐗, 𝒚𝒋 ) into training and test sets using a 9:1 ratio and tuned the following hyper-parameters via 5-fold cross validation performed on the training set: maximum tree depth, the minimum number of observations in each node, the fractions of rows and columns to be randomly sampled to grow each tree, and the learning rate. Given the optimal hyper-parameters, the number of boosting rounds for the final model was determined by monitoring the out-of-sample prediction accuracy evaluated on the test set. Finally, we calculated the “Shapley additive explanations” (SHAP) value 𝑆𝑖𝑗𝑠 for each combination of predictor variable 𝑖, outcome variable 𝑗, and simulation run 𝑠 [264]. Specifically, the SHAP value 𝑆𝑖𝑗𝑠 quantified the change in the predicted value of 𝑦𝑠𝑗 due to 𝑥𝑠𝑖 , where the comparator was set to be the predicted value of 𝑦𝑠𝑗 had the predictor variable 𝑖 been excluded from the XGBoost model. All the analyses of the simulation output were performed using Python 3.9.

Results

Following a sustained reduction in the mosquito population size, the monthly number of dengue infections immediately dropped to near zero and continued to hover just above zero for a number of years, with a marked difference in the time point at which dengue incidence started to rebound across different transmission settings (refer to Figure 4.2 for a visualization of the general model behavior). We found vector control intensity and the baseline PE9 to be the most important predictors for the critical time point (i.e., the time point at which vector control stopped having an impact), followed by the degree of individual-level heterogeneity in the biting risk score (Figure 4.3). Notably, the higher the level of vector control intensity, the more pronounced the negative impacts of baseline PE9 and heterogeneous biting on the critical time point (Figure 4.3). The above findings also held true for the case of vector control effectiveness across the entire 30 years of the intervention period (“overall intervention effectiveness”) (Figure 4.4). During the first five years of the intervention period, vector control was substantially more effective when implemented at a higher intensity, and the distribution of intervention effectiveness became less dispersed as vector control intensity increased (Figure 4.5). However, the median value of intervention effectiveness diminished towards zero over time and became almost indistinguishable between different levels of vector

control intensity in the end (i.e., within the time window (25y, 30y]) (Figure 4.5). Interestingly, the median value of intervention effectiveness was found to be negative within the time window (10y, 15y] when the vector control intensity was within the range of 30%–45% (Figure 4.5). Similar to vector control intensity, none of the other predictor variables had a substantial impact on vector control effectiveness in the long term. For example, an increase in the importation risk (𝜋), from 20 × 10-5, to 24 × 10-5 per human per cycle was estimated to reduce intervention effectiveness by ~15 percentage points on average within the first five years of the intervention period, but the impact became much less significant beyond year 5 (Figure 4.6). Besides, there was an inverse relationship between the baseline PE9 and intervention effectiveness within the first 15 years of the intervention period, with the impact of baseline PE9 being more pronounced at a relatively low vector control intensity during (0y, 5y], moderate vector control intensity during (5y, 10y], and moderately high intensity during (10y, 15y] (Figure 4.7). Thereafter, an increase in the baseline PE9 was associated with a decrease in intervention effectiveness only at high levels of vector control intensity within the time windows (15y, 20y] and (20y, 25y] (Figure 4.7). The same conclusion held true for the relationship between heterogeneous biting and vector control effectiveness, except that during the last five years of the intervention period, there was an overall weak positive relationship between the degree of individual-level heterogeneity in the biting risk score and intervention effectiveness (Figure 4.8). In addition, neither population ageing nor host mobility was found to have a substantial impact on vector control effectiveness within any of the 5-year time windows considered in this study (Figures 4.9 and 4.10), although a 12-year increase in the median age of the population during the 30-year intervention period was estimated to increase the overall intervention effectiveness by ~8 percentage points on average at the highest level of intervention intensity (i.e., 𝑝𝑅𝑀 = 0.7) (Figure 4.4). When we increased the degree of antibody-dependent enhancement in our simulations, there was a modest decrease in both the short-term and the overall intervention effectiveness, as well as the value of the critical time point (Figures 4.11 and 4.12). Furthermore, the average duration of cross-protection was found to have a negligible impact on both the intervention effectiveness and the critical time point (Figures 4.11 and 4.13). Overall, varying the strengths of immune interactions did not result in a substantial change in our model results.

Discussion

Our study highlights the contrast between the shortand long-term impacts of sustained dengue vector control in endemic settings, in congruence with the findings from previous studies: Following the suppression of the Aedes mosquito population, the equilibrium between the basic reproduction number and level of herd-immunity was disrupted, causing dengue incidence to drastically decline [111,128]. This in turn reduced population immunity over time as new births were added to the susceptible pool and immune individuals were gradually removed from the population due to death [111,128]. Consequently, dengue incidence gradually bounced back, and the existing intervention was no longer effective in the end, requiring the intensity of vector control to be further enhanced to reduce dengue transmission [111]. We have additionally demonstrated that the time point at which vector control stopped having an impact depended mainly on the degree of mosquito suppression, pre-intervention transmission

intensity, and the degree of individual-level heterogeneity in the risk of being bitten by the Aedes mosquitoes. It is thus important for future studies to account for the effects of these variables, as well as their interactions, to forecast the time-varying impact of an Aedes control program for planning purposes. For all the levels of vector control intensity considered in this study, a higher pre-intervention transmission intensity was associated with a lower proportion of dengue infections averted within each of the following time windows (0y, 5y], (5y, 10y], and (10y, 15y]. A likely explanation is that an increase in the pre-intervention transmission intensity will generally reduce the average number of years before sustained transmission resumes, thereby causing intervention effectiveness to decrease: Suppose the basic and effective reproduction numbers prior to the intervention are 𝑅0 and 1 respectively (𝑅0 > 1), and hence the fraction of the population being susceptible to dengue at baseline is approximately 1/𝑅0. If vector control reduces the basic reproduction number to 𝑅0 /𝑘 (𝑅0 > 𝑘 > 1) and if the susceptible fraction of the population increases by 𝑐 each year during the early phase of the intervention period, the number of years before the effective reproduction number bounces back to one will be

which is inversely proportional to 𝑅0. While the above conclusion may rely on some simplifying assumptions, our individual-based model has produced similar results, which showed that the baseline PE9 had a negative relationship with the critical time point especially at a high level of vector control intensity. It should be noted, however, that the relationship between baseline PE9 and vector control effectiveness became much weaker after 15 years (except when the mosquito population size was suppressed by over 60%), since dengue incidence had already started to rebound by the 15th year in most of the simulation runs, regardless of the baseline PE9 value. In addition to the important role of pre-intervention transmission intensity in modifying the effectiveness of dengue vector control, our results suggest that the degree of individual-level heterogeneity in the biting risk also needs to be accounted for to forecast the epidemiological impact of a vector control program. Earlier studies have produced strong evidence for the heterogeneous blood feeding patterns of the Aedes mosquitoes using DNA profiling of bloodmeals [253,265], and their results have important implications for vector control. For example, maximum impact will not be achieved if a uniform strategy is implemented in settings where a small number of individuals have a disproportionately large contribution to transmission [266]. Besides, heterogeneous biting may increase the success rate of pathogen invasion, thus making disease eradication more difficult [267]. The results obtained in this

study highlight another challenge posed by heterogeneous biting: When the biting risk is more heterogeneous at the individual level, there will be a higher chance for multiple infectious mosquito bites to land on the same susceptible individual, and the resulting infection cannot be averted unless most, if not all, of the aforementioned mosquito bites are prevented. Hence, given a fixed level of reduction in vector density, the percentage decrease in the number of incident dengue infections will be lower in the presence of heterogeneous biting compared with the case where each infectious mosquito chooses to bite a unique individual. Note that to avoid a high degree of collinearity between the baseline PE9 and the variability in biting risk, we set the maximum standard deviation of the biting risk score conditional on the body surface area to be 1, which corresponds to a setting where 55% of the human population receive 90% of all the mosquito bites. In reality, the distribution of mosquito bites may be more skewed in some transmission settings, where the negative impact of heterogeneous biting on the vector control effectiveness can be even larger than what we estimated in this study. More research that quantifies and explains the individual-level heterogeneity in biting risk would be helpful to inform the development of more effective dengue vector control policies that can be implemented to achieve the desired epidemiological impact. We have observed a weak positive impact of population ageing on the effectiveness of vector control over the 30year intervention period. However, this should not be interpreted as a minor effect of demographic transition on dengue epidemiology, as previous work has shown that the decline in birth and death rates can cause a substantial decrease in the force of infection, as well as a change in both the age distribution of dengue cases and the periodicity of dengue outbreaks [268]. Instead, our results suggest that population ageing may play a relatively limited role in modifying the effect of a vector control program on dengue incidence. Similarly, we did not find host mobility to have an appreciable impact on vector control effectiveness despite its important influence on the epidemic variability and the risk of serotype extinction [36]. Furthermore, our study has shown that the average duration of cross-immunity has a minimal impact on vector control effectiveness, while the impact of antibodydependent enhancement was found to be somewhat higher. Sensitivity analysis should remain an important component of future modelling studies to assess the uncertainty in the predicted effectiveness of dengue vector control with respect to the uncertainty in the strengths of immune interactions.

In the absence of vector control tools that are currently available to be rolled out on a large scale to achieve dengue elimination, the results obtained in this study highlight the need to adopt an integrated approach to achieve a sustained reduction in dengue transmission. Special considerations are needed to maximize the cost-effectiveness of combined interventions in each particular context, given that there may be interactions between different intervention strategies. For example, the effectiveness of the CYD-TDV vaccine was predicted to become lower when combined with targeted indoor residual spraying due to the reduced rate of natural infection [129]. Nonetheless, the combination was shown to outperform either alone in high-transmission settings [129], where it may be still beneficial to consider introducing a combined intervention program in view of the relatively low vector control effectiveness based on our model estimates. There are several limitations in this study. First, we did not explore the impact of spatial heterogeneity in mosquito density on the effectiveness of vector control, and the host movement behaviors have also been simplified in our simulations. More work will be required to study the spatio-temporal variation in vector control effectiveness on heterogeneous landscapes, and how this may be modified by complex host movement patterns. In addition, we also did not consider alternative age structures in the warm-up period of the simulations, and hence the estimated magnitude of interaction between demographic transition and vector control may not generalize to other settings. Besides, our study did not account for the practical deployment of vector control and assumed an instant reduction in the mosquito population size across the entire study area at the start of the intervention period. Scenario planning would also need to be incorporated in future work, to predict the change in the incidence of dengue infections due to unforeseen circumstances such as failure to achieve sustained mosquito suppression. Finally, we may have overestimated the variability in the distribution of vector control effectiveness due to the highly stochastic outputs from the simulation model. Hence, our study has mainly focused on the average effect of each predictor variable on the predicted intervention effectiveness, which we derived separately under different levels of vector control intensity.

Conclusion

We used a detailed individual-based model to demonstrate the contrast between the short-term and long-term impacts of sustained vector control in dengue endemic settings. The finding that short term gains are mostly reversed as population immunity wanes may lie behind the resurgence of dengue in settings such as Singapore where previously successful vector control created a generation with very little immunity to dengue. Such a reduction in immunity necessitates ever stronger vector control efforts to continue to suppress incidence. Our model has also identified factors that have a substantial impact on the effectiveness of vector control, and how the magnitudes of their impacts may vary over time under different levels of vector control intensity. These insights will be useful to inform future model-based studies that seek to predict the impact of dengue vector control in their local contexts. As more countries start to direct their investment towards implementing novel dengue control measures, timely and detailed surveillance data complemented by mathematical modelling will provide valuable evidence to guide the development of public health policies to achieve sustained reduction in the burden of dengue.

Chapter 5: Discussion and conclusion Globally, an estimated total of 6.1 billion people will live in areas at risk of dengue transmission in 2080 [98], and the burden of Zika, yellow fever, and chikungunya is also anticipated to grow as the geographical range of the Aedes mosquitoes further expands [97]. While huge progress has been made in the recent decades in developing diagnostics, vaccines, and novel vector control technologies to reduce the transmission of Aedes-borne diseases, numerous knowledge gaps still remain that pose challenges to the optimized resource allocation for public health surveillance and the formulation of more effective disease control strategies. In this thesis, I used mathematical and statistical modelling to address some of these important research gaps, and the results of my studies are of direct relevance to the making of evidence-based public health policies going forward.

Main findings and their public health implications

In chapter 2, I developed a novel modelling framework integrating diverse sources of information to unveil locations at a high risk of undetected ZIKV transmission during the 2015—2016 global epidemic. Although more than four years have passed since the drastic decline of Zika cases worldwide, the results obtained in this study still remain useful to guide ZIKV surveillance systems strengthening efforts: In July 2021, the Kerala state in India, which is among the 24 high-risk locations identified in our study, reported its first Zika outbreak in history, with more than 60 cases detected as of mid-August [269]. Notably, phylogenetic analysis of partial sequences suggest that the virus from Kerala belonged to the Asian outbreak strain that was previously circulating during the 2015—2016 Zika pandemic, and retrospective testing of serum samples indicates that ZIKV had already started to spread silently prior to the index case infection [269]. Moreover, the Maharashtra state in India (again one of the 24 high-risk locations) also detected their first ever Zika case in July 2021 [270]. The recent Zika outbreak in India has demonstrated the value of our study results for pinpointing locations that should be prioritized for surveillance system strengthening, which is critical for mitigating the risk of another large-scale Zika epidemic in the future. In chapter 3, I built regression models using longitudinal data generated from Singapore’s Gravitrap surveillance system to identify the environmental and/or anthropogenic variables that best explain the spatio-temporal

dynamics of the Aedes population in the country’s high-rise public residential zones. I found building age to be the strongest predictor for the abundance of both Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus in Singapore, and this new finding can be used to improve the allocation of resources for vector control inspections and environmental management. Besides, a lot of land-cover variables (e.g., managed vegetation cover) were found to have a statistically significant association with Aedes abundance, and the interesting insights generated from this work can inspire future studies that attempt to utilize satellite imagery data to predict the spatial abundance patterns of Aedes mosquitoes in their local urban environments. Furthermore, my results showed that weather had a much weaker impact on the Aedes abundance in Singapore’s public housing estates than previously thought, suggesting that policy makers may need to re-evaluate the use of meteorological data to guide vector control operations. In chapter 4, I developed an agent-based model to study the time-varying epidemiological impact of sustained Aedes control in a wide range of dengue endemic settings. I showed that while a fixed level of reduction in vector density may result in a temporary cessation of dengue transmission, the expected value of intervention effectiveness would gradually diminish towards zero in the long run unless the lowered vector density is able to reduce the basic reproduction number to below one, which can be extremely difficult to achieve given the currently available tools for Aedes control. Furthermore, I have identified the main drivers of the difference in vector control effectiveness across different transmission settings. Specifically, the percentage reduction in dengue infections is determined not only by the level of reduction in mosquito density, but also by the transmission intensity prior to intervention rollout and the individual-level heterogeneity in biting risk. I further showed that the intensity of vector control interacts with the other two factors mentioned earlier, and the interaction magnitude also changes over time. My results underscore the need for policy makers to consider the joint effects of mosquito density reduction, preintervention transmission intensity, and heterogeneous biting on dengue incidence during vector control policy planning. Moreover, the contrast between the short-term and long-term impacts of sustained vector control shown in this study highlight the importance of continued investments in developing and testing more effective vector control technologies, as well as the need to adopt an integrated approach whereby vector control is implemented in combination with vaccination to achieve sustained reduction in the burden of dengue.

Future work

A key contribution of my study in chapter 2 is the identification of locations that should be prioritized for ZIKV surveillance systems enhancement in the future. More testing should also be conducted in these locations to examine whether any past or ongoing ZIKV transmission is present, which will help to further narrow the gaps in understanding the geographical extent of the virus and to strengthen global Zika outbreak preparedness and response capacity. Besides, for countries that were hard-hit by ZIKV during the 2015—2016 pandemic, further research is required to study the long-term antibody kinetics after ZIKV infection and how this may impact protective immunity, which will shed light on the future risk of a new Zika outbreak. Reliable diagnostics also need to be developed to distinguish between the Asian and African ZIKV lineages, the latter of which has been demonstrated to have higher transmissibility and pathogenicity [271] and has recently been isolated from both Aedes mosquitoes and non-human primates in Brazil [272]. To date, little consensus has been reached as to where dengue transmission occurs. While my study in chapter 3 has generated interesting insights into the spatio-temporal variation of Aedes abundance in Singapore’s public housing estates, more research is required to quantify the proportion of dengue cases attributable to infections inside versus outside of home so that the epidemiological impact of vector control can be further enhanced. Future research should also seek to understand how the spatial distribution of Aedes mosquitoes, host movement behaviors, and the immune landscape jointly influence the complex transmission dynamics of dengue in Singapore. Filling this knowledge gap will be critical to optimizing resource allocation to reduce dengue transmission. Finally, my results in chapter 4 highlight the important influence of heterogeneous biting on the effectiveness of dengue vector control. More research is needed to empirically quantify the individual-level heterogeneity in exposure to mosquito bites in their local contexts and to evaluate its potential impact on the effectiveness of dengue control measures, including both vector control and vaccination. Besides, as more countries start to implement intensive Aedes control measures, it will be beneficial to develop new approaches to integrate diverse sources of information collected from the field during the early phase of the intervention, such as dengue seroprevalence, proportion of reported cases being imported versus locally acquired, and individual-level dengue case data, to predict how long the impact of the current intervention will last. Recent studies that created new

methods to infer malaria transmission dynamics in near-elimination settings may provide useful insights into how this might be achieved [185,273].

Final statement

In this thesis, I used mathematical and statistical modelling to expand what is currently known about the epidemiology and ecology of Aedes-borne diseases as well as the impact of Aedes control on disease transmission. It is hoped that the work produced in this thesis will help to improve the evidence base for the making of key public health policy decisions to reduce the burden of Aedes-borne diseases at the city, national, and global scales.

A2. Additional information on the global risk model validation We took the list of 𝑅0 estimates that were obtained by Armstrong et al. (2020) via a systematic search within Web of Science and PubMed, and applied the following selection criteria: 1) The 𝑅0 value was derived by estimating the early growth rate of the epidemic based on the reported case data (i.e., using a simple exponential growth model, or Richards’ growth model). In other words, we did not include estimates derived from mechanistic models, which could vary substantially in terms of the functional forms as well as the input parameter values used. In fact, even for the same location and time window, there can be very different literature estimates of 𝑅0 obtained by these models, where the differences in model specifications played an important role. This is not to say that these model results were all invalid, but the complex nature of the models has made it challenging to standardize the results, as well as to examine the validity of the methods given the large number of studies included in the list. Hence, we only included estimates that were directly driven by the reported case data as described in the beginning. 2) The time period of the reported case data that were used to estimate 𝑅0 was clearly specified. This would allow us to average our 𝑅0 estimates across the corresponding time window to be compared with the literature estimate. 3) The spatial resolution of the location of the reported case data that were used to estimate 𝑅0 should not be higher than the spatial unit of analysis in our study. 4) The width of the uncertainty interval of the 𝑅0 estimate should not exceed 10.
