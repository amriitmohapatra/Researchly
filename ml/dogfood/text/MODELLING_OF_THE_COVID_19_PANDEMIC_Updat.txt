SUMMARY

On 11 March 2020, coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) was declared as a pandemic by WHO at 118,319 cases and 4,292 deaths reported globally. As of 31 December 2021，278 million cases have been reported across the world, and among all reported cases, there have been more than 44,000,000 (16.0% of all reported cases) cases in the WHO’s South-east Asia region, 11,000,000 (4% of all reported cases) in the Western Pacific, and 2,177,701 (0.8% of all reported cases) cases in Singapore. COVID-19 posed severe challenges to healthcare systems in the early phase of the pandemic, resulting in a shortage of healthcare resources including personal protective equipment (PPE), intensive care unit (ICU) beds and inpatient beds, which not only left frontline healthcare workers (HCWs) at grave risk, but also brought challenges for policymakers and society as a whole. Studies showed that N95 respirators (N95s) offer good protection against viral respiratory pathogens, especially during aerosol generating procedures or when a patient’s COVID-19 status is positive or unknown. Accordingly, N95s are in high demand for HCWs compared with other PPE categories due to the limited production capacity. To address the shortage of N95s, health care planners suggested the extensive-use or re-use of N95s. A study which screened 27 countries or regions revealed that only 5 countries (19% of 27 countries or regions) allowed extended use; 2 countries (7% of 27 countries or regions) mentioned reuse; and 3 countries (11% of 27 countries or regions) recommended both strategies for rationing N95s. Therefore, soon after of the pandemic, optimizing N95s requirements based on different strategies to maximize the protection of HCWs safety has been an urgent issue.

While timely, stringent, and effective intervention policies, as implemented in many countries in early 2020, was demonstrated to have mitigated deteriorating COVID-19 outbreaks, they were not viable long-term strategies as it was impractical to implement strict lockdowns or border control policies for the months or years for mass vaccination occurred. Obviously, COVID-19 infection has setting-specific heterogeneity due to differences in living and/or health conditions, such as the significant differences between general communities and nursing homes, migrant workers, homeless and travellers. In addition, outbreaks in army and prison settings were reported. Hence, it is essential to update responsive interventions promptly according to the type of infection by realtime forecasting of COVID-19. Such forecasting can inform the need to escalate or reduce the degree of interventions, which has proven to be one of the approaches taken by the Singapore government to avoid rapid resurgence following lockdowns. Singapore began lockdown measures for the COVID-19 outbreak on 6 April 2020, enforcing social distancing and widespread closure of schools, workplaces, and commercial buildings. Exiting lockdown on 1 June 2020, two significant heterogeneous disease states emerged in Singapore: a low endemic community state and a high dormitory outbreak state (over 95% of cases belong to the latter). Consequently, policy responses differed between sites. In dormitory settings, operators were expected to limit inter-mixing of workers from different blocks or sites, performed movement tracking and regular 14day swabbing of uninfected workers. We seek to understand the spread between each dormitory to improve the interventions strategies to minimise the risk of new disease clusters emerging.

This thesis aims to use statistical and mathematical models to predict and evaluate the COVID-19 pandemic in a timely manner, and provide reliable evidence for public health officials, health care providers and individuals to better prepare and respond to the disease. Specifically, Chapter 1 provides an overview of the development of COVID-19 pandemic, including the chronological spread events and epidemiological characteristics of COVID-19, the implementation of public health control measures, the development of the COVID-19 vaccine, and the importance of modelling the spread of the pandemic and modelling control measures to evaluate their effect on the spread. Chapter 2 estimates the demands of N95s required for the healthcare system in the early outbreak of the pandemic, among three epidemic levels under different strategies, including single-use, extenduse, and prolong-use policies, to run with the maximized possible protection for HCWs. Chapter 3 proposes to develop a system to estimate COVID-19 cases of different infection types in Singapore and forecasts of ICU needs evolving from all infections while multiple epidemics were concurrently ongoing. Chapter 4 explores the contact network in dormitories in the period once transmission levels had fallen, and further develops a simulation model to evaluate multiple control measures via the empirical contact patterns.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION 1.1 Chronology of the COVID-19 pandemic Coronavirus Disease (COVID-19), caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) (1–3), was one of the three global coronavirus outbreaks in the world over the past two decades (4). The other two were severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS-CoV) and Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV) in 2003 and 2012, with mortality rates of 10% and 37%, respectively (5–9). Although SARS-CoV-2 has a lower mortality rate (10), it is overwhelmingly more contagious and can spread rapidly between individuals and countries because of its low severity. As of this writing, the COVID-19 outbreak has been ongoing. DECEMBER 2019 — The first record of pneumonia-like symptoms for COVID-19 was on 1 December 2019, in Wuhan, the capital of Hubei Province, China (11), while some phylogenetic analyses showed that the SARS-CoV-2 has first arisen in October or November 2019 (12–14). Subsequently, some sources quoted more patients presenting the same symptoms on December 8, the first day of the COVID-19 outbreak (15). On December 27, the Hubei Provincial Hospital of Integrated Chinese & Western Medicine reported three new coronavirus cases causing abnormal pneumonia to the China Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (16). Confirmed with an investigation, China CDC officially reported to the World Health Organisation (WHO) that there was a cluster of 27 pneumonia-like cases with an unclear source of viral transmission, of which seven were in critical condition on 31 December 2019 (15).

JANUARY 2020 — The outbreak in China has started. On January 8, SARSCoV-2 was officially announced as the causative pathogen of the outbreak by WHO (17,18). The day after, the first death (a 61-year older man with severe underlying conditions) among 41 confirmed cases was reported in China (19). WHO shared preliminary results of whole-genome sequencing with the public on January 12 (20). The results show that the novel coronavirus has similarities with the SARS-CoV. Since then, cases have been detected nationwide in China, starting from Shenzhen and Guangdong (21,22). Cases reported in Shenzhen were in the same household, indicating human-to-human transmission of SARS-CoV-2 (21). A large-scale outbreak was observed in Wuhan, and the virus continued to spread for two main reasons. One is that the novel coronavirus hit during the

Chinese New Year, an annual reunion that culminates in the peak traffic

movement between cities throughout China. Another one is that limited knowledge of SARS-CoV-2 prevented the rapid identification of cases (23–25). After that, the Beijing government isolated Wuhan, the epicentre. The lockdown policy was first proved to be a successful and effective intervention in controlling the virus’s spread in the early pandemic outbreak (26,27). Transmission of SARS-CoV-2 outside China began in early January 2020, starting in Thailand, a 61-year-old Chinese traveller from Wuhan reported on January 8 (28–30). A week later, on January 16, Japan confirmed its first case (31). In the second half of January, South Korea (32), Singapore (33), Vietnam (34), Nepal (35), Malaysia (36), Cambodia (37), Sri Lanka (38), India (39) and the Philippines (40) reported their first cases in succession. On January 21, the first case was identified in Washington, US (41), indicating the beginning of

COVID-19 outside Asia. Following that, France reported its first cases on January 24 showing that SARS-CoV-2 had arrived in the European region (42). With the widespread of the virus, on January 30, WHO declared COVID-19 a public health emergency of international concern (43). As of 31 January 2020, there were 9,826 cases reported worldwide, including 9782 cases in the Western Pacific, 17 in South-East Asia, 9 in the Region of the Americas, 14 in the European Region, and 4 in the Eastern Mediterranean (44). FEBRUARY 2020 — COVID-19 continues to spread globally. On February 11, the new coronavirus disease was officially named COVID-19 by the WHO. In the same month, the first death case outside China was reported in the Philippines on February 2 (45). By the end of February, 37 countries reported their first COVID-19 cases, including the first case in Egypt, the African continent, on February 14 (46), bringing the spread of COVID-19 to a total of 62 countries or territories with 85,403 reported cases and 2,924 deaths (45). Among those, China, South Korea, Iran and Italy heavily experienced their first epidemic wave. MARCH 2020 — The spread of COVID-19 has severely affected the world, especially the European Region. On 11 March 2020, WHO declared that COVID-19 is a pandemic (47). This month, 148 countries were first infected by SARS-CoV-2, with the reported cumulative cases and death as 750,890 and 36,405, respectively (48). Europe accounted for 63.5% of all cases reported this month, showing severe suffering from the COVID-19 pandemic, while the initial epicentre—China—represented a sharp decline in the number of new cases, with zero new cases in the general community on March 30 (49).

The pandemic has posed severe challenges to healthcare systems, resulting in a shortage of healthcare resources. At the end of February, WHO announced that there was a global shortage of personal protective equipment (PPE), which indeed leaves frontline healthcare workers (HCWs) at grave risk (50). Unfortunately, the resource shortage continued within the next half month. On March 3, WHO warned of a shortage of PPE and a lack of testing capacity. Thus strategy recommendations were elevated accordingly (50,51). APRIL to DECEMBER 2020 — A study showed that pre-symptomatic and asymptomatic

implementing social measures and developing a COVID-19 vaccine are crucial to prevent the spread of COVID-19. On April 16, WHO published guidelines for adjustments to public health and social measures, including mass movement restrictions, commonly referred to as "lockdowns" (53). In May, the WHO adjusted the guidelines of contagious measurements by varying degrees of restrictions among different transmission scenarios in heterogeneous settings. For instance, intensive measures have been implemented in dense places or vulnerable populations such as workplaces, schools and nursing homes, and mass gatherings have been restricted (54). Despite implementing public health and social measures, the transmission of SARS-CoV-2 continued. A total of 15 countries reported their first cases of COVID-19 from April to June (13 countries in April and 2 in May). As of June 30, the reported cases exceeded 10 million worldwide (55). At the end of August, this number reached around 25 million, with 800,000 reported death (56). Moreover, those numbers reached 33 million and 1 million at the end of September (57). Meanwhile, a survey showed that healthcare systems were

disrupted in almost every affected country, with lowand middle-income countries reporting the most significant difficulties (58). As of October, over 42 million cases and 1.1 million deaths have been reported worldwide (59). Furthermore, 3 new countries and territories were affected by SARS-CoV-2. Subsequently, the cumulative number of globally reported cases reached ~50 million in November, and the European Region accounted for the most significant proportion of new cases and deaths (60). In December, there were three new variants of SARS-CoV-2, designated as Alpha (B.1.1.7 and Q lineages), Beta (B.1.351 and descendent lineages), and Gamma (P.1 and descendent lineages) spread widely (61). They were first detected in the United Kingdom, South Africa, and Brazil, respectively (62–64). With new variants spreading more extremely and existing vaccines offering less protection against infection, the total number of reported COVID-19 cases had surpassed 79.2 million with over 1.7 million deaths (65), and SARS-CoV-2 had not infected only 17 countries and territories worldwide by the end of 2020. Scientists have pledged to work with the WHO to speed up the development of a vaccine against COVID-19. Brief chronologies of vaccine development can be found in Section 1.3.2. In addition, the treatment of COVID-19 has been developed since April. At the end of May, WHO announced the clinical management of COVID-19 to standardize the care and management of COVID19 cases (66). In June, there were several improvements in health technologies for COVID-19 patients. For example, dexamethasone has been used to rescue severe cases (67); and hydroxychloroquine was discontinued for the nonreduction of mortality for hospitalised COVID-19 patients (68). Furthermore, the second summit on COVID-19 research and innovation held by WHO on

July 1 agreed that more trials testing antivirals, immunomodulatory drugs, antithrombotic agents, and combination therapies at different stages of the disease were necessary. Moreover, in September, antigen-based rapid diagnostic tests (ART tests) were suggested by WHO in the community, which can identify cases rapidly and alleviate the burden of testing laboratories (69,70). JANUARY to DECEMBER 2021 — The implementation of the COVID-19 vaccine program has started worldwide. In January 2021, the recommendation of using the Moderna COVID-19 vaccine was issued by WHO (71), and 40 million doses of the Pfizer–BioNTech vaccine were ordered by COVAX (72). From February, the COVID-19 vaccine was distributed globally (73). As a result, half of WHO's Member States will have vaccinated 40% of their population by the end of 2021 (74). Another study showed that 9.25 billion COVID-19 vaccines were administered in 2021. However, most doses (76–78%) were distributed in highand upper-middle-income countries (75). Also, the vaccination data worldwide was tracked and published on the WHO COVID19 dashboard from March (1). By January 25, 70 countries and 31 territories across six WHO regions reported SARS-CoV-2 with the three new variants — Alpha, Beta and Gamma. Peaks of those new variants appeared in January; however, there was no indication of the disease severity caused by them (76–78). As of February 23, there have been 110.7 million cumulative cases and more than 2.4 million deaths, with three main variants circulating simultaneously (79). In March, the cumulative of reported cases increased by 14%, reaching over 126 million with more than 2.7 million deaths (80).

In April 2021, a new variant labelled Delta swept India leading to more than 400,000 daily reported cases (61). In addition, it has been shown to have faster viral replication and greater infectiousness (81–84). Subsequently, the Delta variant sparked a new round of COVID-19 waves in over 170 countries in the remaining year (81,85), including the severe waves observed in the UK (86), the US (87,88), and countries in Africa, Europe and Asia (89–92). A new variant named Omicron was first reported in South Africa in November 2021 and has since rapidly spread globally (61,93). It has been proven more transmissible but less severe than the Delta variant (94,95). Multiple variants brought the cumulative number of cases to 278 million by the end of 2021(96), and the world was panicking again. JANUARY 2022 onwards — Several studies have shown that unvaccinated or two doses of COVID-19 vaccine are insufficient to prevent new variants from being infected (i.e., Omicron and Delta variant), so booster vaccine doses are highly recommended (97–100). By mid-August 2022, 29.5 per 100 people had received a booster worldwide (101). Global vaccination rates are on the rise, and Omicron — a variant that is less severe than other variants, has started to become the dominant variant worldwide (102). The US and other countries have gradually lifted COVID-19 social restrictions since February 2022. For example, compulsory maskwearing when outside was not required in the US and UK from February (103,104). In addition, from June 2022, rescinding the COVID-19 test requirement for international travel (105,106).

Furthermore, more and more countries removed COVID-19 rules, including China. From December 2022, China has lifted strict intervention policies, including lockdown and mandatory facilities isolation of cases. On December 23, the Healthy Code system was closed. And from January 2023, quarantine for inbound travellers has been removed (107).

Epidemiology, clinical manifestations, the transmission of

COVID-19 Epidemiological characteristics of COVID-19 have been shifting over time with the mutation of virus variants. At the beginning of the transmission, older men (with co-morbidities) are more likely to develop severe symptoms or even die (108). However, the age differentiation has weakened since the Delta variant attack (109). Additionally, initial racial and ethnic disparities in COVID-19 infection rate have been reduced from Asian, Black and Hispanic ethnic groups, although individuals of black and Asian ethnicity are at relative higher risk of COVID-19 infection compared to Hispanic ethnic groups (110). The median incubation period for SARS-CoV-2 is shorter for the Delta and Omicron variants than for the Alpha (83,111,112). Over time, the generation time of SARS-CoV-2 also gets shorter, indicating a faster transmission (83,113). However, the mortality rate is different, rising first with the predominance of the Delta variant in 2021, then decreasing with the Omicron variant ascendancy in 2022 (114–116). Fever, muscle aches, anosmia, dysgeusia, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, malaise/fatigue, and reported headaches are the main symptoms of all variants of COVID-19, while shortness of breath is not common when the Omicron predominance (117–122).

However, with the new variants (Delta and Omicron) overwhelming the world, the effective reproduction number increased again (127–130). Studies also found that the transmission of COVID-19 differs in various settings. The secondary attack rate of household transmission can be up to 32.4% (131,132). Public health interventions and vaccination programmes

Public health interventions

COVID-19 is a highly contagious disease, first validated in an enclosed environment– the Diamond Princess cruise ship off Japan, with an initial reproduction number 𝑅0 estimated above 11 (141–143). Lessons learnt from the

previous pandemic–public health measures can effectively mitigate the spread of infectious diseases (144–146). Therefore, individualand community-level interventions have been implemented to contain COVID-19 transmissions.

First, wearing a face mask shows strong protection for individuals from

COVID-19 infection (147,148), and the WHO has recommended limiting the spread of the virus. Research evidence indicated that using a face mask could significantly reduce the infection risk (148). Keeping good personal hygiene, such as sanitisation of hands and protection of eyes in public and health-care settings from containment, effectively decreases the transmission of infectious diseases (148,149). A study has shown that respiratory tract infections can be reduced by 6%–44% in various settings via hand hygiene intervention (149). In addition, community-level interventions include contact tracing and quarantine of close contacts, school and workplace closures, reduction of mass gatherings, border temperature screening, travel restrictions (including quarantine of importation, health status declaim and Polymerase Chain Reaction [PCR] test) and surveillance. They have been proven effective in limiting the spread of SARS-CoV2 and controlling the COVID-19 pandemic when COVID-19 vaccines had been developing in the early period of the pandemic (141,143,150,151). Such as, from

4 February to 19 February 2020, 619 of 3700 passengers and crew (17%) confirmed cases were reported; however, the infection rate would reach 79% if there had been no timely implementation of non-pharmaceutical interventions (143). Thus, policymakers implement those in mitigating COVID-19 transmissions (150,152).

Vaccination programmes

Since April 2020, clinical trials of the COVID-19 vaccine have been in progress. On June 24, China approved the CanSino vaccine for emergency use in the military and high-risk groups (153). Moderna and Pfizer, as the leaders in the race of vaccine development, provided promising results regarding vaccine effectiveness in early-phase clinical trials and on non-human primate models in July (154,155). From August, Phase III trials of Moderna and Pfizer immediately began, and both showed encouraging efficacy rates of vaccines (154,155). As of December 2020, 52 candidate vaccines were in human trials. Among the candidates, the vaccines developed by Pfizer-BioNTech (i.e., BNT162b2 mRNA COVID-19 vaccine) (156,157) and the Moderna (i.e., mRNA-1273 COVID-19 vaccine) (158) were given emergency use authorisation by the FDA in December 2020. Since July 2020, the COVID-19 vaccines global access (COVAX) facility (159) has accepted more than 150 countries' applications to guarantee their rapid, fair and equitable access to COVID-19 vaccines (160). As a result, as of February 2021, more than 250 million people worldwide have been vaccinated against COVID-19 (161). Furthermore, as of September 2022, 67.9% of the world population has received at least one dose of a COVID-19 vaccine (10). In addition, research evidence suggested that a two-dose regimen of BNT162b2 provided 95% protection in people 16 years of age or older from diagnosis of infection, and mRNA-1273 conferred 94.1% protection against being diagnosed as COVID-19 in the population of 18 years old or above, respectively (154).

While the two-dose vaccine was less effective in protecting people from Delta and Omicron variants (162), a booster dose showed efficacy (98,100). 1.3.3

Planning and selection of interventions

Effective interventions to the pandemic should be practical, responding to the features of the different variants. They should not cause a worse impact in terms of a social and economic burden than the pandemic itself. Thus, selecting suitable interventions for the pandemic is challenging for policymakers and public health professionals. For example, extreme non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs), such as lockdowns, were effective until vaccines were rolled out. Continuing strict NPIs when promoting vaccination globally could pose social and economic burdens (163–165). The transmission dynamics, the severity and the spread of infection are essential when determining the appropriate intervention strategies. For example, extreme and comprehensive NPIs should be implemented for an early outbreak with higher morbidity/mortality and attack rates and when lacking vaccines and essential healthcare resources, such as the pandemic caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus (excluding Omicron variants). Furthermore, when these factors are unclear, an effective surveillance system combined with appropriate statistical models to rapidly assess and understand critical features is critical in selecting recommended interventions. Additionally, heterogeneous infection in different settings is crucial to investigate when effectively tailoring intervention strategies. For instance, in a closed or semiclosed environment with close contact, such as migrant worker dormitories, schools, jails, etc., which has been proven to be a high-risk setting for spreading infectious

diseases, such as COVID-19, comprehensive NPIs are necessary to curb the spread of the pandemic (166,167).

Overview of thesis

As presented in chapter 1.1, before the vaccination programme has been implemented globally, the world experienced the COVID-19 pandemic from an unknown novel coronavirus and lack of healthcare resources to panic with high mortality and morbidity rates. In the early outbreak of COVID-19, the cases were climbing aggressively. In addition, the lack of healthcare resources made society havoc and pushed HCWs at grave risk, causing a considerable challenge to societies, especially healthcare societies. Meanwhile, the WHO announced that PPE was in shortage, and then the WHO recommended re-use or extended-use policies (168). Moreover, studies showed that N95 respirators (N95s) offer good protection against viral respiratory pathogens, especially during aerosol-generating procedures or when a patient’s COVID-19 status is positive or unknown. Accordingly, N95s are in high demand for HCWs compared with other PPE categories due to the limited production capacity. To address the shortage of N95s, healthcare planners have suggested the extensive-use or re-use of N95s (171). A study which screened 27 countries or regions revealed that only five countries (19%) allowed extended use, two countries (7%) mentioned reuse, and three countries (11%) recommended both strategies for rationing N95s (172). Therefore, from the beginning stages of the pandemic, optimising N95 requirements based on

different strategies to maximise the protection of HCWs safety has been an urgent issue. While timely, stringent and effective intervention policies, such as strict lockdowns or strict border control rules, have been demonstrated to have mitigated deteriorating COVID-19 outbreaks in the early outbreak of COVID19, they are not viable long-term strategies (173,174). Obviously, COVID-19 infection has setting-specific heterogeneity due to differences in living and/or health conditions (175,176), such as the significant differences between general communities and nursing homes (177–179), migrant workers (MWs) (136,180,181), homeless (182,183) and travellers. In addition, outbreaks in the army (184) and prison (185–187) settings have been reported. Hence, it is essential to update responsive interventions in a timely manner according to the type of infection by real-time forecasting of COVID-19. Furthermore, such forecasting can inform the need to escalate or reduce the degree of interventions, which has proven to be one of the approaches taken by the Singapore government to avoid rapid resurgence following the lockdown. Vaccines have been proven efficient to COVID-19 in preventing people from severe disease, as shown in Section 1.3.2. However, the cases climb in closed/semi-closed, high-risk, density accommodation settings, such as migrant worker dormitories, jails, schools, etc. In addition, in the event of a viral respiratory pandemic due to new viral pathogens, the world may require a prompt response to curb the transmission, especially in high-risk and density environments to control rapid virus transmission. Therefore, investigation of the impact of contact patterns on virus spread in closed/semi-closed, high-risk,

density environmental settings is critical for better preparation for future pandemics. 1.4.2

This PhD thesis aims to use statistical and mathematical models to prepare promptly, understand and evaluate the COVID-19 pandemic and provide reliable evidence for public health officials, healthcare providers and individuals to prepare better and respond to the disease. In addition, this proposed thesis fills current knowledge gaps and assists in evaluating and monitoring the pandemic trends to guide effective non-pharmaceutical public health intervention policies. Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic is posing severe challenges to healthcare systems, leading to shortages of PPE which leaves frontline HCWs at grave risk. Among the lessons learned during the outbreaks of SARS-CoV in 2003 and the MERSCoV in 2012 were the need for stringent infection control in healthcare settings, clear criteria for isolation and quarantine measures, and continued evaluation of the effectiveness of PPE in infection prevention (188,189). The WHO has warned of the risk of the global supply of PPE rapidly depleting (190). Despite these effort, hospitals have faced severe shortages of PPE (191). Shortages of PPE lead to HCWs being dangerously ill-prepared to care for patients with COVID-19 (51). Studies showed that N95s offer good protection

against viral respiratory pathogens, especially during aerosol generating procedures or when a patient’s COVID-19 status is positive or unknown (192– 194). N95s are of critical importance for confronting the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic among the various PPEs. Strategies to optimise the usage of N95s are therefore vital. These should focus on conserving N95s use while providing adequate protection for HCWs working with COVID-19 patients. HCWs may need to have contact with confirmed or suspected patients and their surroundings within inpatient facilities, often over an entire work shift. Extended-use—or even reuse—of N95s has therefore been suggested, with recommendations in place to use respirators for up to 4 hours if N95s is in short supply (168,195,196). A study which screened 27 countries or regions revealed that only 5 countries (19%) allowed extended use; 2 countries (7%) mentioned reuse; and 3 countries (11%) recommended both strategies for rationing N95s (168). Several ways to decontaminate N95s for reuse have also been well studied (197). The potential extended-use of N95s requires serious consideration as the demand for respirators will increase in response to rising case counts, especially among those with severe disease who require intensive care and prolonged hospitalization. One in five cases in China had severe symptoms requiring long stays at hospital facilities (198), while a quarter of those required admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) due to complications of acute respiratory distress syndrome, arrhythmia and shock, which significantly increase hospital stay (199). The same study observed a median hospital stay of 10 days (IQR 7–14) among those discharged. Per patient, multiple N95s are consequently required to avoid transmission between HCWs and patients.

In Singapore, a city of ~5.6 million people in the Malay Archipelago, 51,197 cases were diagnosed until 28 July 2020 (200). This number is expected to rise in the coming months, and to ensure adequate provisions it is important to project the number of N95s required. Here we estimate the number of N95s required over the course of the epidemic under three strategies: single-use, extended-use and prolonged-use. By estimating the demand, we aim to aid planning efforts assessing what preparations are required over the coming months for the healthcare system to run with the maximized possible protection for its workers. Although described for the Singapore setting, our results hold for planners in similarly sized cities elsewhere.

Methods

We developed a Susceptible-Exposed-Infectious-Recovered (SEIR) model to estimate the number of cases with COVID-19 being present in healthcare facilities, and necessary N95s use under three policies: single-use, extended-use and prolonged-use. For planning purposes, we developed three variants of the SEIR model which describe the relative outbreak size, labelled as mild, severe and moderate.

Epidemic Scenarios and model structure

In the Susceptible-Exposed-Infectious-Recovered model (SEIR), we classified the outcomes of COVID-19 infection into six levels, relating to the need for hospitalisation or being admitted to the ICU, and the risk of onward transmission (Figure 1). These were: level 1 for asymptomatic infections; level 2a for mild symptoms that remain undiagnosed at healthcare facilities; level 2b for mild symptomatic ambulatory cases which are identified; level 3 for those

who required hospitalisation but not the ICU for their recovery; level 4 for those who entered ICU on admission; and level 5 for those dying among levels 3 and 4. We combined levels 3–5 as severe and levels 1–2 as mild. We also combined levels 2b-4 as known and levels 1–2a as cryptic, i.e., cases which are not documented by the healthcare system.

Suspected cases are divided into negative cases and positive cases based on two consecutive coronavirus RT-PCR tests. Negative cases included those with mild symptoms that remain undiagnosed at healthcare facilities (level 2a) and suspected but negative cases. The positive cases included mild symptomatic ambulatory cases (level 2b), those requiring hospitalisation (level 3) and ICU cases (level 4). To assess the effects of case misclassification, six ratios were used to reflect the proportion of suspected but negative cases with positive cases, ranging from 0– 10 (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10). A zero ratio (1:0) has no case misclassification and conversely a ratio of 10 indicates significant uncertainty in preliminary diagnosis upon admission where a substantial proportion are incorrectly identified as being COVID-19 positive but later are confirmed to be negative. A separate set of analyses were conducted where rapid testing was implemented, or a zero ratio, at 2 to 5 months from the epidemic start point, to estimate its potential effects on usage of N95s.

Under the mild epidemic scenario, a 5–month and 2–month delay in the introduction of rapid diagnostic testing results in N95 usage of 33.3 million and 28.6 million for single-use, 22.4 million and 19.2 million for extended-use, and 4.4 million and 3.8 million for prolonged-use (Figure 8C).

Discussion

Optimal N95 use is required for national and global supplies to be sustained, ensuring HCWs safety and allowing for proper patient care across all healthcare facilities. We projected N95s usage across a wide range of scenarios where for a moderate epidemic scenario, our projections of consumption for a single-use policy with no misclassification of disease at hospital entry (ratio 1:0) was found to be 117.1 million, 71.6 million and 12.8 million. This reduced by 38.9% and 89.1% when extended and prolonged-use policies are implemented. These considerable reductions can extend the lifetime of N95s stocks across time and

allow for a wider distribution between different regions or countries to reduce the stress on the demand. When more case misclassification occurred at a ratio of 1:10, N95s use increased to 1.10 billion, 748.4 million, and 148.2 million on, which would be considerably more challenging to obtain and distribute effectively. The introduction of a rapid diagnostic kit at any point of the epidemic which can immediately ascertain if an individual is positive of SARSCoV-2 is therefore advantageous and should be immediately implemented when possible provided test accuracy is high.

Our approach can be utilised elsewhere with considerations of the

implementation of such extended-use policies. This is especially paramount at this time as the WHO, despite already shipping more than 900,000 surgical masks, 62,000 N95s and 34,000 face shields with other PPEs to 133 countries to supplement supplies for those at contingency or crisis capacities, has stated that overall global supplies are rapidly depleting (208). This is further complicated by the fact that the PPE supply chain landscape is complex. Numerous national PPE stockpiling systems exist, such as the US Strategic National Stockpile, Canada’s National Emergency Strategic Stockpile, Australia’s National Medical Stockpile and Taiwan’s 3-tier stockpile (209).

These often require multiple private contractors and vendors to balance

procurement and deployment to facilitate demand and require prioritization in allocating resources to different hospitals and healthcare centres with the ongoing supply strain. Manufacturers, particularly in China, are also struggling to ramp up production due to the travel restrictions and quarantining procedures in place which requires other strategies to be considered such as extended-use.

During the last pandemic (influenza A(H1N1-2009)), Hashikura and Kizu recommended a minimum of 8 weeks of PPE supply for stockpiling with 4 sets being utilised per day by high risk workers, 2 sets for medium and low risk groups, 2 surgical masks for each worker and inpatient, and 1 for every suspected case (210). Recommendations at the Ministry of Health, Singapore, are more risk averse by aiming to maintain a 3 to 6-month stockpile, with individual capacities set for each medical institution (211). The overall stockpile capacity is a function of the renewal rate among workers, the protective robustness of the equipment and corresponding costs. Based on our findings, the extended-use policy could be utilised within wards before supply chains are stressed where multiple infected patients are being visited simultaneously, provided infection risk can be mitigated. Should infection become widespread with significant patient load at healthcare facilities, prolonged-use may have to be utilised to prevent supply shortages. Where possible, it is recommended that gowns and N95s use be prioritized for high-risk aerosol generating procedures including endotracheal intubation and bronchoscopy (168). With 42% of 52 ICU patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation and 13.5% of this group acquiring the infection in hospital, considerable transmission risk exists (212,213). This risk is further exacerbated by the presence of environmental contamination from patients (214). Singapore is currently at an orange level of its Disease Outbreak Response System Condition, which reflects a high risk of importations of cases and large clusters of cases occurring within foreign worker dormitories. Although PPE, including N95s, only forms a component of the protective procedures, it remains crucial alongside limiting the number of encounters, implementation of

negative pressure isolation rooms, physical barriers and exclusion of non COVID-19 patients by testing. Communication with the public remains the first barrier of defence with recommendations of appropriate mask usage and prioritization of supplies for HCWs and symptomatic individuals highlighted. Public engagement will not be sufficient however as infection events will continue to occur, therefore countries facing even greater short and long term shortages of N95s could potentially consider the policy findings here for implementation (215). As with all modelling studies, many assumptions were made. Firstly, the SEIR model did not account for any heterogeneities in the infection or symptomatic rates as a result of factors such as the age structure. We also did not consider the effects of ongoing control methods, primarily social distancing, on the number of infections through time. In addition, we did not consider the infection risk to patients from HCWs who have acquired infection from other infected patients. This is due to Singapore’s outbreak in the community kept being relatively small, making estimations of the control’s effects very challenging. Secondly, parameters such as the ratio of confirmed and suspected cases remain largely unknown. Thirdly, the number of N95s used assumes all HCWs adhere to the guidelines, not counting for accidents or personal preferences, which may inflate the requirements. It is furthermore unknown as to whether the reuse will cause an increased risk of infection among HCWs due to mask degradation. Fourthly, hospital procedures may change where fewer workers stay longer in N95s within the respiratory isolation wards so as to avert N95s use. In addition, the proportions allocated to each level here may change according to specifications set out by policymakers should resources become more limited.

Lastly, the shift schedules of HCWs in NUH in isolation and ICU wards were provided by experts rather than collected from nurses, doctors and other HCWs via survey form directly. This is due to the access restriction in the early outbreak in hospital settings. Determined by the epidemic size, an extended-use policy should be considered where short-term supply chains are strained but planning measures are in place to ensure long-term availability. With severe shortage expectations from a severe epidemic, prolonged-use can be proposed as a necessary policy to significantly prolong supply. Should infection become widespread with significant patient load at healthcare facilities, extensive reuse, via the extended-use and prolonged-use policy, may have to be utilised to prevent supply shortages.

Motivation for Next Chapter

This research work implemented SEIR model to provide evidence to decisionmakers healthcare settings. Research has shown that the demand for N95s needs under different scenarios and uses policies, which help assist healthcare resource distribution. However, the approach and methodology proposed for estimating the epidemic size in this chapter cannot account for the heterogeneous infection types. Also, the next step for healthcare managers to consider is the hospitalisation and ICU needs. The next chapter, i.e., Chapter 3, showed the active ICU needs from the cases of different infection types.

CHAPTER 3. ESTIMATING THE NUMBER OF COVID-19 CASES BY INFECTION ORIGIN IN

SINGAPORE

Abstract

Background: Heterogeneity in transmission rates has been observed among different groups in the wider population, which are important to assess when predicting the number of infections and the impact on severe disease among them. Methods: We used case data from 19 January 2020 to 31 December 2020 to model past and current infections in a Bayesian framework for three distinct infection groups within Singapore: the general community, foreign workers and those imported. Specifically, mobility data was utilised for the fitting of general community cases across time and an SEIR model for dormitories with the fitting of staged efficacies for the lockdown intervention. We also estimated the number of ICU cases that evolved from each infection group. Model performance was assessed through one-week ahead projections in a rolling window. Results: Our models performed well throughout the period of estimation. For foreign worker cases, we project an average of 1 daily case (range: 0–6) in the next 90 days from 31 December 2020. For the general community, 2 are expected (range: 0–16), and for imported cases 8 (range: 2 –14).

Conclusions: Case projections for the ongoing planning of interventions require frameworks that utilise tailored methods for each subgroup population. Word counts = 198

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has been marked by heterogeneous impacts on different population groups, dividing society through factors such as race, age, and social capital (216,217). In western countries, the hardest-hit groups are racial and ethnic minority groups, low-income groups, or among those where working from home is not practical such as migrant communities, HCWs, prisons, and nursing homes (136,139,179,185,218,219). In lower-income countries, high-density urban areas and slums have been especially hard hit as maintaining social distancing is challenging (220,221). Thus attack rates of 48.7% (95% CI: 22.1%–76.8%) to 76% (95% CI: 66.6%–97.9%) have been estimated in cities in India (222) and Brazil (223) in September 2020 and October 2020, respectively. Furthermore, despite sharing similarities in income levels and climatic geographies, countries have had remarkably different outbreak patterns among groups as has been observed for Thailand and Vietnam when compared to Indonesia and the Philippines (224) in 2020. Issues causing such disparities can include misdiagnosis, access to care, low awareness of hygiene and housing density which can make it challenging to estimate cases and apply interventions effectively (225,226). Singapore is no exception in terms of heterogeneity. Since its first case in January 2020, Singapore has collated details of cases throughout its outbreak,

which are publicised in the national media and government websites (227), and further broken down into general community, foreign worker dormitory and imported cases. Case data highlights the different phases of the pandemic with an early phase marked by low importation levels from China and limited local spread (Jan–Feb 2020), followed by more significant numbers of imported cases from Europe and the Americas with substantial local spread (Mar 2020). With the implementation of lockdown measures (Apr–Jun 2020 (228,229)) which coincided with a massive spread in migrant workers (MWs) (Apr–Aug 2020), a phase of high levels of imported cases with limited local spread followed (Aug 2020 to end 2020). Overall for 2020, the proportion of cases in Singapore has been highly skewed towards MWs (93% of the total) and travellers (3%), with relatively few in the general resident population (4%) (230). The MWs were generally young (median age: 44 years old (231)) and healthy. Hence, very few of the infected population required care in ICU (232,233). Consequently, policy responses have differed between these three groups. To control spread within the general community, a multi Ministry team set up regional screening centres, contact tracing protocols, enforced crowd management, mandatory mask-wearing and isolation procedures for test positives (234,235). For foreign-worker dormitories, the inter-mixing of workers was limited across different blocks or sites, and movement tracking was performed with regular 14-day swabbing of uninfected workers (236). Imported case identification has been largely addressed through border policy control which began with travel restrictions from Hubei, China to multiple countries globally. Those who were allowed to enter the country initially quarantined at home and then moved to quarantine in approved hotels with

testing. Throughout 2020, many policies have been introduced due to concerns of healthcare capacity, especially ICU needs, and increasing case numbers which are unlinked within disease clusters, with the overall aim of introducing economic and social normalcy to the national population. This has and continues to require informed projections of case counts separated by the origin of infection for effective planning of interventions moving forward. Here, we therefore describe how we have projected epidemic trends within the general community, foreign worker cases, imported cases, and each of their ICU needs, over the course of the pandemic in 2020 to create a more tailored output for use by policymakers as part of the wider COVID-19 surveillance system effort. We demonstrate the importance of estimating cases across heterogeneous origin when tallying national totals and provide real-time projections across these settings.

Materials and Methods

Data Sources

Daily reported case data published by Singapore’s Ministry of Health were utilised to derive case projections. We extracted data for the period 19 January 2020, the date of Singapore’s first case, to 31 December 2020. The dataset distinguishes cases by their origin—general community, imported, and foreign worker—because of notable differences in observed infection rates (236) and different public health measures needed to control infection in these three groups. All cases in Singapore were isolated in healthcare institutions until recovery, some were admitted to ICU. The number of cases in the ICU was also

estimated by accounting for transferring from heterogeneous groups from different origins—general community, imported, and foreign workers. Mobility data have been found to be significantly correlated with COVID-19 case counts (237,238). We, therefore, obtained this dataset from the COVID-19 Community Mobility Reports (CMR), published by Google (239), for the equivalent time period. Baseline pre-outbreak mobility, defined by the CMR as averaged mobility from 3 January to 15 February 2020, was used as the reference value. From January 19 to February 15, when CMR data were not available, the reference value was assumed. Thereafter, the data describe the amount of time spent, relative to the pre-COVID-19 baseline, in six location types: residential; transit stations; retail and recreation; grocery stores and pharmacies; parks; and workplaces. For the residential category, the change in duration was presented; for other categories, the change in the total number of visits was available. The CMR data were used to model and project general community cases, for which social distancing measures were a dominant strategy for epidemic mitigation in Singapore. In contrast, mandatory quarantining of affected foreign worker dormitories led to their movement was heavily restricted upon lockdown, which was not reflected in the CMR data. The number of imported cases were assumed not to be affected by time spent in these six location types.

Results

A total of 58 599 cases were reported in 2020. Peak numbers of imported, general-community and foreign worker cases occurred in March 2020, March 2020, and April 2020, respectively (Figure 11A and B). Over this time horizon, 93% of cases were among the foreign worker community. On 7 April 2020, in response to the surge of cases in all three groups, Singapore started an 8-week lockdown, following which cases in the general community fell, from 38 general community cases on 7 April to four on 2 June 2020. Due to strict border control, there were almost no imported cases during this time. In the initial period of confinement, the daily number of foreign worker cases rose rapidly, reaching 1401 cases on April 20, before dropping to 540 cases on June 2, at which point the rest of Singapore emerged from lockdown, though foreign worker dormitories remained isolated as outbreak areas until July–August, when they were finally declared ‘clear’ of the virus. From June 2, a staged process of relaxation of control measures was introduced, and from then to 31 December 2020, spread in the general community was effectively controlled by

the intervention policies, with an average of 3 cases per day (range: 0–24; Fig Figure 11A). From mid-July, when the last foreign worker dormitory was ‘cleared’ of SARS-CoV-2, the number of cases has remained low (on average 10 per day, range 0–98, Figure 11B).

Figure 11. Description of COVID-19 cases in Singapore as of November 13. A) imported and general community cases; B) foreign workers cases. Note the difference in scales; C) the overall trend of google mobility changes from 15 February 2020 to 31 December 2020.

As in many settings, the lockdown policy in Singapore greatly impacted

residents’ lifestyle, with more time spent at home, and less time elsewhere, which when combined with other policies such as the test, isolate, trace and quarantine programme, was able to contain the infection. We show the overall

trends of Google mobility changes (239) from 15 February 2020 to 31 December 2020 in Figure 11C. Changes to time spent in residential locations showed an n-shaped curve that peaked at +55% on 1 May 2020 in the midst of lockdown. Time spent in public transport, retail and recreation, parks, and workplaces fell from the start of the COVID-19 outbreak, with a u-shaped response that reached a trough in April–May 2020. A rush to stockpile before border closure with Malaysia and general lockdown was evident in visible spikes in grocery and pharmacy store visits on 17 March and 6 April 2020, respectively. Model performance was assessed through specific forecast windows for all types of infection and active ICU cases (Figure 12). Projections were affected by the initial scarcity of information in data in the early period of the pandemic or updating policies, and the resulting uncertainty in the parameters. For instance, algorithms overestimated the number of foreign worker cases and the number of people in ICUs and underestimated general community cases from the information before lockdown. From mid-April, predictions of general community cases began to forecast with reduced error at an average projection error from –22.0 to 7.0 cases per day (Figure 13A). The forecast of infection among foreign workers was accurately declining in general due to intervention policies in place. However,

the projection error of foreign worker cases increased before lockdown and after the clearing of dormitories with mass testing due to rapid and substantial increases in testing capacity (Figure 12C and Figure 13C). Discussion

Groups with different social characteristics have shown significant

heterogeneity in the spread of SARS-Cov-2 in Singapore (244). The rapid response of intervention policies within the community succeeded in limiting transmission rates to low levels (245), preventing a widespread and national outbreak. With herd immunity unlikely to occur at high level of case suppression, the use of traditional SEIR models may be inappropriate to represent this disease state (246). Our model instead used community mobility data, representing the movement of residents in the general community, as a proxy of the magnitude of disease spread (237). It showed good performance in the fitting and projections for community cases, which has also been observed elsewhere (33). Foreign workers, in contrast, experienced an epidemic in comparison (136) due to high-density housing conditions, insufficient sanitation and the daily mixing of transient and regular contacts from other dormitories at work sites before COVID-19 pandemic. Starting from April 2020, the transmission rate within dormitories was observed to be relatively high in Singapore with herd immunity developing across multiple dormitory sites. SEIR models have been widely used in high-density environmental settings such as prisons and nursing homes as they are relatively small populations with high contact rates, as observed in a US prison where a COVID-19 outbreak had an estimated basic reproduction number of 8.44 (95% CI: 5.00–13.13) in the early part of the outbreak (247). The dormitory model within this study utilised testing rates (248) when available to improve predictions, substantially improving fitting, which has

wider applicability for other SEIR models operating within semi-closed environments with nonpharmaceutical intervention policies in place. For border importation estimation, shifting control policies, changing travellers flows and the occurrence of overseas epidemics can substantially complicate projection modelling. Studies (249,250) have estimated imported cases by prevalence in source countries and flight volumes, providing short term predictions. However, such models require large datasets and face complications such as cross-country prevalence differences due to varying national public health practices, data collection issues and political containment decisions. We developed a relatively simple model to project imported cases using historical daily imported case data and border control policies over time, assuming for Singapore that border control measures would remain constant for the foreseeable future. In estimating ICU utilisation of COVID-19, rates may vary by age group and physical condition (232,233). In Singapore, as foreign worker cases have comprised 93% of all cases, they are generally young adult males in good physical condition (251,252). Thus, the need for ICU has not been regarded as a major issue. One study forecasted ICU utilisation, accounting for characteristics of the subgroup population through patient-specific data, including the length of stay and demographics (253). It however required realtime patient-level data to ensure forecast accuracy. Other studies (254) forecast ICU utilisation by compartmental models. The significant limitation of these models is that they assumed a homogeneous proportion from infected individuals to ICU, which however not applicable in reality. Our model

projected ICU utilisation based on the number of cases reported daily by the heterogeneous severity caused by the type of infection. Several limitations, however, exist in our estimations. Firstly, our model should not be utilised as an early outbreak detection warning system as the introduction of new variants such as B.1.617 variant (255,256) can cause substantially different outbreak dynamics to occur. Secondly, we assumed that mobility was directly related to the general community cases and the primary driver of the fluctuations where policy factors and adherence to control measures may factor in this relationship. Thirdly, changes in border control policies can also cause projections to differ from observed case numbers. Thus, our projection model requires knowledge of upcoming policy changes to reflect transmission spread. However, it is able to provide evidence for policymakers to observe the longterm effects of ongoing policies. Fourthly, we do not account for any interactions between the different infection groups, which are likely to exist. This may be minimised within dormitories under strict isolation measures, and for border control which has strict testing and quarantining measures in place.

Lastly, the asymptomatic status or age structure of individuals was not

accounted for as identifying the former is challenging, and age-structured mobility data is required for the latter for community projections. Despite these limitations, our methodological framework and findings can be used for policymakers in countries with heterogeneous transmission communities, such as the notable outbreaks being observed in large homeless populations (182), nursing homes (178,179,257), MWs (136,181), prisons (185–187,258), slums (220), and the arrivals at borders, to better understand containment policies through projections. We also recommend that countries

can record case registration by source of infection to better understand transmission.

Motivation for Next Chapter

This chapter presented methods for projecting epidemic trends in the general community, foreign worker dormitory and importation groups, respectively, as well as the projection of active ICU cases, during the period from the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic to the widespread implementation of the vaccination programme. This suggests that recording cases by source type not only benefits academic research, but also that a more comprehensive COVID-19 surveillance system is more effective in helping policymakers create targeted outputs due to subgroup heterogeneity. In Singapore, most cases in the early COVID-19 pandemic were from foreign worker dormitories which are high-risk closed settings. Understanding the transmission within dormitories is critical when reopening during the post-pandemic period. In chapter 4, a Bayesian hierarchical model was implemented to characterise the nature of the contact network of foreign workers in the post-COVID-19 restriction period. In addition, a realistic branching process model was developed using empirical contact networks to explore the impact of policies that used varying degrees of stringency in quarantine to mitigate spread. The results are essential for mitigating emerging infectious disease outbreaks in high-risk closed settings, such as COVID-19.

QUARANTINE POLICIES IN HIGH RISK CLOSED

POPULATIONS

Abstract

Containment measures in high-risk closed settings, like migrant worker

dormitories, are critical for mitigating emerging infectious disease outbreaks and protecting potentially vulnerable populations in outbreaks such as COVID19. The direct impact of social distancing measures can be assessed through wearable contact tracing devices. Here we developed an individual-based model using data collected through a Bluetooth wearable device that collected 33.6 million and 52.8 million contact events in two dormitories in Singapore, one apartment-style and the other a barrack-style, to assess the impact of measures to reduce social contact of cases and their contacts. The simulation of highly detailed contact networks accounts for different infrastructural levels, including room, floor, block and dormitory, and intensity in terms of being regular or transient. Via a branching process model, we then simulated outbreaks that matched the prevalence during the COVID-19 outbreak in the two dormitories and explored alternative scenarios for control. We found that strict isolation of all cases and quarantine of all contacts would lead to extremely low prevalence but that quarantining only regular contacts would lead to only marginally higher prevalence, but substantially fewer total man-hours lost in quarantine. Reducing the density of contacts by 30% through the construction of additional dormitories was modelled to reduce the prevalence by 14% and 9% under smaller and larger outbreaks, respectively. Wearable contact tracing devices

may be used not just for contact tracing efforts but also to inform alternative containment measures in high-risk closed settings. Word counts = 238

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has been the first in which digital contact tracing (DCT) tools have been widely deployed as part of several national responses, including in Australia (259,260), China (261), several states of the US (262) and multiple European countries including France (263), Germany (264), Ireland (265), Italy (266), the Netherlands (267), Switzerland (268) and the United Kingdom (269). The ability to rapidly contact trace can assist in timely case identification, allowing for better case management and disease spread control by notifying individuals that they may be infected at an earlier stage (270). The magnitude of outbreak peaks can also be reduced if, following such alerts, infected individuals modify their behaviour to reduce social interactions or to isolate themselves either before they become infectious or at an earlier stage of their infectious period. Early detection is especially important in closed or semi-closed populations—such as residential care homes, boarding schools, military camps, prisons and dormitories—as the dense social network structure in such settings lends itself to explosive outbreaks, as has been witnessed both during COVID-19 (271,272) and before (273,274).

Contact networks in such settings are characterised by a high degree of

connectivity between individuals within the subpopulation and fewer connections beyond the subpopulation than members of the general population have.

Singapore was the first country to roll out DCT tools with the launch of TraceTogether in March 2020, days after the WHO declared COVID-19 to be a pandemic (275). TraceTogether came in the form of a physical token and smartphone app, evolving through multiple versions to monitor access to key areas, proximity to others and testing status of individuals. At the beginning of the pandemic however, an initial form of TraceTogether under the name of BluePass device (BP), comprising of a physical token only, was rapidly deployed to MWs who work in heavy industries and construction as they typically live in high density dormitories. These dormitories observed large outbreaks during Singapore’s lockdown in the early days of the pandemic— during which 56% of the residents had documented infections (242,276). These BP were to be carried when inside and outside their dormitories (277), were designed to be sufficiently durable to withstand use at worksites, and have a unique identifier which allowed for interaction events between devices to be tracked for the quarantining of close contacts of cases. After dormitory residents emerged from lockdowns in July and August 2020, they were subject to longlasting restrictions on their movement, only being allowed to communicate with others that stayed in the same dormitory or worked in the same location to prevent further outbreaks. In this study we pursue two aims, taking advantage of the high volume of social interaction data recorded in an extraction of around 1000 residents of two dormitories working for the same company. The first was to quantify the nature of the contact network itself, to gain insights on local heterogeneities that would possibly hinder control. The second was to use the empirical contact network to

develop a realistic branching process model to explore the impact of policies that used varying stringency of quarantine to mitigate spread.

Materials and Methods

Migrant workers and their dormitory. There are two types of MWs in Singapore; 1) those working in construction, marine shipyard, and processing industries (CMP) who are assigned to 43 licensed purpose-build dormitories and 2) those migrant domestic helpers residing predominantly in employer’s homes (278). In this study, we focus on the CMP MWs staying in dormitories, thus, we refer to MWs as CMP MWs. Licensed purpose-build dormitories do not have individual bedrooms but instead share. In our study site, each bedroom typically has a capacity of 16 to 24 residents and come in two designs. The first is a traditional apartment style, which provides facilities such as showers, toilets, and kitchens within each room unit. There are 8 room units per floor 5 floor levels in each dormitory block, and 6 blocks in total. The second is a denser barracks-style, where multiple units in the same floor share a communal shower room, toilet, and kitchen, and there are 12 room units located on the same floor. Each dormitory block has 6 floors, and 3 blocks in total. From the early outbreak of COVID-19 to re-opening orders implemented in 2022, migrant worker dormitories have implemented long-lasting restrictions on MWs movement. These include all MWs being only allowed to stay in their home-dormitories and workplaces, and MWs only having contact with those in the same dormitory and at work sites. To understand the contact pattern among MWs during the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic period, we therefore selected

MWs who were working for the same company but stayed in two dormitories

to observe the impacts of dormitory and worksite contact. Although the two dormitories are 7km apart, it is expected that MWs between dormitories will have contact with each other at worksites. Data collection in selected dormitories. Starting October 2020, all MWs in Singapore were to carry BP for contact tracing purposes (277). Each BP has a unique registration ID and MWs are required to carry their own BP at all times to track interactions (277). In February 2021, BPs were updated with improvements in design. As to the time to collect contact information, MWs were only allowed to communicate with others in the same dormitory or at workplaces, and all MWs assigned to the same dormitory should work for the same company. In collaboration with Keppel Corporation, we conducted fieldwork in two MW dormitories, Acacia Lodge (Dorm A) and Cassia@Penjuru (Dorm C), to obtain records of interactions from BP devices. In the data collection procedure, I was responsible for poster design to recruit MWs, coordinated the collection of contact trackers, collected the contact records via BP on site, and extracted contact records from the designated server. I also helped to set up the necessary field equipment before we went to the dormitory to collect the contact records. We recruited 1611 MWs using convenience sampling, which is a nonprobability sampling method to obtain sample from a group of people who are easily to contact or to reach, with approximately equal numbers in each dormitory, and after obtaining verbal informed consent, extracted their records of interactions for the period of February to April 2021. Study participants also filled in a brief questionnaire, which we used to collect some demographic

Because of the ongoing restrictions at the time of the study, MWs interacted relatively constrainedly, with workmates and roommates typically having longer and more frequent interactions, with interactions between individuals living on different floors or in different blocks being more random and of shorter duration. The simulated dormitory outbreak ran for four months (the final attack rates were similar when extended to 6 months, Figure 24b). Using an infection rate of 0.40 to represent a smaller outbreak, and 0.87 for a larger outbreak, we then investigated the effects of intervention strategies on epidemic size at 120 days under multiple NPIs: baseline where only positive cases are isolated, quarantining of regular contacts, quarantining of all contacts, and no isolation and quarantining at all. All were run with and without delay in case identification. With the case identification delay we found that if the infection rate of 0.40, the proportion of infected was 39% (95% CI: 30%–45%) when only isolating cases with a moderate delay (i.e., 3 days) (Figure 25a). When all contacts were quarantined with a moderate delay, the proportion of infected was 2.1% (1.7%–2.5%) (Figure 25b). When only regular contacts were quarantined with a moderate delay, a significant decrement in the infected proportion was also observed at 11% (8%–13%) (Figure 25c). If the infection

rate of 0.87, the proportion of infected was 70% (95% CI: 66%–71%) when only isolating cases with a moderate delay (Figure 25d). When all contacts were quarantined with moderate delay, the proportion of infected was 9% (8%–11%) (Figure 25e). When only regular contacts were quarantined with a moderate delay, a significant decrement in the infected proportion was also observed at 46% (42%–48%) (Figure 25f). For the baseline scenario with a less infectious variant, 31% (95% CI: 28%– 41%) of the total resident population across both dormitories would be infected by day 120 (Figure 26a), while losing 22,064 man-days accordingly. When all contacts were immediately quarantined, the infected proportion decreased substantially to 1.1% (1%–1.3%) (Figure 26b) as 66,667 man-days would be lost accordingly. When only regular contacts were immediately quarantined, the proportion was also low at 6% (5%–8%) with 35,504 man-days lost (Figure 26c). Without any isolation or quarantining, the proportion is at its highest at 41% (31%–47%) (Figure 26d). In a lager outbreak, 67% (62%–68%) of the whole resident population were infected under the baseline scenario with 38,892 man-days lost (Figure 26f), decreasing to 33% (28%–35%) when only regular contacts were quarantined while 87,500 man-days lost accordingly (Figure 26h), and to 3.8% (3.3%–4.4%) when all contacts were quarantined with 112,500 man-days lost (Figure 26g). In contrast, the proportion getting infected was high at 72% (71%–73%) when no quarantining or isolation was implemented at all (Figure 26i). To further investigate the impact of resident density, we reduced the number of social contacts of each resident by moving 30% of residents to a hypothetical new dormitory. Discussion

DCT data can be coupled with infectious disease models to simulate the spread of disease. In this study, we utilised a branching process model and simulated contact network derived from empirical social interaction data to model an epidemic outbreak in a closed population. The heterogeneous mixing at different infrastructural levels was captured based on the data collected from portable Bluetooth devices, revealing group-based contact patterns and the relations within the dormitory hierarchy in which transmission risk may be elevated. The effectiveness of various NPIs designed to reduce the risk of transmission through blocking of certain interactions was investigated using our model. Through understanding viral spread in closed high-density settings, more informed planning for future outbreaks can be carried out. We found that the severity of the outbreak between the two study dormitories is significantly different. The infection rate was lower in Dorm A than in the Dorm C (135). It could be plausible that Dorm A, which is laid out more in the style of sparser populated apartment blocks, had less environmental transmission when compared to the barrack style of Dorm C. To show this, we assumed that contacts would be reduced by building new dormitories for MWs, and we simulated the epidemic size with a reduced overall number of contacts by 30%, decreasing infection rates from 31% and 67% to 19% and 57% for a more and less infectious pathogen, respectively. This indicates that reducing

resident density, potentially through the construction of new dormitories could substantially lower the risk of rapid infectious disease spread when new outbreaks emerge. In the face of rising cases, lockdowns are critical tools which can reduce the spread of a respiratory virus in closed settings. However, costs can be exceedingly high when the virus is highly transmissible (282) due to the containment measures required to ensure compliance, often bringing additional mental health burdens to the residents (283–285) and, despite ongoing efforts at containment in China, these measures do not seem sustainable in other settings. Our simulation results indicate that the quarantining of all or regular contacts can substantially reduce outbreak size, but such methods should be used at the beginning of new outbreaks or when the risk of the illness developing severe complications warrants such constrained measures for the residents, such as the implementation of lockdowns in Wuhan city (27,286) and other cities and territories/countries early in the COVID-19 pandemic (287). Notably, there is a difference between the baseline of only isolating cases and no control at all at. 10%, and 5 % reduction in the infection proportion if only isolating cases compared to no control actions when the infection rate of 0.4 and 0.87, respectively. At a latter part of an outbreak, the isolation of cases may therefore bring relatively few benefits to the population as a whole as the majority have already been infected. Nevertheless, mass testing is strongly recommended and individuals should be given a choice to self-isolate if they are positive or wish to as they have been suspected of being exposed (288). The simulations revealed that a policy of quarantining of regular contacts only would be relatively efficient, in reducing infections with relatively little impact

on the number of people being quarantined. In contrast, quarantining all contacts would be necessary to prevent epidemic spread, but due to the large number of casual contacts in a high density living environment such as those considered in this paper, such a policy would lead to large numbers of people in quarantine, and without long term means of preventing spread, such quarantine would need to be implemented over too long a time period to be feasible. Overall, the use of Bluetooth technology via wearable devices such as the BP is useful when modelling epidemic sizes under different quarantine orders, which is critical information for policymakers in their planning efforts for closed settings, especially ones with low vaccination rates. Having stand-alone

Bluetooth devices can overcome the disadvantages of common digital

communication technologies (289) as such devices do not require any modification to an individual’s mobile cellular device to incorporate contact tracing functioning, which reduces the perceived level of intrusion and may encourage participation. Moreover, all BPs are secure, with the devices deidentified, and social interaction records encrypted before being stored locally on each device, so that data retrieval is only possible by accessing the memory storage of the device. In our study, this was obtained by physical extraction, but this was a labour-intensive endeavour that could not be sustained in routine use. The study has its limitations. We had the assumption that the likelihood of transmission of SARS-CoV-2 was proportional between the two contact categories with transmission to transient contacts occurring at 0.009 times that to regular contacts. This was determined through the proportion of regular and transient mean interaction duration from the DCT data obtained from the BPs.

While we know that the risk of transmission decreases with shorter verbal conversations (290), this could be an underestimation of transmission likelihood if individuals are being infected through surfaces and fomites. In addition, the contact networks we observed were for a period in which residents were able to leave their dormitory for work and in some other limited circumstances for other purposes, and the structure of the network may have been different at earlier and later periods in the pandemic. Also, we assumed that MWs had the same probability of being the initial case in our simulation model. Factors such as heterogeneous mixing and contact behaviour for MWs could not be incorporated for initial cases as this information is largely unknown.

Despite these limitations, using DCT devices was able to investigate

heterogeneities in contact within the networks of a closed environment, which has implications on how epidemics spread. The impact of various policies on SARS-CoV-2 transmission can be explored, feeding into policymaking decisions on how to prevent future outbreaks of respiratory illnesses in high risk closed environments where epidemics can rapidly take hold and spread.

Summary of major findings

Rapidly climbing COVID-19 cases in the early outbreak posed a severe challenge to healthcare systems, resulting in a shortage of healthcare resources, including PPE (291,292), ICU beds, and inpatient beds. That not only leaves frontline HCWs at grave risk but also challenges policymakers and society as a whole. Hence, a comprehensive approach is necessary for timely estimating PPE demands. Chapter 2 of this thesis estimated the demand for N95s, the critical type of PPEs, to aid planning efforts assessing what preparations are required over the coming months for the healthcare system to run with the maximised possible protection for its workers. This study estimates the epidemic size throughout mild, moderate, and severe epidemic scenarios within Singapore as a case study, using a transmission dynamic model. In addition, we assess the number of N95s required within the respiratory isolation wards and ICUs throughout the epidemic, considering multiple strategies, including single-use, extended-use, and prolonged-use, for N95 respirators for use by HCWs treating suspected but negative (misclassified) or confirmed COVID-19 patients. Also, the study calculates the demands of N95s under different usage strategies over the course of the epidemic if there is rapid testing is implemented. Our approach can be utilised elsewhere with considerations of implementing N95s extended-use policies. This was especially paramount in the early outbreak of COVID-19 as the WHO had stated that global supplies were rapidly depleting overall. This is further complicated by the fact that the PPE supply chain landscape is complex due to the quarantine and lockdown policies in developing countries, such as China, which are the primary producers of PPE.

While timely, stringent and effective intervention policies, as implemented in many countries in early 2020, have been demonstrated to have mitigated deteriorating COVID-19 outbreaks, they are not viable long-term strategies (173) as it is impractical to implement strict lockdowns or border control policies for the months or years needed for mass vaccination to occur (174). COVID-19 infection has setting-specific heterogeneity due to differences in living and/or health conditions (175,176), such as the significant differences between normal communities and nursing homes (179,271,272), MWs (136,180,181), homeless (182,183) and travellers. In addition, outbreaks in army (184) and prison (185) settings have been reported. Hence, it is essential to update responsive interventions in a timely manner according to the type of infection by real-time forecasting of COVID-19. Chapter 3 of this thesis used a Bayesian hierarchical statistical model to estimate epidemic trends within the community, foreign worker infections and imported cases separately, thus providing real-time projections for SARS-CoV-2 cases in Singapore in heterogeneous transmission settings. Such forecasting can inform the need to escalate or reduce the degree of interventions, which has proven to be one of the approaches taken by the Singapore government to avoid rapid resurgence following the lockdowns. Before the vaccination programme was implemented (i.e., by the end of 2020), Singapore's remarkably heterogeneous disease states existed: a low endemic community state and a high dormitory outbreak state (over 93% of cases belonged to the latter). Consequently, policy responses differ between sites. In dormitory settings, operators are expected to limit the inter-mixing of workers from different blocks or sites, perform movement tracking, and perform regular

14-day swabbing of uninfected workers (236). Hence, understanding the spread between dormitories to improve the intervention strategies to minimise the risk of new disease clusters emerging is essential. Chapter 4 of this thesis took advantage of the high volume of social interaction data recorded in an extraction of around 1,000 residents of two dormitories working for the same company, to quantify the nature of the contact network to gain insights into local heterogeneities that would possibly hinder control. In addition, the study used the empirical contact network to develop a realistic branching process model to explore the impact of policies that used varying stringency of quarantine to mitigate spread. Wearable contact tracing devices may be used not just for contact tracing efforts but also to inform alternative containment measures in high-risk closed settings. The simulation results under multiple policies indicated that strict isolation of all cases and quarantine of all contacts would lead to very low prevalence but that quarantining only regular contacts would lead to only marginally higher prevalence, but substantially fewer total manhours lost in quarantine. Moreover, the construction of additional dormitories (to reduce the density of contacts by 30%) can reduce the prevalence by 14% and 9% under smaller and larger outbreaks, respectively.

Public health contributions and implications

There are several public health contributions and implications of this thesis. Firstly, this thesis provides researchers and healthcare policymakers with different degrees of information to adjust intervention policies across different stages of the pandemic—before widespread community transmission had occurred, while multiple epidemics were concurrently ongoing, and in the

period once transmission levels had fallen—and thus make well preparation and knowledge for next infectious disease outbreak. Secondly, the thesis contributes to an estimated demand for N95s in the early outbreak of infectious disease. Considering the different severe degrees of the epidemic with multiple use policy, should infection become widespread with significant patient load at healthcare facilities, extensive reuse, via the extended-use and prolonged-use policy, may have to be utilised to prevent supply shortages. By doing so, the findings provide information for use in planning purposes which can be utilised not only in the COVID-19 pandemic in Singapore but other outbreaks globally also. Thirdly, this thesis proposes a methodological framework to project the number of infections by the origin of infection and the impact on server cases among them. This finding can be used by policymakers in countries with heterogeneous transmission communities, such as the notable outbreaks observed in countries with large homeless populations, nursing homes, MWs, prisons, slums, and arrivals at borders, to better understand containment policies through projections. Finally, the findings of this thesis support that wearable contact tracing devices may be used not just for contact tracing efforts but also to investigate heterogeneities in contact within the networks of a closed environment, which has profound implications on how epidemics spread. Meanwhile, this provides new insights into the contact network in a specific community and can help policymakers choose appropriate containment measures under various restrictive policies by building simulation models on the network.

Limitations

Limitations were discussed in each project. However, the following limitations are worth emphasising again. Reinfection was not modelled, instead, lasting immunity was considered. Accounting for reinfection adds excellent value, however, a level of complexity requires a different methodological framework, i.e., an additional comparator to reflect the reinfection group at the population level and factors to become reinfection cases at the individual level will cause computationally burden. However, reinfection was rarely observed in the early outbreak of the COVID19 pandemic, it also depends on the mutations in SARS-CoV-2 variants. Additionally, this thesis provides a representative view of different COVID-19 waves, each with a mono-dominated variant in each phase. Again, the reinfection situation may be far more complex with a longer-term phase to evaluate. Chapters 2 and 3 of this thesis are based on data before the massive vaccine implementation programme. As a result, it only provides informative sights to policymakers from 2021, when vaccine implementation programming has started, rather than clear findings to them. Moreover, the models developed may need to characterise importation cases' impact on local transmission fully. But the concern is probably minor for this thesis because strict travelling restrictions and quarantine policies in each country were implemented before the vaccination programme. This thesis also assumes that infections likely are the same in a similar setting, especially in closed environments. However, the distance may result in different

infection probabilities. Such as, in chapter 4, based on our observations of dormitory layouts during fieldwork, Dorm A is laid out more in the style of apartment blocks, sparser and with more spacing between blocks than Dorm C, which is more like a traditional dormitory design, with blocks placed in close proximity to each other. It could plausibly be that infected workers in one block could expose those in a neighbouring block to infection at night through open windows for instance. There also seemed to be fewer social spaces in Dorm C, which may have led to greater crowding in those available.

Future studies

As per the limitations of this thesis, several future studies can be implemented to fill in the knowledge gaps. Future studies are needed to investigate the with a longer duration of follow up would anticipate more reinfections, thus raising the importance to account for such reinfection events within the model. Moreover, the data would also provide a way to estimate the risk of such reinfection events. Furthermore, infectivity between/within hospitalized and ICU groups by patients to HCWs to patients’ contacts can be considered in the model when estimating N95 needs to prepare for future infectious diseases. In addition, a comprehensive model accounting for multiple pandemic phases and mutation of variants is necessary in future work. Due to the ventilation properties, the distance between individual is worth considering in the context of the spread of airborne infectious diseases. The effects of such anecdotal evidence would be an interesting future research topic.

Finally, we used the simulated contact network within the whole dormitories (i.e., Dorm A and Dorm C) to calculate the infection risk under the mild and severe situations, separately. However, the heterogeneity in dormitory design was observed of these two dormitories and this might cause the infection risk bias. However, calculating the infection risk with contact records collecting from more dormitories can mitigate this bias.

Conclusion

This thesis provides a set of statistical modelling studies for discussing and better understanding the preparation, suitability, sustainability, and optimal implementation of control measures. The results obtained are essential for public health decision-makers in deploying limited resources and adjusting intervention policies with different phases of the campaigns.
