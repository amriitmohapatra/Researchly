Introduction

Dengue

Dengue remains a major global health concern, with an estimated 390 million annual infections throughout the tropics and subtropics [1]. The dengue viruses are primarily spread by the Ae. aegypti vector but may also be spread, albeit less efficiently, by female mosquitoes of other Aedes species such as Ae. albopictus. Four serotypes are in active circulation in swathes of Asia, Africa and Latin America, and although infection from a particular serotype confers lifelong immunity to that specific serotype, infection with a different serotype raises the risk of complications [2]. The broad implementation of vaccination with Dengvaxia (CYD-TDV), a recently developed vaccine, has been complicated by modest efficacy and the risk engendered by vaccinating immuno-negative individuals [3]. In particular, the low seropositivity rate in Singapore makes the application of population wide vaccination challenging. The other candidate in development, TAK-003, while showing promising results, is yet to be licensed, and has different rates on protection across the four dengue strains [4]. The only way to prevent dengue transmissions currently, therefore, is to reduce the population of the Aedes aegypti mosquito via vector control. There have been few outright successes in preventing dengue through vector control. In the years after the second world war, dengue ceased to be a public health problem in much of continental Latin America, as the vector was suppressed through a combination of denying vector breeding through oiling larval habitats and spraying of insecticides [5]. The majority of Cuba similarly became free of the vector following community-based combination interventions and indoor residual spraying, leading to an extended period of no outbreaks [6]. In Australia, a vector control effort in 2003 comprising of indoor residual spraying led to protective effects in nearby premises [7]. Finally, in Singapore from the 1970s to the present, using entomological surveillance and larval source reduction which led to 15 years of low dengue incidence [8] and has reduced the current true infection rate drastically, even though the reported number of cases has actually risen [9]. However, despite the past successes of vector control, there has been a resurgence of dengue in Latin America where the present co-circulation of all 4 serotypes in the region and lack of sustainable vector control programs in the region has led to an increasing number of individuals subject to frequent outbreaks [10]. While the vector premises index is drastically reduced over the years in Singapore, a stark increase in reported dengue incidence in Singapore has also been observed.

Dengue Trends in Singapore

Lowered Herd Immmunity, Increased Infectivity

Historically, reduced dengue transmission have resulted in a concomitant decrease in herd immunity to the dengue virus in Singapore [11, 12]. Despite the decrease in Aedes aegypti population due to efficacious vector control, the low level of population immunity, as evident from recent serological surveys conducted in 2009 and 2013 [13] provide an ideal condition for the transmission of dengue virus. In particular, the levels of seropositivity among younger adults is far lower at around 19% for both the 2009 and 2013 surveys, compared to around 80% in the above 50 age group. A recent study reconstructing the seroprevalence of dengue in Singapore across age groups over time from 1960 to 2017 also suggests that there is a stark decrease in seroprevalence especially among the under 25 age group as well as the force of infection of dengue due to successful vector control practices which has reduced mosquito breeding rates to extremely low levels [9]. Previous serosurveys have also shown that a majority of young adults had antibodies reactive to a single dengue serotype, but the majority of subjects aged 56–60 had antibodies reactive to 3 or more dengue serotypes [14, 13]. Overall, the seroprevalence of dengue has consistently decreased from 2004 to 2017, from 59% in 2004 [15], 50.8% in 2009 [13] and 42% in 2017 [9].

In comparison, recently conducted seroprevalence studies signal that 90% of 15–19 year olds urban dwelling Indonesians are seropositive [16] and over 80% of 12–15 year olds from a nongovernmental pediatric hospital in Siem Reap Province, Cambodia [17], compared to the less than 20% among the below 20 age group in Singapore. The low seroprevalence of dengue among young adults in Singapore is also in contrast to other endemic countries such as Vietnam, Sri Lanka and Mexico where the prevalence is more than doubled compared to Singapore [13]. This suggests the success of Singapore’s stringent vector control programme over the recent decades, which has resulted in a decrease in the force of infection of dengue but lowered immunity against the virus, especially in the younger than 30 years age group [9]. Several implication exist due to the lowered herd immunity in the Singapore population. It has rendered the population vulnerable to outbreaks despite a low Ae. aegypti population [18], but the reduction in dengue transmissions over the years yield many benefits, such as having fewer pediatric cases and thus fewer children suffering from severe secondary infection [18]. This is also important as the risk of severe complications in symptomatic dengue is higher in children than adults [19]. However, the lower herd immunity also diminishes gains from successful vector control practices, due to the greater chance of infected mosquitoes infecting one or more susceptible individuals. An increase in the median age of dengue cases is also attributable to increasingly many infections in the elderly [11, 18]. Even as clinical management and admission criteria for dengue continues to refine drastically over time [20], combined risks of increased dengue severity with those having comorbidities [21] and the inflecting demographic composition in Singapore [22] may pose drastic challenges to the clinical management of dengue in the near future.

Cyclical Virological Patterns Characterize Outbreaks from 2000 to 2020 The hyper-endemic nature of dengue in Singapore, where all four serotypes are in active circulation, are associated to large outbreaks the past two decades. On the population level, Rajarethinam et al. described the serotypic features where two epidemic cycles lasting from 2004 – 2007 (the first epidemic cycle since 2000) and 2013 – 2016 both have a pattern of switching in the predominant cosmopolitan serotype DENV-2 to DENV-1 in the 2 year epidemic period; DENV-2 then returns to being the predominant serotype when the epidemic cycle ends [23]. Genotype analysis however, did not provide evidence for adaptive evolution in any period within the epidemic phase for at least the first epidemic cycle [24]. This pattern has also been observed in other localities where dengue remains hyper-endemic, such as Indonesia [25] and Laos [26]. A previously infected individual subject to infection of a different serotype leads to potentially severe complications [2], with DENV-2 being the variant most associated with secondary infections and also has the greatest percentage of severe cases among secondary infections [27, 28, 29]. Collectively, clinical manifestations of dengue in outbreaks may have been more severe in Singapore, due to epidemic patterns in Singapore being characterized by a surge mainly in DENV-2 the past two decades. As these switches pre-empt looming epidemics and have large clinical implications, viral surveillance has also been incorporated as a key facet of Singapore’s surveillance policy [30]. As a major travel hub, importation of dengue cases remains a key concern. Previous work detailed dengue genotypes from reported cases which are related to those found in Saudi Arabia, and Côte d’Ivoire [31] and with recent numbers of imported cases fluctuating between 1% to 10% from 2000 to 2016. Around 75% of the imported cases are attributable to Southeast-Asian countries [23, 8]. There is a lower proportion of DENV-3 and DENV-4 cases detected in Singapore’s population, possibly due to less genetic compatibility with Singapore’s Ae. aegypti mosquitoes [23]. This differs from the rest of the region where DENV-3 was the dominant serotype in Thailand from 2013 to 2016 [32], DENV-4 was the dominant serotype in Vietnam in 2013 [33], and both DENV-3 and DENV-4 were major serotypes in Malaysia from 2012 to 2013 [34]. Sporadic emergence of DENV-3 strains in Singapore were found to be related to strains in neighbouring countries such as Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand and the Philippines, suggesting importations of DENV-3 [35]. Importation of DENV-3 strains or less common genotypes are a concern as lowered herd immunity to these strains may be the driving force of heightened dengue transmissions in the future [36]. The higher genotypic diversity of DENV in Singapore compared to other dengue endemic countries but less connected locales such as Puerto Rico, also further suggests the importation of different genetic variants due to increased globalization, which may then become established at various levels and develop into outbreaks [31]. The huge influx of travellers to and from nearby Southeast-Asian metropolises poises a large challenge to Singapore due to the co-circulation of dengue strains regionally [37, 38]. Therefore, surveillance of the dengue virus has been undertaken extensively in Singapore and in the region.

Surveillance

Several major efforts have refined vector control surveillance and implementation in Singapore over the past 15 years, within the National Environment Agency (NEA), the body responsible for vector control and surveillance in Singapore. Current practices incorporate an approach combining entomological, ecological and virological components. Namely, identification of favourable areas, climatic and geographic parameters for vector breeding, rapid diagnostic testing of suspected dengue cases and incorporation of case surveillance with secondary and primary care institutions in Singapore.

Cross-border Surveillance

Southeast Asia has increasing levels of connectivity and human movement which has contributed to elevated mortality and high economic losses from past pandemics such as influenza A (H1N1-2009) [39]. Singapore is a highly connected city-state with an annual influx of more than 182 million travelers and 61 million vehicles crossing the Johor-Singapore causeway [40]. 65.6 million passengers are also being served annually by Singapore’s international airport [41]. Local virus surveillance, which was based on a network of primary healthcare clinics enabled detection of several arbovirus outbreaks in Singapore. This was formally expanded into a cross-border surveillance system, when the UNITEDengue (UNited In Tackling Epidemic Dengue) network was set up together with Malaysia and Sumatra, Indonesia in 2012. It has since included 7 other entities, including the health ministries of Brunei, Laos, Myanmar, Phillipines, Sri Lanka, Thailand and Vietnam, providing a comprehensive sharing database incorporating case and virological data across countries [42, 43]. The surveillance system has yielded insights into factors which may affect outbreaks at the same period of time over geographically close localities [34] and is currently being used to monitor potential outbreaks in the region, which then informs NEA and other agencies of heightened risk of dengue case importation, along with other arboviruses.

Local Surveillance

Improvements in dengue diagnostics and the national notification system drastically increased the reporting rate of dengue infections in Singapore over the past two decades with the estimated ratio of notified cases to true infections decreasing from 1 to 14 in 2005–2009 to 1 to 6 in 2014—2017 [9]. The large data streams generated from surveillance in Singapore are also especially suited for analysis using modern machine learning tools, to generate inference on current and future plausible dengue dynamics. Applications undertaken locally include the generation of dengue risk maps [44], temporal [45], spatiotemporal trends [46] and forecasting. Inference on outbreaks and the patterns of outbreaks are also conducted [47]. Understanding dengue risk potentially enables identification of high risk areas in Singapore, allowing optimization of vector control resources, while inferring future periods or localities where dengue outbreaks may occur as well as their magnitudes advises NEA to ramp up vector control measures before an epidemic occurs within a specific locality or on a national scale.

Mitigating Vector Breeding Potential

Wolbachia

Wolbachia is a gram-negative bacteria that is commonly found to infect a large proportion of insects [48]. Certain strains were found to restrict the reproductive capacity of the Aedes aegypti mosquito as well as restrict the replication of dengue virus within the vector once introduced [49, 50]. In Australia, successful establishment of Wolbachia in Aedes aegypti populations through field releases demonstrate their practical nature as an intervention [51], and has been noted to prevent large epidemics despite constant importation of dengue from travellers into the country [52]. In Indonesia, a cluster randomized controlled trial further demonstrated the applicability of Wolbachia in a dengue endemic setting, by demonstrating an over 73% reduction in dengue transmission following releases [53, 54]. This intervention has also been found to work in a Singapore specific Aedes aegypti context [55]. NEA’s Project Wolbachia, started in 2016 as small scale field studies in localities which have potential for dengue outbreaks or suffered from them previously. Wolbachia infected male mosquitoes were released at public spaces and community engagement/dialogue was conducted at a regular basis to address concerns on mosquito releases within the vicinity. There were 3 subsequent expansions in geographical coverage of Project Wolbachia study sites in 2018 and 2019 due to repeated successses in addressing study objectives and suppressing mosquito populations [56]. The studies conducted in Project Wolbachia have found various favourable entomological factors such as vertical migration, biological and reproductive competitiveness as evidence of the utility of Wolbachia to succeed in a highly urban environment. Expansion of the Project Wolbachia study sites have also led to increasingly efficacious suppresive effects on Aedes aegypti reproduction. Results from multiple field studies have also allowed NEA to fine-tune Wolbachia implementation, such as decreasing release periods and combining Wolbachia release with community based interventions aimed at eliminating mosquito breeding habitats [56].

Community Measures

Destruction of vector breeding habitats lead to a reduction in vector breeding potential and thus transmission potential of dengue. Through the implementation of direct vector control measures, the vector breeding potential of dengue and force of infection of dengue in Singapore has reduced significantly [57]. The efficacy of community engagement for dengue breeding prevention is well-known [58] and has been implemented in Singapore as the primary approach taken for vector control in Singapore at the local level. The strategy is continually refined to date. First, educating the public on eliminating potential mosquito breeding sites [59], through the provision of education material, volunteers to provide community outreach, as well as community events and dengue prevention exhibits to promote dengue awareness amongst the community [60]. Social media is additionally supplemented together with traditional communication materials for outreach, especially in periods of emerging arbovirus outbreaks locally [61]. Community alert systems for dengue are also implemented through the display of different colour-coded banners to inform residents within a locality of the dengue situation [62]. This form of community engagement has been relatively successful in raising the public awareness regarding dengue [63]. Especially in areas with multiple recurrent dengue clusters, residents that were surveyed on their knowledge of dengue in a 2010 study were found to be more knowledgeable than areas without any reported dengue clusters [60]. However, adherence to mosquito breeding prevention measures at the household level in Singapore from community engagement is still not complete [64], with constant implementation of community engagement required to ensure onboarding and participation in dengue vector breeding prevention [65]. Lastly, Stop Work Orders (SWO) are implemented in construction sites which have a higher probability of becoming the epicentre of dengue transmission or a potential dengue outbreak. These areas may include migrant worker work locations where the population may have a larger percentage of sero-negative individuals compared to the general population – increasing their susceptibility to dengue [66]. Reduction of dengue exposure risk through SWO is also able to further reduce knockoff effects of dengue transmissions away from these areas due to the high movement rates at baseline [67].

Challenges

Despite significant reductions in the vector breeding potential of dengue through Singapore’s vector control measures, the breeding percentage, number of cases and severity of dengue outbreaks has seen an upward trend in the past two decades. Increasing urbanisation has led to the geographic expansion of Ae. aegypti into new territories [68], while low level of immunity due to successful vector control practices has paradoxically placed residents of both old and new territories at risk [18]. The changing demographic structure has shifted the average age of reported dengue infections towards the elderly population, which in turn has heightened the overall severity of dengue infections clinically [14]. Increasing globalisation and connectivity in South-east Asia heightened the likelihood of dengue importations [31], which as evinced from virological surveillance in Singapore, led to greater genetic diversity of the virus. This may potentially increase the overall fitness of the virus at the population level [69] and also seed future outbreaks. While most dengue cases are asymptomatic or clinically mild, the scale of dengue outbreaks continue to escalate in size over the years and continue to create massive healthcare and economic burdens [70]. Pharmaceutical therapeutics have been slow to develop, with few candidates in the pipeline for vaccination or treatment of the dengue virus.

The ramp up of existing surveillance strategies and vector control measures needs to be maintained in order to dampen the potential spread of dengue in Singapore and more broadly, dengue endemic regions of the world. The development of novel vector control strategies is also pertinent to prevent potential vector adaptation to current strategies in place – such as pesticide resistance. Yet, for control strategies to be employed strategically and efficiently due to inherent resource limitations, we must understand how dengue, and more broadly, vector-borne diseases behave.

Characterizing the transmission dynamics of

A key question is then, how we can characterize the transmission dynamics of vector-borne diseases, such as dengue. Mathematical and statistical modelling tools are helpful in this respect, as they can exploit the information collected over space and time from surveillance mechanism and inform policy makers on risk factors for vector-borne disease transmission, whether current interventions in place are effective and whether a ramp-up in vector control measures is needed. Three broad classes of modelling tools were broadly defined by Siettos and Russo [71]. The first includes regression models, which characterize the observation process of diseases and can crucially account for observation process time dependence, related covariates which may influence disease transmission as well as error assumptions. The next category includes mathematical-mechanistic state-space models, including the SIR model and network based models [72]. These can include stochastic assumptions on parameters and also crucially an observation equation for observed disease data, which then allows state-space models to be viewed as a special case of the regression model (due to state equation restrictions). The third category consists of machine learning-based models, such as neural networks [73]. Crucially, while mechanistic models are able to provide better interpretations of the biological and ecological processes [72] compared to regression and machine learning-based models, construction of these models are often subjective, requiring a user defined choice of the number of components, with an increasing number of components compounding issues in model calibration. While simple models can be effectively estimated from methods such as least-squares and simulation from time series data [74], complex models having multiple strains, vector-host dynamics suffer from statistical identifiability [75] and a large number of parameters – compounding the difficulty of effectively using these tools in a large number of settings, such as hyper-endemic dengue [74]. Infectious disease models can help to characterize outbreaks and enable us to understand why outbreaks occur, the reasons for an outbreak and how these outbreaks may be effectively mitigated [71]. In terms of vector-borne diseases, these tools effectively provide a means to perform computer experiments where it is not ethical or practical in the real world. This includes examining the impacts of varying vector control practices or how a change in population structure may affect vector-borne disease transmission. Dimensionless, but important epidemiological quantities such as the effective reproduction number, static reproduction number and force of infection can then be post-hoc inferred for these scenarios under certain classes of models which allows policy makers to understand the long-term implications of vector-borne disease burdens from risk factors. Forecasts of infectious disease can also be generated from a large suite of mathematical and statistical tools for resource planning and healthcare management purposes and have been well-explored in the literature. Some examples include the use of conventional time series models such as the autoregressive model with covariates to forecast dengue case counts [76], count process models for severe dengue case counts and/or deaths counts [77], forecast combinations of models incorporating both time-series and mechanistic tools [78] and machine learning frameworks which induce sparsity in the observation equation [46].

Key gaps in modelling vector-borne diseases

Conventional tools to model and forecast vector-borne disease usually do so via the stochastic process of observed data, such as vector-borne disease case counts or death counts. A key goal for public health practitioners is to characterize the drivers of these stochastic processes, such as through covariates in regression models [76] or to ascertain their potential trajectory in the future, through forecasting exercises [46]. While modelling the observation process is well-studied, key gaps still remain in modelling vector-borne diseases in general. First, characterizing when an infectious disease outbreak starts or ends may be a subjective exercise. While it may be easier for diseases with strong seasonality, such as influenza in temperate climates, where there are clearly defined periods with elevated case counts [79, 80], for vector-borne diseases which are hyper-endemic, such as dengue in Southeast Asia, reported case counts may always be non-zero and outbreak seasons are less clearly defined. Yet, detecting the onset of an epidemic as soon as possible can have a great impact on the number of lives saved, as it enables prompt ramp up of resource planning, healthcare management and a reduction in outbreak severity [81]. It is therefore crucial to propose and explore tools for outbreak detection/classification for hyper-endemic diseases. Second, characterizing the extremes of infectious disease transmission is also not explored in depth. Examples for the extremes of infectious disease transmission are the maximum of recorded case counts over a specific period of time or recorded case counts above a certain high quantile over all observed data. The maxima across a block of time provides the largest number of observed cases over that specific frequency and the data over a threshold over all observed data characterises values above the observed mean. Characterizing the extreme points of data, where health systems are likely to be most strained, is essential for policy planning purposes and can be done so with extreme value theory. While there are a few applications of extreme value theory to clinical settings [82, 83], an extensive exploration of extreme value theory to a population setting over hyper-endemic disease settings is scarce. These tools can provide notions of return periods or return levels, which provides the average time or an estimated average time between events and the level that is expected to be exceeded on average once in a prespecified number of years respectively. These are helpful in health policy planning as they allow us to understand the likelihood of healthcare capacity limits being exceeded. Third, while spatial risk factors for dengue infection are widely explored, with key drivers being urbanicity [84], climate [85] and population density [86], key questions remain on the continued persistence of dengue, especially in a hyper-endemic setting, such as Southeast Asia and, more specifically, Singapore. In particular, high movement patterns between more than one dengue hyper-endemic region may lead to sustained dengue transmission due to importation/exportation of strains across regions. Novel population mixing conditions such as population-wide social distancing policies to curb the onward community spread of SARS-CoV-2 may also change exposure risk for dengue across entire populations. Understanding these may allow us to further understand the implications of increased travel between regions and how other non-pharmaceutical interventions for respiratory diseases may spill-over to vector-borne diseases.

Specific aims of the thesis

The central aims for this thesis are thus motivated by key gaps in modelling vector-borne diseases such as dengue. Specifically we will: 1. Explore tools to classify and characterize dengue outbreaks, using Singapore and Thailand as case studies and regime switching models as the primary tools for analysis. These are compared against current measures already developed for use in infectious disease outbreak classification. We will explore key temporal and spatial drivers of these outbreaks post-hoc, to provide a more complete epidemiological perspective for these methods. 2. Explore tools to characterize the extremes of dengue transmission, primarily by exploring the applicability of tools developed from extreme value theory. We further develop novel methods and estimation strategies taking into account parameter dependence in extreme value distributions and explore key drivers for extremes of dengue transmission. 3. Develop new strategies to infer the propensity for dengue transmission to be sustained over multiple dengue hyper-endemic regions. We will assess the utility of these tools in a public health setting by exploring their abilities to conduct forecasting over multiple regions. New notions for inferring how dengue transmission may persist over a large spatial scale are also explored. 4. Exploit social distancing policies for SARS-CoV-2 as a natural experiment, develop new identification schemes to correctly characterize these policy interventions and explore how changes in population mixing and exposure patterns lead to a rise or fall in dengue transmission over time.

Dengue, a mosquito-borne infectious disease caused by the dengue viruses, is present in many parts of the tropical and subtropical regions of the world. All four serotypes of dengue viruses are endemic in Singapore, an equatorial city-state. Frequent outbreaks occur, sometimes leading to national epidemics. However, few studies have attempted to characterize breakpoints which precede large rises in dengue case counts. In this chapter, Bayesian regime switching (BRS) models were employed to infer epidemic and endemic regimes of dengue transmissions, each containing regime specific autoregressive processes which drive the growth and decline of dengue cases, estimated using a custom built multi-move Gibbs sampling algorithm. Posterior predictive checks indicate that BRS replicates temporal trends in dengue transmissions well and nowcast accuracy assessed using a post-hoc classification scheme showed that BRS classification accuracy is robust even under limited data with the AUC-ROC at 0.935. LASSO-based regression and bootstrapping was used to account for plausibly high dimensions of climatic factors affecting dengue transmissions, which was then estimated using cross-validation to conduct statistical inference on long-run climatic effects on the estimated regimes. BRS estimates epidemic and endemic regimes of dengue in Singapore which are characterized by persistence across time, lasting an average of 20 weeks and 66 weeks respectively, with a low probability of transitioning away from their regimes. Climate analysis with LASSO indicates that long-run climatic effects up to 20 weeks ago do not differentiate epidemic and endemic regimes. Lastly, by fitting BRS to simulated disease data generated from a stochastic Susceptible-Infected-Recovered model, mechanistic links between infectivity and regimes classified using BRS were provided. The model proposed could be applied to other localities and diseases under minimal data requirements where transmission counts over time are collected.

Introduction

An estimated 390 million dengue infections occur annually creating considerable health and economic burdens [1]. Dengue is widespread across South-East Asian countries and is classified as hyper-endemic due to all four serotypes being in active circulation [87]. Widespread ongoing urbanization and greater host movement rates via both domestic and international travel have increased transmission, particularly across highly connected cities such as Singapore. With favorable climatic conditions, a large daily influx of travelers and high population density, the conditions for dengue transmission are ideal, as reflected in national case counts being non-zero for every week in the past 10 years. Vector control remains the primary control method for dengue, of the two dengue mosquito vectors Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus in Singapore [88]. The low seroprevalence rates across the national population make the implementation of vaccination using Dengvaxia (CYD-TDV) challenging, therefore techniques such as Wolbachia, fogging and breeding site reduction are utilized to both prevent and control epidemics [89]. The successful application of these methods in epidemics depends on the correct timing for control ramp up in which house inspections increase, community engagement campaigns are rolled out to generate awareness in breeding site reduction and fogging in areas deemed at high risk of transmission [88]. The characterization of dengue transmission dynamics through time is therefore critical. Finite resources for ramp up of vector control measures also beg the question of estimating the duration and severity of epidemics in different climates. Compartmental models and statistical models such as time series estimation and machine learning can characterize dengue transmission dynamics. Compartmental models model infection as a function of separate compartments, and the evolution of the epidemic may be described by ordinary differential equations [72]. However, parameter estimation and inputting initial values for compartmental models often only estimate an epidemic curve, but usually cannot explicitly model different endemic and epidemic phases of disease transmission where case counts are low and high respectively [72]. Statistical frameworks often characterize time series through autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) modelling or machine learning. ARIMA and machine learning both explain the current realization of infectious disease dynamics by its past observations and past exogenous variables. ARIMA type models have been widely used to fit dengue time series [90] due to the ease of interpretability. Lagged climatic variables have been assessed to affect dengue and influenza transmission counts linearly in temperate climates [91], but the signal for climatic variables on dengue transmission counts has been found to be weak in tropical climates [92]. A combined approach using time series subsceptible-infected-recovered (TSIR) models takes into account the evolution of population across time as well as autoregressive disease dynamics, was first introduced by Bjørnstad, Finkenstädt and Grenfell for measles [93]. It was then further developed for modelling multi-strain diseases such as dengue by including cross-immunity dynamics [94, 95, 96]. A limiting factor of these models is that they cannot account for the plausibly nonlinear and time varying structure of infectious disease transmission across time. Calibration of multi-strain TSIR models also require virological surveillance data, which may not be always available. Machine learning tools such as random forest and least squares shrinkage operator (LASSO) have been proven to outperform ARIMA type models in predictive metrics such as root mean square error for H5N1 [97] and the ROC in classifying dengue outbreaks respectively [92]. However, these tools have difficulties in inferring the variables driving infectious disease transmission counts and do not have standard confidence intervals to determine model compatibility with data. The variable importance factor calculated for ensemble methods such as random forest remains only asymptotically valid and may not be useful for small count infectious disease time series data [98]. Regime switching models are used to model phenomena in which time series are characterized by characteristic changes in behavior [99]. They originated from econometrics to account for the changes in behavior in macroeconomic variables such as inflation and debt

[100] and they have potential applicability in modelling disease states and transmission due to the differential behavior of disease transmissions in epidemic and endemic periods [81]. Martinez et al. explored influenza epidemic detection in Spain using a regime switching framework where highly seasonal dynamics of infection allowed distinct classification of epidemic and endemic disease states [81]. The framework used could detect periods and behavior of high influenza transmission counts and low influenza transmission counts. However, regime switching models have yet to be applied for transmission dynamics which are highly non-seasonal, irregular and persistent such as dengue within tropical climates [87, 101]. This chapter therefore explores the utility of regime switching models to investigate the dynamic signature of dengue within Singapore. We aim to classify the irregularity in epidemic lengths, estimate the different dynamics in dengue transmission across the different regimes and examine whether climate characterizes the estimated regimes. First, Bayesian Autoregressive (BAR) models of various lags derived using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) estimate dengue transmissions across time as a benchmark model to explain dengue transmission counts. Next, we utilized Bayesian fixed transition probability regime switching models (BRS) to account for the endemic-epidemic structure of dengue while allowing autoregressive parameters to vary in separate regimes. Model explanatory power was assessed with the mean-absolute percentage error, log Bayes factor, relative deviance information criterion, as well as the predictive power of the BRS by ex-ante classification accuracy of regimes. Next, we estimate the influence of climate on dengue transmission behavior by using the classified regimes from BRS as a dependent variable to climate with the least absolute shrinkage operator (LASSO) with logistic link using area under the receiver operator characteristic (AUC-ROC) as a tuning criterion. The LASSO was subject to nonparametric bootstrapping to recover confidence intervals for inference of climatic variables on the classified regimes. Lastly, using simulated data generated from a stochastic Susceptible-Infected-Recovered model, we provide possible mechanistic links between infectivity and regimes classified using BRS.

Sources of Data

Dengue incidence data were collected by the Ministry of Health, Singapore with mandatory notification of virologically confirmed or laboratory-confirmed cases [62]. We aggregated individual-level data into the weekly number of cases from 2000 to 2017. The Institutional Review Board of the National University of Singapore provided the ethical approval for this study. Climate data were obtained from ERA5, published by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts. ERA5 provides hourly estimates across a 30km grid [102], which we have aggregated over a weekly timescale and spatially averaged over Singapore. Mean, minimum and maximum air temperature at 2m was calculated to represent thermal forcing and stress on vector population growth [103, 104, 105], and total rainfall for the weekly interval obtained for breeding site availability [106, 107, 108]. Air temperature and dewpoint temperature were utilized to calculate saturation vapor pressure and actual vapor pressure using Tetens formulae, whence relative and absolute humidity could be estimated using standard formula [109].

Model Assessment

First, Geweke convergence diagnostic checks are conducted to ensure that MCMC estimation is well-behaved [112]. Residual autocorrelation is computed to ascertain whether dengue transmission dynamics are properly accounted for and to determine the maximum lag order for each specification. Next, posterior predictive checks are conducted by comparing the fit of the posterior predictive distribution with the actual data. Fourth, posterior probabilities of the fitted regimes, which provide a measure of uncertainty to regime classification are computed. Fifth, we used mean-absolute percentage error (MAPE) and log Bayes factor as the model assessment criterion for comparing model fit of dengue differenced transmission counts between BAR and BRS as it balances explanatory power of the estimated model along with parsimony. The log Bayes factor was computed using naïve Monte Carlo simulation as detailed in Appendix A. Additionally, the relative deviance information criterion (DIC) comparing BRS models to the BAR models was computed, as detailed in Appendix A. Lastly, ex-ante classification efficacy of BRS on regimes is conducted in a rolling manner, where we fit the BRS specification sequentially from around 1/3 of the data set at the 250th week onwards and increase the information set provided to the BRS by 1 more week in each refitting. We obtain the contemporaneously classified regime from the regime fitted to the final timepoint in each model iteration and compared the classification to the case where BRS is estimated on the full dataset.

BRS models above can identify characteristic changes in the behavior of dengue case counts, which form different repeating phases where regimes alternate. The methods utilized here can also estimate the variables which explain the trends of dengue case counts in each of these regimes. We interpret regime 1 as a stable endemic regime where changes in dengue counts are pushed down to a proportion of the week before, and regime 2 an epidemic regime which is characterized by an increase in change of dengue differenced dengue counts in comparison to the week before. Both the endemic and epidemic regimes are highly noncyclical with varying temporal lengths in Singapore (Figure 2.1,2.2). Although 3 regimes were considered for dengue transmission, constant switching and non-persistence between regimes indicated overfitting for this specification (Appendix A) therefore 2 regimes were used to be representative of Singapore’s dengue transmission behavior. The results show varying levels of persistence across the epidemic and endemic dengue regimes with the endemic regime generally being more persistent on average. However, when we explored the effects of climate on the estimated regimes, results suggest that climatic factors up to even 20 weeks before do not affect the probability of being within a regime or another. (Appendix A). While the importance of climatic factors such as temperature and humidity for dengue counts were previously discussed for Singapore [117] and other countries [118, 119], along with preliminary estimation of a BAR-3 with climatic variables pointing towards near term effects of precipitation and dewpoint temperature on change in dengue counts up to 3 weeks before (Appendix A), our results suggest that regimes in dengue transmissions are driven structurally by the changes in dengue counts themselves rather than climatic factors. Mechanistically, the epidemic regimes classified using BRS on simulated data also point towards the epidemic regime as periods of high infection probability (Figure 2.7). Lastly, the BRS method allows nowcasting of dengue epidemics through inferring differenced dengue transmission counts with fairly high predictive accuracy (Figure 2.4, Table 2.2). Exploring long-term structural dynamics of dengue is important for vector control as it signals that considerable forward planning and financial resource allocation is necessary for successful implementation. Distinguishing between epidemic and endemic regimes, prediction of upcoming regimes and characterizing the persistence and climatic differences of epidemics provides policy makers with the estimated duration required for epidemic control should other data such as serotype switching be unavailable. The methods described here could be easily applied to other countries where dengue transmission counts are collected. One potential application could be to compare the regime lengths and dynamics of different countries, and explore the factors driving different dengue regime patterns. There are several limitations of the approach outlined above. The regime switching model structure demands parsimony as each additional regime requires an additional fold of explanatory variables for estimation. Longer dynamics are thus harder to estimate for BRS. While the model estimates phenomenological components, such as the evolution of dengue case counts through autoregressive parameters and structural components, such as the regimes of dengue transmission, the interpretation of structural breaks within the model remains a largely qualitative exercise. Serotype switching, which is documented to be a possible cause of epidemics in endemic regions [23] is omitted due to the unavailability of data. Sensitivity to misclassification may also make BRS models a suboptimal forecasting tool [120], which limits BRS to in-sample analysis of dengue counts. Lastly, further work is required to enhance the model to incorporate more policy components which may affect dengue transmission such as vector control efforts. Incorporating vector control and serotype switching will allow investigation in the important interactions between structural and phenomological effects on the temporal evolution of dengue. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first application of regime switching autoregressive models for analyzing dengue transmission dynamics across separable states. We found evidence that epidemic and endemic regimes which characterize dengue transmission are highly persistent and are not associated to climatic factors. Our results point towards the need for long-term policy formation for effective vector control which is timed with upcoming epidemic switches.

Motivation for the next chapter

It remains unclear whether this finding was only applicable in the context of a large tropical city or whether the dynamics of alternating regimes could be replicated across other countries in the region. In the next chapter, I will address the issue of expanding the proposed BRS notion to a larger spatial scale. I will further attempt to understand the characteristics and drivers of epidemics classified under the regime switching model proposed.

Revealing Two Dynamic Dengue

Epidemic Clusters in Thailand

This work has been published as: Lim JT, Han YT, Dickens BS, Cook AR. Revealing Two Dynamic Dengue Epidemic Clusters in Thailand. BMC Infectious Diseases. 2020 May 1;16(5):e1007839.

Background: Thailand is home to around 69 million individuals. Dengue is hyper-endemic and all 4 serotypes are in active circulation in the country. Dengue outbreaks occur almost annually within Thailand in at least one province but the spatio-temporal and environmental interface of these outbreaks has not been studied. Methods: We develop Bayesian regime switching (BRS) models to characterize outbreaks, their persistence and infer their likelihood of occurrence across time for each administrative province where dengue case counts are collected. BRS was compared against two other classification tools and their agreement is assessed. We further examine how these spatio-temporal clusters of outbreak clusters arise by comparing reported dengue case counts, urban population, urban land cover, climate and flight volumes on the province level. Results: Two dynamic dengue epidemic clusters were found nationally. One cluster consists of 47 provinces and is highly outbreak prone. Provinces with a large number of case counts, urban population, urban land cover and incoming flight passengers are associated to the epidemic prone cluster of dengue. Climate has an effect on determining the probability of outbreaks over time within provinces, but have less influence on whether provinces belong to the epidemic prone cluster. BRS found high agreement with other classification tools. Conclusions: Importation and urbanization drives the risk of outbreaks across regions strongly. In provinces estimated to have high epidemic persistence, more resource allocation to vector control should be applied to those localities as heightened transmission counts are likely to occur over a longer period of time. Clustering of epidemic and non-epidemic prone areas also highlights the need for prioritization of resource allocation for disease mitigation over provinces in Thailand.

Background

Thailand is home to around 69 million individuals [121], with dengue considered to be hyper-endemic due to all four serotypes being in active circulation within the country. Each of 77 provinces in Thailand have on average, non-zero reported counts over the past 10 years, which create considerable health and economic burdens. Widespread urbanization, favourable climatic conditions and increased human mobility across provinces are ideal for dengue transmission [85]. Vector control has been and continues to be the primary control method for the two dominant dengue mosquito vectors, Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus as other control methods such as Dengvaxia (CYD-TDV) vaccination face issues of low seropositive rates, especially among the younger age groups [122]. The resources allocated for vector control are unfortunately limited even for the case of high income countries. A deep understanding of the epidemiological factors leading to dengue epidemics is therefore necessary, for appropriate resource planning and effective public health policy making. The identification of high risk, epidemic prone areas with frequent outbreaks would help allocate resources to these zones to reduce the public health burdens of dengue – which is especially important for countries which are vast, such as Thailand. Considerable work has been conducted to understand the temporal behaviour of dengue in Thailand, ranging from peri-urban settings [28], provincial level studies analyzing province specific trends [123] as well as national level transmission patterns [122, 77]. At the national level, studies often split reported counts at the province level in order to reconstruct suitable inference on how dengue is transmitted and the ways surveillance can be implemented on different frequencies [124]. A substantial number of studies also focus on understanding predicting dengue incidence in Thailand [77, 125]. A key gap, however, is understanding the patterns of disease outbreaks, instead of the number of reported cases. This is less frequently attempted due to the difficulty in classifying what an outbreak is, or when an outbreak starts or ends. This difficulty in ascertaining outbreak patterns and understanding the drivers of outbreaks, is further compounded for a hyper-endemic disease such as dengue in Thailand, where case counts are consistently reported over time. Yet, as it has been estimated that early detection and in-time clinical care will drastically reduce the dengue fatality rates [126], the identification of outbreaks, their patterns and where they occur is essential to public health decision making for medical resource allocation and staffing. This will help optimize medical care delivery in an outbreak scenario and provide substantial gains in population health. In this study, we therefore aim to understand the patterns and biological reasons for dengue outbreaks in Thailand, by first developing suitable methods to characterize outbreaks and infer their likelihood of occurrence across time for each administrative province where dengue case counts are collected. We further examined whether spatio-temporal clusters of outbreak clusters arise, and whether human population and biologically relevant factors such as reporting rate, urban land cover, population and climatic forcing affect these outbreaks over a large spatial scale.

Dengue case count data

Monthly dengue case count data in Thailand were collected by the Ministry of Public Health, Thailand with mandatory notification of virologically confirmed or laboratory-confirmed cases. To account for difference in population sizes among different regions and further allow interpretability of the regression coefficients, we normalized each province’s dengue case count time series to a scale of 0 to 1 for the analysis by subtracting each province’s timepoint by the province’s minimum observed value and divide them by the range of values observed across time. Data is available for 76 regions in Thailand from 2007 to 2018. We removed Bueng Kan from analysis as it was split from Nong Khai province in 2011. Dengue case count data was used to infer dengue outbreak patterns across provinces from 2007 to 2018.

Climate data

Climate data were obtained from ERA5, published by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts. ERA5 provides hourly estimates across a 30km grid [102], which we have aggregated over a monthly timescale and spatially averaged for provinces of Thailand. Mean, minimum and maximum air temperature at 2m was calculated to represent thermal forcing and stress on vector population growth, and total rainfall for the weekly interval obtained for breeding site availability [127]. Air temperature and dewpoint temperature were utilized to calculate saturation vapor pressure and actual vapor pressure using Teten’s formula, where relative and absolute humidity could then be estimated using the standard formula [109]. Absolute and relative humidity are used to represent the impacts of ambient moisture on breeding potential [117]. Climate data was used to determine whether climate is a driver of dengue outbreaks.

Human population data

Human population data for Thailand were obtained from Thailand’s Official Statistics Registration Systems website, published by the Office of Registration Administration, Department of Local Administration. The population data provided is available for 77 regions in Thailand from 2007 to 2018, with the inclusion of Bueng Kan only after 2011. The yearly total population and births for each province were obtained for the required time period to determine the reporting rate of dengue via time series susceptible-infected recovered analysis [93]. Urban population and land cover data was used to determine whether outbreak prone clusters have higher rates of urbanization.

Flight data

Monthly number of air ticket bookings during 2015-6 were obtained from OAG for every origin-destination route with up to two connections. The number of incoming flight passenger numbers for each airport within each province was then used to determine whether outbreak prone clusters have higher numbers of incoming flight passengers.

Identifying dynamic clusters

We then assessed the tendency for outbreaks to be clustered across space and time, by using the graphical dissimilarity approach [130]. After which, we classified provinces into dynamic clusters using the partitioning around medoids (PAM) algorithm. This analysis allows us determine whether province-level dynamic clusters of epidemic prone areas exist over time. Dynamic time warped distances are used to determine the clusters each province are in and to account for possible temporal dependence between outbreak probabilities between provinces. We preset the number of clusters to 2 to 20 sequentially and ran the PAM algorithm, computing cluster validity indices to determine the optimal number of clusters for the classified outbreaks. The full assessment details for the optimal number of dynamic clusters is described below.

Clustering assessment metrics

Clustering validity indices were used to determine the optimal number of clusters. Namely, we used the Silhouette index, Calinski-Harabasz index, Davies-Bouldin index, Dunn Index and COP index (Table B.1). The optimal number of epidemic clusters were then determined by majority consensus across these indices. Temporal cluster stability was conducted by subsetting our dataset to different blocks. Namely, a starting point was randomly selected and we applied the DTW-PAM clustering algorithm for 1 to 10 clusters and 20 to 50 observations after the starting point. CVI was evaluated for each subsetted time series, with the classified clusters for each region matched against the classified clusters on the full time series. Graphical analysis of the dissimilarity matrix indicates that the estimated epidemic probabilities across regions have clustering tendencies across time. 2 to 10 clusters were pre-specified and initially fit using DTW-PAM, with cluster validity indices computed on each iteration. Among the 6 cluster validity indices computed, majority consensus indicates that 2 was the optimal amount of clusters. Only the COP index indicated 10 as the optimal number of clusters. Cross-validating over 30 repetitions for cluster stability together with computing the cluster validity indices (CVIs) for each repetition showed that 2 clusters were the optimal number across samples under majority consensus of the CVIs.

Two dynamic dengue epidemic clusters were found, after conducting cluster analysis and assessment of the optimal number of clusters. The full results of these assessment checks are reported in the Appendix B. Cluster analysis indicates that cluster 1 (C1) consists of 29 provinces compared to cluster 2 (C2), which has 47. C1 is less prone to outbreaks compared to C2, with a concentration of outbreaks for C1 around 2013 to 2014, while C2 has outbreaks occurring almost yearly, asides from 2017. Spatially, the outbreak prone C2 is concentrated around the Northern region of Thailand, while the less outbreak prone C1 dispersed around the central and southern regions of Thailand (Figure 3.1). Substantial variation in the persistence of the epidemics is estimated at the province-level, varying from 82% for Chon Buri province (C1) to 73% for Sing Buri province (C1) for staying in an epidemic. Likewise, for the baseline endemic regime, Phitsanulok (C2) province has the highest level of persistence at 99% and Prachuap Khiri Khan (C1) has the lowest level of persistence at 87%. In general, the likelihood of transitions from an epidemic regime into the endemic regime are in general higher compared to the likelihood of transitions from an endemic regime to an epidemic regime. The likelihood of staying within an epidemic regime is also lower, as compared to the likelihood of staying within an endemic regime. Jointly, this indicates that the epidemic regimes are less persistent compared to the endemic regimes across provinces (Figure 3.2). C2 which is more outbreak prone, is also observed to have a larger group of provinces having a higher likelihood of staying within an epidemic regime, compared to the less outbreak prone C1 group (Figure 3.2).

Model assessment

Fitting the BRS indicated that a 3 lag specification was sufficient to account for residual autocorrelation in dengue time series across all regions. In general, the BRS specification was able to detect breakpoints which precede large rises in dengue case counts across provinces when estimated using the full set. It was able to detect these breakpoints even when restricting observations to only contemporaneous and past timepoints (Figure 3.1C). Concordance as measured by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) showed that there is agreement between other outbreak detection measures with an average AUC-ROC of 0.840 across provinces with more than 60% provinces producing an AUC-ROC larger than 0.8, indicating good outbreak classification concordance in the majority of provinces [132]. Comparing CUSUM(d=2, k=1) and BRS, there is an average AUC-ROC of 0.840 across provinces with more than 60% provinces producing an AUC-ROC larger than 0.8, indicating good outbreak concordance in the majority of provinces [132] .AUC-ROC is highest in Samut Sakhon (0.989), Chumphon (0.978) and lowest for Phang Nga (0.438) and Phayao (0.451). The simple regression model was also run sequentially with data inputs increasing from 5 to 11 months, with outbreak classification against BRS conducted at each step. The AUC-ROC aggregated from all provinces increases from 0.780 to 0.869 when data input for the simple regression model increased from 5 to 11 months respectively. Increasing the data input into the simple regression may have increased the concordance with BRS as the BRS accounts for the full history of dengue cases in each province. Using the simple regression model with dengue counts of the preceding 5 months, we ascribe the highest concordance between simple regression and BRS in Ang Thong (0.956) and Chiang Rai (0.951), while Khon Kaen and Phayao showed lowest concordance producing AUC-ROC 0.523 and 0.550 respectively. However, the overall concordance is good with the average AUC-ROC across provinces being 0.815. Simple regression classifies outbreaks with less similarity compared to CUSUM when compared the the BRS.

Results indicate two dynamic epidemic clusters in Thailand from 2007 to 2018; a baseline cluster 1 (C1), with a large majority of epidemics occurring sporadically, and an epidemic prone cluster 2 (C2), where outbreaks occur on an almost yearly basis. In epidemic prone C2, results indicate that epidemics are more persistent and estimated epidemic persistence probabilities are higher (Figure 3.2), compared to the baseline C1, where epidemics are less likely to persist over time. This result is consistent with our results analysing dengue outbreaks in dengue hyper-endemic Singapore, where epidemics are found to be less persistent compared to the baseline state (Chapter 2). For Thailand, central to policy planners are four suggestions. In provinces estimated to have high epidemic persistence, more resource allocation to vector control should be applied to those localities as heightened transmission counts are likely to occur in C2 over a longer period of time compared to C1. Minimizing dengue transmissions in areas which have higher urban land cover and possess larger importation risk is also important, due to their epidemic prone nature as elaborated below. Clusters as estimated in this study showed key endogenous and exogenous drivers. Urban land cover and urban population, are especially prominent in Bangkok and its adjacent areas, where it is assigned to the epidemic prone C2 and there may be several ecological reasons for its occurrence. Urbanization is strongly associated with increased dengue incidence [37], and increased population density from urbanization leads to an increase in viral diversity and the number of possible dengue transmission chains [133]. Urban practices such as storing water in plastic containers also increase the breeding potential for the Ae. aegypti vector [134], which thereby increases the vector’s viability in these regions. The tendency of Ae. aegypti to live in human homes [37], also compounds transmissibility, due to the higher density of urban centers as compared to peri-urban and rural settings in other provinces. Our results corroborate with trends which have emerged from other dengue endemic countries such as Vietnam, where dengue transmission is concentrated in large, urbanized areas such as Ha Noi and Ho Chi Minh [135]. In totality, these factors may have induced the epidemic prone conditions in Bangkok and the surrounding locations. The classification of Bangkok, Chiang Mai and Chiang Rai to epidemic prone C2, which are the provinces which correspondingly have the largest number of incoming flight passengers and largest number of case counts reported is also notable. Several key pathways may have led to elevated dengue transmissions and outbreak risk within the region. Increased risk of dengue epidemics may result from importation of viraemic mosquitoes due to air travel from Thailand’s status as an air traffic hub [136]. Viraemic individuals may similarly result in dengue importation, as showed from the co-circulation of dengue serotypes within South East Asia and the importation of different serotypes into Thailand [136, 137]. As a result, the status of Bangkok, Chiang Mai and Chiang provinces being air travel hubs may have further heightened the importation risk of new dengue serotypes or genotypes, increased viral diversity, caused selection of strains of higher fitness which the local population are not exposed to previously and potentially seed new epidemics [31]. The influence of climate on mosquito breeding potential and the transmissibility of dengue is well-studied [138, 91]. In this chapter, varying levels of importance are ascribed to climatic factors in determining dengue epidemic probability, with 1-4 months lagged average temperature and total precipitation having the highest level of inclusion. This constrasts prior results of climate not being able to substantially explain dengue epidemics in Singapore [47] and may be due to the limited geographical range of Singapore as compared to Thailand. In Thailand, the importance of climate on dengue epidemics is observed across both epidemic prone C2 and baseline C1, which suggests that climatic factors do affect probabilities of epidemics occurring with some degree (Figure 3.3). However, as inclusion probability intervals overlap across both C1 and C2 for variables on all lags, there is only evidence that climate provides a signal to dengue epidemic risk temporally, rather than affect said risk through the epidemic clusters as classified. Studies have also shown the effects of longitude and latitude on dengue transmission potential, through climate and thus vector breeding potential [77]. Although the epidemic clusters detected do have some geographical patterns (Figure 3.1), the dispersion of provinces through the entirety of the Thailand may have written off potential distinguishing climatic factors between clusters. Our study centrally extends previous work conducted on dengue in Thailand [124, 77] by examining dengue epidemic probabilities and outbreaks over time as the quantity of interest, rather than dengue case counts. Compared to standard outbreak classification tools, BRS as developed in this chapter, can be used to infer separable regimes and crucially, provide information on the duration and persistence of epidemics without pre-specified thresholds, even when only limited information on reported dengue cases at the provincial level is provided. It can capture the growth and decline of monthly dengue transmission counts across provinces in Thailand, and outbreak classification using BRS is also concordant with other benchmark methods in outbreak classification, which further validates the framework (Appendix B). Cluster analysis is also robust, according to majority consensus of cluster validity indices in our analysis, which then allows us to draw links between our analysis and biologically relevant factors. Lastly, climate is associated with dengue epidemics but with varying levels of importance; there are inclusions of at least one lagged climatic variable being associated to epidemics across time in all provinces. Several limitations are present in this chapter. First, using monthly dengue transmissions as demonstrated in this chapter would not be suitable for vector control purposes as policy planners would want to ramp up vector control as soon as an outbreak is classified or forecasted. Forecasting outbreaks under only 1 step or more ahead monthly frequencies is only possible due to monthly frequencies being the model input for BRS in this study and hence would not be timely enough for surveillance purposes. However, BRS could easily be applied to weekly nowcasts, forecasts and inference as demonstrated by other infectious disease applications [81, 47]. The reporting rate of dengue transmissions may have been driven by province level variations in healthcare accessibility, which may affect our estimates of epidemic persistence and classifications. However, these estimates are likely skewed towards the null due to under-reporting likely to occur rather than overreporting of dengue transmission counts. The variations in reporting rate are also minimal as estimated within this chapter. Number of asymptomatic dengue cases are also not available, but is not likely to affect the results as the primary parameters of interest are regimes, rather than predicted case counts. Reported number of dengue haemorrhagic fever and dengue shock syndrome cases, while also available, was not used as a dependent due to zero-inflation. Further work should develop and assess models which can generalize the BRS to such settings. Serotype composition and number of individuals travelling across land routes are important factors which may influence dengue outbreaks, but are not available. Future studies should attempt to study the relation between outbreaks and these covariates. Lastly, as data on biologically relevant factors such as urban land cover, urban population, and flight passengers are not available at the same frequency as dengue case counts, their effects on regimes and outbreak clusters can only be conducted post estimation of BRS.

Being able to classify and infer the risk and persistence of dengue outbreaks is important, as significant health risks surface from these events. Contemporaneously classifying outbreaks, understanding outbreak characteristics and the drivers of these outbreaks is important for public health policy, as it provides policy makers with an indicator to ramp up the delivery of care and vector control to those localities. Our study found that provinces with a large number of case counts, urban population, urban land cover and incoming flight passengers are associated to the outbreak prone cluster of dengue. Climate has an effect on determining outbreak probabilities, but have less influence on whether they belong to the outbreak prone cluster. To the best of my knowledge, this is the first study to classify dengue outbreaks, estimate transition probabilities and show the clustering of epidemics on a national level in Thailand. The methods developed are also easily extendable to any infectious disease with transmission counts recorded over a substantial period of time.

Motivation for the next chapter

In this chapter, we have explored how dengue transmission on the national and regional level can be explained by alternating regimes. We have also post-hoc found dengue outbreaks to have spatial clustering tendencies. The spatio-temporal clustering of dengue outbreaks is corroborated with other studies which have done so by examining variations in human population density [86] and weather [139] in relation to dengue transmission. Accounting for spatial dynamics pre-estimation may provide more information on how dengue case counts evolve. However, the proposed methods in this chapter only accounted for these clustering tendencies to occur after classification of regimes and did not consider spatio-temporal structures in their estimation. Imposing spatio-temporal structures onto the model specification may provide efficiency gains in inference and allow us understand the interactions in dengue transmission between regions better [140]. In the following chapter, I will propose methods to infer spatio-temporal interactions of dengue transmissions and how these interactions persist over time using a spatial extension of the vector-autoregression.

Introduction

Malaysia and Singapore are populated by around 38 million people. In both countries, dengue is classified as hyper-endemic due to all four dengue serotypes being in active circulation, putting most individuals at risk of dengue infection, and creating considerable health and economic burdens [1, 141]. Widespread urbanization, favourable climatic conditions and increased human mobility across highly connected cities such as Singapore and Kuala Lumpur are ideal for dengue transmission [85, 47], as reflected in the long term non-zero weekly counts in Singapore and all administrative regions in Malaysia [142]. The use of the only licensed vaccine, Dengvaxia (CYD-TDV), is problematic given low seroprevalence rates especially among younger age groups due to past history of successful vector control [9, 88]. As a result, vector control remains the primary public health intervention, targeting the two dominant dengue mosquito vectors, Aedes aegypti and Ae. albopictus, which are endemic to both countries [142, 143]. Vector control means include community outreach, breeding site reduction and, more recently, Wolbachia, for epidemic prevention and control [142, 144]. The timing and scale of these interventions are critical for effective epidemic mitigation and warning systems and require an understanding of the spatial patterns of dengue transmission and the risk of spillover of epidemics from one region to another. Southeast Asia has increasing levels of connectivity and human movement which has contributed to elevated mortality and high economic losses from past pandemics such as influenza A (H1N1) [39]. Substantial literature exists on using mathematical models to describe spatio-temporal patterns of disease behaviour such as foot-and-mouth disease and Ebola through animal and human host movements respectively [145, 146]. Statistical approaches include space-time Bayesian hierarchical modelling, cluster and network analysis between geographical regions [147, 148, 149]. However, these often have a substantial computational burden or suffer from dimensionality issues due to the temporal persistence of disease transmissions and large number of regions, which result in a large number of parameters to describe the spatial, temporal and spatio-temporal patterns. Space-time recursive autoregressions (STARs) are used for geographical, financial and economic modelling where multiple time series have to be estimated simultaneously [150] while taking into account spatio-temporal dependencies. This approach overcomes the constraint of having a substantial number of spatial parameters by subsuming them under a weight matrix describing geographical distance and/or connectivity across regions. While vector autoregression is a model structure which can capture interdependencies among multiple evolving variables—such as disease case counts across regions—using its own lagged values and that of the other model variables [100], the STAR is a special case with additional spatial terms accounting for these same endogenous variable interactions between-regions. Even though between-region dependencies are subsumed within the weight matrix, a large number of parameters can still be individually estimated for an appropriate specification in disease dynamics from complex interdependencies between climate, dengue transmission counts and inter-regional transmissions over long time lags. In this chapter, we propose regularization as a potential solution as penalties are imposed on a STAR specification to induce sparsity, thereby creating what we denote a sparse space-time recursive autoregression (SSTAR). Dengue case warning systems and predictions have been carried out multiple times for both Singapore [46] and Malaysia [151]. Yet, there has been limited exploration on the effects of importation between the Malaysian regions and Singapore through human movement. The two countries lie on the Malay peninsula and part of Borneo, with Malaysia having 13 administrative regions on the former and three on the latter. Singapore is highly connected to Malaysia through two crossings linking Pulau Ujong to Johor, with a daily movement of 300 000 individuals, comprising ≥ 5% of Singapore’s total population. Additionally, the

flight route between Kuala Lumpur and Singapore is the busiest in the world, with 30 537 flights made annually [152, 153]. Once an infectious person enters a region in Malaysia or Singapore, there is potential for local transmission to occur with subsequent infections and a potential outbreak due to the presence of the vector and suitable vector breeding conditions [126]. With such multipath complexities, dengue transmission within Malaysia and between Malaysia and Singapore should be considered contemporaneously to better understand the observed dengue transmission counts in each region. This chapter therefore explores the use of STAR and SSTAR to investigate the interregional dynamic signature of dengue between Malaysia and Singapore. We aim to estimate the level of dengue transmissions in each region attributable to other regions, while controlling for local dengue dynamics, and create a regional forecasting model for estimating changes in dengue transmission. To this end, we first examine the descriptive ability of inter-regional counts on each region using Granger causality and likelihood ratio tests. Next, using the STAR and SSTAR models, we ascertain how attributable previous dengue transmissions within or outside of a region are on local dengue transmission using forecast error impulse response analysis. Models are then assessed according to their ability to recreate each region’s dengue transmission count density using external dengue counts and locally observed climatic factors in a quasi-imputation approach. We forecast 1 to 4 week ahead with the mean absolute error criterion used to compare the predictive ability of STAR versus SSTAR for each region. Lastly, variable importance and statistical inference on variables which describe dengue transmission for each region are conducted using a block-bootstrap procedure.

The STAR is fit sequentially using equation-by-equation ordinary least squares or feasible generalized least squares (FGLS). Penalized Estimation of STAR

While STARs are able to estimate the effects of dengue transmission counts across different regions, having an additional set of dependent variables or time series drastically increases the number of parameters that need to be estimated. For example, having an additional lag term within our STAR would increase the number of coefficients to estimate by the number of time series, while having an additional time series added to our model would increase the number of coefficients to estimate by the number of coefficients within the observation equation. Within (C.3), we would have 16 regions and 10 maximum lags, estimating a plausible number of 320 coefficients. In this case, equation by equation ordinary least squares estimation of inter-regional dengue transmission would lead to unimportant but nonzero coefficients. This may overestimate the true inter-regional effect of dengue transmission and poorly forecast dengue transmission out of sample [113]. We thus conduct regularized estimation of our STAR specification, leading to sparse space-time recursive autoregression (SSTAR), which is a solution that provides a jointly parsimonious and interpretable model of inter-regional dengue transmissions. Inducing sparsity will allow for automatic variable selection among the many covariates with a large number of lags, and can still provide valid inferences on dengue transmission dynamics. Furthermore, it can allow better out-of-sample predictive performance [113]. This estimation extends the equation-by-equation OLS minimization problem to include some penalty term for our coefficients, with a tuning parameter ⁄ being estimated by a cross-validation scheme and the overall objective function minimized (4.2) using cyclical coordinate descent [156]:

For our model problem, we used 5-fold cross validation due to its ability to balance test error estimation bias and variance [113]. 5-fold cross validation was conducted by first partitioning our data to 5 samples of equal length, with a single subsample retained as the test set. The other subsamples are used to train the SSTAR model. This is repeated 5 times to produce cross-validation error over various ⁄ values. Model Inference

Coefficients in the STAR and SSTAR are difficult to interpret, unlike those in the canonical univariate autoregressive models. Lagged relationships across regions and time mean that standard interpretations for the canonical univariate time series do not apply. We utilize the forecast error impulse responses (FEIR) as a tool to interpret the models [157]. The FEIR is manifested through the impact of a shock on the noise term ‘t,j (on Yt+h for a region j) on the expected value of case counts for each other region at a future time horizon t + h. A unit shock ”j on some region and the subsequent FEIR value on other regions can be thought of as the effect of how an increase in the shocked regions counts on other regions case counts over time. As these procedures are normally reserved for time-dependent only vector autoregressions, we modify these procedures for the STAR and SSTAR. Model Adequacy

Granger causality tests (GCT) indicated that other regions counts are significantly Granger caused to dengue transmission counts in 8 out of 16 regions: Johor, Kedah, Negeri Sembilan, Pahang, Perak, Sarawak, Singapore and Terengganu through the connectivity matrix parameterization (Table 4.1). In 5 out of 16 regions, the GCT was also significant on the adjacency matrix parameterization, namely: Kedah, Pulau Pinang, Sabah, Sarawak and Singapore. In 6 regions, namely: Kelantan, Kuala Lumpur, Labuan, Melaka, Perlis and Selangor, none of the GCT for each matrix were significant, which indicates that for a majority of regions, dengue case counts from other regions may be Granger causing locally. Forecast Performance

SSTAR was able to beat 3 other comparison models, namely the space-time autoregressive (STAR), feasible generalized least squares estimated space-time autoregressive (STAR2), feasible generalized least squares estimated autoregressive (FGLS-AR) as well as the canonical autoregressive model (AR) on all regions for 1 to 4 week ahead forecast as indicated by a relative mean absolute error which is smaller than 1. In general, improvements in forecasting error increase as the forecast horizon increases for the proposed SSTAR model, with up to 18% improvement in forecast performance at 1 week ahead against the STAR and FGLS-AR models across all administrative regions. Improvements in forecast performance 2 to 4 week ahead similarly increased with the most improvements made against the baseline STAR movement at 34%, 47% and 56% respectively. In terms of absolute mean absolute error (MAE), the SSTAR has an average error of around 0.52 to 43.29 for one week ahead forecasts for the regions of Labuan and Selangor respectively. The largest errors in terms of absolute MAE are for 3 week ahead forecasts for Selangor at 72.03 (Appendix C) In comparison, the MAE for the baseline AR model is at around 0.69 to 49.19 for one week ahead forecasts for the regions of Labuan and Selangor respectively. The largest errors in terms of absolute MAE are for 3 week ahead forecasts for Selangor at 93.55 (Appendix C).

Covariate Importance

Bootstrapping SSTAR over 1000 iterations by rerunning the SSTAR over subsamples provided a measure of variable significance. 95% bootstrap intervals of connectivity/adjacency matrix for SSTAR showed that there is at least one lag term whose interval did not cross zero for all regions (Appendix C). Likewise, the bootstrap inclusion probabilities which provide a measure of variable importance indicated that the regions with connectivity matrix lags above the 0.5 probability cutoff on at least one occasion are Johor, Kedah, Kelantan, Kuala Lumpur, Labuan, Negeri Sembilan, Pahang, Perlis, Pulau Pinang, Sabah, Sawarak, Selangor, Singapore and Terengganu. Regions with no connectivity matrix lags below the 0.5 cutoff are Melaka and Perlis (Figure 4.3.4). In general, the adjacency matrix was included more often in bootstrap samples compared to the connectivity matrix in 11 of 16 regions: Johor, Kedah, Melaka, Pahang, Perlis, Pulau Pinang, Sabah, Sarawak, Selangor, Singapore and Terranganu. Inclusion of each regions own counts were also consistently above nonzero for at least 1 lag overall.

Shock Persistence

Lastly, FEIR analyses revealed long run shock persistence of dengue transmission across most regions when a particular region is subject to a one standard deviation shock (Appendix C). However, for regions where connectivity is not indicatively important on the assessment measures above (such as Labuan), shocking Labuan would lead to an almost instantaneous reversion to zero for all other regions, which indicates that a large increase or decrease in that region’s counts would not affect the other regions considered. In terms of largest cumulative effects across 50 weeks after a one standard deviation shock of some region, FEIR analyses indicated that Johor, Pahang, Melaka and Pulau Pinang provide the biggest long run effects to the entire region, whereas Singapore, Johor, Melaka and Perlis received the most effects from shocks when other regions are perturbed. In terms of overall effect from receiving as well as transmitting shocks to other regions, Singapore, Johor, Melaka and Kuala Lumpur ranked the highest in terms of effects (Figure 4.7).

The incidence of dengue, and thus the global burden of this particular infectious disease, has grown dramatically around the world in recent decades. For Singapore and Malaysia, a sharp increase in incidence has been observed in 2019 and 2020. Both countries are at increased risk of infectious disease spread due to their high population density, especially in Singapore, and high connectivity to other population centres in Asia and beyond, and thus would greatly benefit from the ability to conduct inference and dengue surveillance using readily available data. This can inform national public health officials to assist in controlling and combating the disease and its adverse effects once an outbreak is observed in a nearby region. The methods described in this chapter provide characterisation of inter-regional disease transmissions, which allows for the mentioned inference and forecasting to dengue cases transmitted across regions. Modelling inter-regional dengue transmission dynamics of dengue is important for understanding the transmission of cases across highly connected countries and disease surveillance purposes. However, little work has been conducted on modelling higher frequency weekly data due to data unavailability and the large number of parameters required to estimate these models. While [159] has estimated multi-annual synchronous relationships between dengue in Southeast Asian nations, these low frequency dynamic signatures are used more for long-term policy planning rather than real-time surveillance. The SSTAR methodology is able to explicitly account for a priori information on spatial connectivity to estimate relationships between regions in the Malay peninsula and northern Borneo on a weekly basis and account for changes in dengue dynamics across the region (Figure 4.1). While spatio-temporal effects of other region’s climate may be experienced locally, it can only affect dengue case count for each region only through dengue case counts from neighbouring regions from the connectivity matrix parameterisation. Climatic edge effects from surrounding regions may also influence vector breeding potential and thus case counts [160], and is likely to generate negligible noise on spatio-temporal inference due to the large spatial scale of the data in consideration. SSTAR is also able to disseminate and regularize medium term dengue transmission signatures for out-of-sample forecasting (Figure 4.4-4.5) and lastly, conduct inference on long term external attribution of dengue cases between regions with cumulative FEIRs (Figure 4.7). This allows for several public health applications. First, by inferring the spread of dengue across counties using SSTAR, officials may be provided with signals of a possible increase in local case counts from an outbreak in another a region. Second, by demonstrating SSTAR’s forecasting ability over 1 to 4 week ahead forecast assessment, the likely size of case counts in the near term can be estimated. This allows for vector control and other interventions to be ramped up to reduce the transmission potential. Results from univariate testing indicated that a region’s dengue transmission counts may affect those of its neighbours. In especially connected areas such as Johor and Singapore with a daily movement of 300 000 individuals over the straits of Johor, the connectivity matrices were important as indicated by the Granger causality test. The quasi-imputation study showed that even if we do not consider a region’s dengue transmission counts over long periods of time, substantial inference on the density of its dengue transmission counts may be achieved using the SSTAR approach compared to looking at climatic measurements alone. Notably, this exercise showed that the SSTAR approach with the connectivity matrix but with and without climate have similar imputation densities. As the connectivity matrix subsumes both the influence of dengue case counts from other regions and thus climatic forcing of other regions through external dengue case counts, it is likely that some of the results for imputation are driven by synchrony in climate as well as dengue case counts. Ascertaining the contributing magnitude of each factor, however, requires further data on human mobility. Bootstrapping the SSTAR also showed variable importance and significance of both a region’s past counts as well as other regions’ past counts to explain contemporaneous dengue transmission, with high levels of local dengue transmissions attributed to external dengue transmissions (Figure 4.3.4–4.7). Highly connected areas such as Johor, Singapore and Kuala Lumpur showed elevated cumulative FEIR (Table 4.2). Our results point towards long-run persistence of dengue transmission behaviour, with a one-time one standard deviation shock in a highly connected region leading to long-run perturbations in the region even after 50 weeks (Appendix C). Lastly, by inducing a parsimonious structure on all 16 administrative regions’ dengue transmission counts while taking into account regional connectivity, we created a specification which surpasses the baseline STAR across regions in forecasting performance, although in absolute terms, the error rate is still noticeable relative to the weekly incidence in each region.

The STAR model as described has been applied to problems such as inferring and accounting for spatio-temporal variations in phenomena such as ageing, employment [161] as well as property prices [162]. It can also be applied to problems in infectious disease across regions as evidenced from this chapter and inference could be conducted for recorded influenza or measles case counts across regions. Penalized estimation as proposed in SSTAR can also be used for valid inference in the same specification but can take into account a much larger number of covariates and perform better out of sample, where the traditional least-squares approach fails [113]. There are, however, several limitations of the approach outlined above. STAR and SSTAR demands model parsimony as each additional region or lag leads to an increase in the number of parameters to be estimated, and estimation breaks down as soon as this exceeds the sample size. Regularized estimation of parameters also means that complete statistical inference with proper confidence intervals cannot be recovered in SSTAR, even though our approach to bootstrap approximation has valid asymptotic properties. Other spatiotemporal specific penalties could be more appropriate for the model problem such as fused lasso, but these pose even larger difficulties for statistical inference and may not be useful for forecasting. More comprehensive inference on inter-regional dengue dynamics is not possible using SSTAR due to the well-known issue of identification for the spatio-temporal class of vector-autoregressions [163]. Dengue serotypic history across regions which may inform the relative rate of infectivity between regions also cannot be account for within the model due to a lack of publicly available data of serotype composition across time. Serotype data across regions and time may be placed into separate SSTAR equations for each region, which may allow valuable inference on how genetic interactions may lead to the rise and fall of dengue cases on a larger scale. The proposed method also assumes the availability of data, which makes it more suitable for endemic infectious disease. For novel emerging diseases in the absence of data, the SSTAR cannot be calibrated well and mechanistic epidemic models taking into account spatial heterogeneity would be more appropriate [164]. Lastly, while all sub-national regions should be ideally accounted for in this chapter, dengue transmission data at a weekly frequency is not publicly available for many countries and future work can incorporate spatial restrictions to global-local time series models by taking into account multiple weight matrices integrating land and air transport volumes, population size and dengue incidence [165] for a more accurate characterisation of dengue transmission counts worldwide. To the best of my knowledge, this is the first application of space-time autoregressive specifications for conducting inference on inter-regional dengue transmission dynamics on a weekly basis. We develop a sparse estimation strategy (SSTAR) to delineate interregional dengue transmission counts by explicitly accounting for geographical patterns in connectivity and adjacency. We also showed that the SSTAR is not an artifact of a priori knowledge on connectivity, through assessing model predictive densities even when own region counts are assumed missing. Through forecast impulse responses, we found that substantial local dengue transmissions are externally attributable and highly connected regions transmit long-run persistent shocks to other regions. Finally, the proposed penalized estimation methodology is shown to dominate inter-regional forecasting of dengue transmission counts up to 4 weeks ahead.

Motivation for the next chapter

The past 3 chapters consider all dengue transmission behaviour and may not adequately account for the extremes of dengue transmission dynamics, due to mean reversion in the considered methods. They are thus unsuitable for use as tools to characterizing the extremes of disease transmission. Characterizing the extremes in disease dynamics are important, as these are periods where health systems are most strained [166, 167]. Therefore, the next two projects will attempt to assess the applicability of extreme value theory to extreme disease dynamics. In particular, I will use dengue case counts in Thailand and Singapore as case studies to do so.

Significant health risks arise in Thailand from dengue but little work has been conducted to quantify the extremities of dengue outbreaks – where health systems are likely to be most stretched. In this chapter, we detail the utility of tools derived from extreme value theory (EVT) in modelling the extremes in dengue case counts observed during outbreaks using 25 years of province level dengue case count data in Thailand from 1993 to 2018. We assess the validity of the EVT toolkit by comparing them against 8 competing benchmarks. The inhomogeneous point process representation (IPP) was found to perform best on 5 in and out of sample criteria such as parameter stability, distributional characteristics and out of sample coverage. Lastly, by using the IPP to infer future extreme dengue events, IPP found stark differences at the province level in the mean level of dengue case counts that is expected to be exceeded over the next 10 years. EVT shows considerable potential in aiding health planners for the risk management of dengue. The results in this chapter can be easily translatable to any infectious disease observed over a long period.

Introduction

Dengue is a major global problem in many parts of the world causing epidemics throughout the developed and developing countries [1]. In Thailand specifically, frequent outbreaks in all administrative provinces of Thailand due to favourable vector breeding conditions and hyper-endemic nature of the virus, causes significant impact on public health resources [168]. In Bangkok alone, a recent 2017 outbreak resulted in over 4000 case counts being reported in a single month [169]. These dengue outbreaks are also further aggravated by the nature of their transmission dynamics on a broader scale; they are persistent [47] and also tend to occur in distinct spatial clusters across provinces within the country and may potentially affect any of the 69 million people who are likely at risk [38]. Being able to infer the nature of dengue allows health and planning services at the national and provincial level to be equipped with the necessary information to handle the events. Typically, this has been approached by modelling and/or forecasting the entire dataset using statistical and machine learning tools [46, 45], which has considerable value in terms of understanding the transmission dynamics of the dengue virus, as well as aiding short term resource planning in terms of vector control and disease surveillance. However, the tools used for learning and predicting the transmission dynamics minimize overall error; these are not suitable for characterizing extreme conditions as they model the mean rather than extremes [170]. As a result, the risk and scale of large outbreaks may be hard to quantify for scenario planning purposes. Furthermore, while having pre-emptive knowledge of a looming epidemic brings considerable input to public health officials, large uncertainty exists in the expected number of dengue case counts during an epidemic due to time varying host behaviour and ongoing climate variations [171, 46]. However, an epidemic response is also likely to be the same for an outbreak period of any size and it is thus imperative to triangulate the scale of response for future outbreaks as this would allow efficient and effective risk management of the dengue virus. Similar problems in other fields use emergency planning, such as the scale and probability of potential floods, earthquakes and surge storms in climatology, oceanography and geography [172, 173, 174]. Characterization of these rare but serious events is often conducted using tools developed from Extreme Value Theory (EVT) with inference conducted on the extremes of observed events using the return period notion – the mean time before a random variable reaches or exceeds a certain threshold over a long period of time [175]. Despite the large body of theoretical and applied work in EVT, sparse exploration and development has been observed in a health setting for EVT, even though modelling the scale and accompanying likelihood of an extreme health event, such as an infectious disease outbreak, may be of huge value to policy makers in terms of risk planning and management. Only recently, studies applied EVT to pneumonia and influenza death rates [82] and mortality and morbidity peaks [83] which signals its promise in a public health setting. Therefore, in this study, we demonstrate the utility of EVT in representing extremes of epidemic dengue through a case study of dengue endemic Thailand, which experiences frequent large outbreaks that vary in size across its communities. Tools derived from EVT were applied to observed dengue case counts over all provinces in Thailand where data is available from 1993 to 2018. Our main goal is to model the likelihood and scale of dengue transmission maxima, where the peak of outbreaks are observed. Previous studies, although sparse, have already applied EVT to an infectious disease setting, such as pneumonia and influenza mortality [82] and Salmonella occurrences [166], which points toward the suitability and utility of EVT in an infectious disease setting. While inference on infectious disease peaks is common in the studies described, explicit pre-treatment of dataset assumptions required for the EVT methodology, assessment of the method’s stability and predictive ability across time under restricted datasets is not commonly conducted. Comparisons between the two frequently used pre-processing steps to generate an EVT dataset, namely the block-maxima and thresholding method, has also not been done for the studies described. Our chapter thus evaluates EVT for dengue outbreak analysis as a new approach and toolkit for risk planning and mitigation. We explore the utility of different approaches for deriving epidemic extremities such as the block maxima and thresholding method, along with the ability for different distributions/processes to describe these extremities. We also conduct two exercises to gauge whether EVTs are valid out of sample: 1) testing for parameter stability by looking at whether the estimated parameters are able to overlap even when information sets are restricted and 2) their ability to reproduce epidemic extremes when the full history of dengue transmission is unknown. Lastly, we conducted inference on the return levels and return period of dengue outbreaks across provinces in Thailand and provide policy recommendations given our results.

Data

Project Tycho provided publicly available monthly dengue case counts from 1993 to 2010 for Thailand across 72 provinces, while 2010 to 2018 monthly dengue case counts for Thailand were obtained from Ministry of Public Health Thailand [169]. These data sets originate from the Thailand Dengue Surveillance System [122], and were merged at the province level to provide a harmonized dataset encompassing 72 provinces from 1993 to 2018. Of these, 5 provinces were omitted from the dataset, namely Buengkan, Amnat Charoen, Mukdahan, Nong Bua Lam Phu and Sa Kaeo, due to them splitting and/or joining other provinces in Thailand over the period of our study [176].

Maxima can also be derived by defining thresholds over the observed data. An appropriate threshold balances bias and variance of the estimated parameters. A restrictively high threshold will select too few peaks, leading to high variance and low bias in parameter estimation. Model Assessment

Testing data assumptions

In order to test the independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) assumption required to fit extreme value distributions to the data, we first pre-process dengue case counts according to bi-annual, annual block maxima and thresholding according to the 70th to 95th quantile to provincial level dengue transmission data. These were then subject to the Mann-Kendall (MK) and Wald-Wolfowitz tests [178] to verify the assumptions of trending and randomness in each province-level maxima dataset respectively. Significant results for the p-values are set at the conventional 5% level. 5.2.3.2

In-sample model fit

To assess whether extreme-value distributions are required for dengue maxima values, we compared the distributional fit of GEV and GPD on block maxima and threshold dengue case count series on all provinces against 3 non-extreme but asymptotic distributions. We fitted distributions commonly used in infectious disease modelling, namely, the exponential, Poisson and normal distributions and compared them against each other using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) [179] and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) [180], as these metrics are able to balance model fit and parsimony. The fitted extreme distributions are also assessed against each province’s empirical density to determine whether these distributions appropriately capture the spread of observed data. 5.2.3.3

Out-of-sample model stability

Dengue epidemic patterns may change during the large time span considered, through disease incidence, vector control measures and reporting rate. This may affect parameter estimates and thus we conducted out-of-sample model stability tests to ascertain the validity of our model over the long time span. To do so, we split observed dengue case counts across provinces into 2 halves. Data was then pre-processed and fitted by the method which was found to perform best for the entire sample using the AIC/BIC criterion. Specifically, we varied threshold tolerances from the 70th to the 95th quantile increments to obtain an extreme value dataset on the provincial level. These halved datasets were then fitted to the inhomogenous point process model, the model which was found to perform favourably among the methods to fit the extreme values for the entire dataset. We then compared whether the interval centered around the parameters estimated and bounded by the 1 standard error overlapped for both the first and second half of the dataset. The frequency of interval coverage for the estimated parameters across all thresholds were then computed for all provinces. 5.2.3.4

Model assessment

In-sample validity

Mann-Kendall tests indicated that in the biannual maxima dataset, 53 provinces rejected the null hypothesis of data having trend, while 61 provinces rejected the null hypothesis of data having trend in the annual maxima dataset. Thresholding data over the historical 70% to 95% quantile indicated that 18 provinces failed the Mann-Kendall test on the 70% threshold, down to 2 provinces on the 95% threshold. The Wald-Wolfowitz test indicate a similar pattern, with a large number of provinces failing to reject the null hypothesis of data being randomly generated on the biannual maxima dataset and 67 provinces rejected on the null hypothesis of random data generation on annual maxima and the 95% threshold dataset. The same checks for i.i.d. data signal that applying a 95% threshold would exclude the fewest provinces from extreme value analysis. In general, setting a more stringent threshold increases the number of provinces with extreme values which are i.i.d., with a general increase in number of provinces failing to reject i.i.d. tests when block sizes or thresholds are increased (Table 5.1). Model fit as indicated by AIC and BIC for extreme value inhomogenous point processes (IPP) also linearly decrease as thresholds are increased and this is consistent against 5 competing distributions. The estimated IPP distribution is also consistent with the empirical distribution above thresholds across provinces. The point process specification was thus used to infer return level and exceedance properties of dengue case counts across each province in Thailand thereafter. On the block maxima datasets, the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) were computed after fitting generalized extreme value (GEV) and non-extreme value distributions. AIC and BIC across provinces were lower for the GEV, Gumbel and exponential distributions as compared to the Poisson and normal distributions on both biannual block maxima and annual block maxima datasets. This indicates that after penalizing for the number of parameters fit on each distribution, the generalized extreme value distributions were more appropriately describing block maxima of dengue case counts. Similarly, extreme value distributions and extreme value point processes had lower AICs and BICs on average across provinces as compared to non extreme value distributions such as the normal and Poisson distribution across the 70th to 95th quantile thresholds used (Table 5.2).

Out-of-sample distributional stability

Across all threshold levels, parameter coverage ratios were overall high at 74.9% across provinces and averaged over the 3 parameters estimated for the IPP model. The location parameter has the lowest average level of coverage (61%) followed by the shape (74.6%) and scale parameters (89.1%), which indicates that the location and scale parameter has the least and most stability across the halved datasets respectively (Table 5.3, Figure 5.1). Varying threshold levels from the 70th to the 95th quantile caused average coverage ratios across parameters to change from 62% to 80.5%, at the 87th and 95th quantiles respectively. There is a general pattern of coverage ratios increasing around the 87th and 92th threshold before declining afterwards (Table 5.3). The high coverage ratios across thresholds indicate that the parameters estimated for the inhomogenous point process model do not vary too much even when restricting the data to seperate halves. By also taking the coverage ratio (84%) for normal distributions with the same parameter space but simulated observations as a benchmark due to the lack of limiting distributions for the EVTs examined, the coverage ratio of 75% across truncated data sets across provinces and EVTs parameters can be considered stable (Figure 5.1).

Out-of-sample distributional coverage

Out-of-sample coverage performance using the IPP and generalized Pareto distribution indicated that averaging across provinces and thresholds, placing the test set observations on the extreme value point process/distribution estimated using the training set resulted in the test set observations occurring at around 37.5-49% occurrence probability (Figure 5.2). The training set distributions therefore underestimate by a small degree the scale of extreme events which occur forward in time. Across provinces, Sing Buri had the most test set observations which were underestimated and Mae Hong Son province had the highest degree of overestimation. In general, dengue case count extremities were more likely to be underestimated on the provincial level rather than overestimated. In the provinces which had dengue case count underestimated in the test data set, setting a lower threshold also helps mitigate underestimation by a small degree, improving distributional coverage by an estimate of around 20%. Around 20 provinces had their coverage within the 25% to 75% bounds across all thresholds and these include population dense regions such as Bangkok, Chanthaburi and Rayong. (Figure 5.4) Quantile-quantile plots showed that for low observed values in the test set, simulated and observed quantiles lie closer to the line of equality and deviations grow wider when observed quantiles increase in values. Among the 16 most populated provinces, Chiang Mai has the most apparent degree of observed right-skewness compared to simulated data, while other provinces such as Nakhon Si Thammrat and Udon Thani tend to have left-skewed observed data in comparison to the estimated distribution (Figure 5.3). However, in general, simulated quantiles generated from the estimated distribution using only training set data are well behaved relative to the test set quantiles.

Stark variations in return levels across provinces

Using the fitted inhomogeneous point processes (IPP) (5.5), we generated return levels from 1 to 10 years for each province in Thailand. The return levels provide the level that is expected to be exceeded on average once in 1 to 10 years respectively. Across provinces, we plotted return levels separated in a low, medium and high risk group using the 10th year level which do not exceed 500, are greater than 500 but less than 1000 or exceed 1000 (Figure 5.4 A–C). Exceedance probabilities, which provide the probability that a specific level is expected to be exceeded at a specified future time point, is also computed, to provide a measure of how regular or irregular the estimated extreme events are relative to each province (Figure 5.4 D–F). Across the 72 provinces where dengue case counts are subjected to extreme value analysis Samut Songkram and Nakhon Si Thammarat have the lowest and highest return levels respectively, but both have fairly regular exceedance probabilities compared to provinces within their own risk groups. In the low risk groups, Samut Songkram and Narathiwat have the lowest and highest 10 year return levels, while Lamphun and Roi Et have the lowest and highest 10 year return levels in the medium risk group. In the high risk group, Prachuap Khiri Khan and Nakhon Si Thammarat have the lowest and highest 10 year return levels respectively. (Figure 5.4 A–C)

Projected occurence of extremal events above and beyond

The computed return levels from IPP are also visualized as a percentage of the historical maximal value observed from 1993 to 2018 to take into account province level population differences which may affect the levels of potential dengue transmission which may surface over time. In general, around 50% of provinces have 1 year ahead return levels which are above and beyond the historical maxima. 7 provinces have mean return levels over the 9 following years which are not expected to exceed historical maxima, namely, Chanthaburi, Kalasin , Khon Kaen, Rayong, Songkhla, Yala and Yasothon, where respectively, at maximum, 302, 355, 701, 342, 934, 198 and 345 case counts have been observed in a month over the past 25 years. 27, 12 ,8, 6 and 4 provinces are only expected to only exceed the historical maxima by an average of 1.1 to 1.5 times respectively in 0.1 time increments over the 9 following years. 9th year return levels are highest for Phuket, Mae Hong Son and Prachuap Khiri Khan provinces for the low, medium and high risk groups respectively, even though in absolute numbers, Prachuap Khiri Khan province has the lowest average return level (Figure 5.4 G-I). There are provinces whose return levels across all risk groups which are not likely to exceed the historical maxima at all, such as Rayong and Khon Kaen. Higher risk groups are likely to have return levels which exceed the historical maxima by a greater amount, such as Prachuap Khiri Khan province having 10 year return levels 6 times the projected historical maxima and Phuket province having 10 year return levels only 1.6 times of the observed historical maxima (Figure 5.4 G–I), where they have had 237 and 300 case counts recorded at maximum in a month over the past 25 years respectively.

Results indicate that in 10 years, more than half the provinces will on average experience a dengue outbreak which is beyond the currently observed historical maxima. This was estimated using the inhomogenous point process (IPP) model, which was found to beat seven competing benchmarks in model assessment checks. The IPP model utilized was also able to recreate and capture the characteristics of future extreme events using only past data throughout all provinces in Thailand. Sparse work has been conducted on EVT in a public health setting and no work has been conducted on dengue or a disease on a large spatial scale using EVT. Our study is the first to demonstrate the utility of EVT in modelling dengue extremes on a large spatial scale. By validating the EVT on in-sample and out-of-sample criterion, we showed that the inference conducted is not an artifact of the IPP specification but driven by the data generating process of extremes in dengue transmission dynamics. The computation of return levels and return periods, are able to advise policy planners of the level of occurrence dengue cases are expected to reach in the short-to-medium term. Our study can complement existing work in dengue surveillance and modelling within Thailand [125, 38]. Specifically, by knowing both the projected case counts from current surveillance models and also the likely scale of extremal events across administrative provinces, healthcare professionals on the ground are able to better triangulate the potential magnitude of the outbreak, compared to simply relying on one tool alone. The return level and exceedance probability estimates are also helpful for resource planning purposes especially for policy makers. Specifically for dengue, the expected level of surge of clinical visits, vector control and medication required to deal with dengue fever (DF) and dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) can easily be computed using prior data on DF/DHF occurrence [181] and the expected number of visits and medication required for DF/DHF patients [182] as a factor of the return levels. The necessity for a scale up in health services can also be determined using how far above the historical maxima on average the next extreme event will be through exceedance probabilities estimates on the province level. This also demonstrates the potential utility of our EVT study for the risk management of dengue in Thailand; modelling individual provinces in Thailand allows the decentralized health districts of Thailand [183] to better respond individually to each dengue outbreak, as these outbreaks may occur in different localities, time and scale. The stark variations in incidence across provinces [169] also lead to expected differentials in each province’s estimated 10 year return levels as a result (Figure 5.5), which allows each district’s health system to calibrate resource planning better. Thus, as compared to modelling the maxima of dengue outbreaks nationally, the province level modelling approach is far more appropriate, considering the spatio-temporal heterogeniety of dengue outbreaks across Thailand. Last, the ease of using EVT tools also allows fast estimation of the yearly expected risk of surpassing some defined threshold of observed dengue case counts. Some limitations however exist. First, only monthly case counts were used for EVT analysis due to data availability issues. For planning purposes, weekly case counts should ideally be used as it provides more information on the weekly maxima of dengue transmission and may helps policy planners conduct outbreak risk management on a weekly rather than monthly basis. Second, neither the thresholding nor block-maxima method i.i.d. assumption was satisfied for a small number of provinces and more complicated modelling – such as hierarchical EVT specifications, should be used if one were motivated to model their maxima – however, we note that the EVT assumptions are consistently satisfied for more than 90% of the provinces. Underreporting and variations in the reporting rate among provinces due to differences in healthcare accessbility, mobility and income levels is likely to be another issue [184, 169, 185], with collective improvements in reporting rates due to improving economic circumstances rendering the projected return levels underestimates of the true expected dengue case count maxima provinces will observe in the future. However, we note that parameter coverage tests were valid for on average around 75% of the provinces, which provides some assurance that underestimates will not be severe given that current demographic and economic trends continue. Other dynamics such as serotype co-circulation and case importation are also not accounted for due to the the lack of data availability at the time of study.

Conclusion

In this chapter, we demonstrate the validity of EVTs – specifically, the inhomogeneous point process characterisation of dengue case count maxima in and out of sample. The methods described point towards significant public health relevance in terms of resource planning and outbreak preparedness. Furthermore, the assessments developed also provide a pipeline by which others can ensure the validity of EVT - in the context of dengue transmission and other topics. Our application of EVT points towards the expected occurrence of dengue transmission above and beyond their observed historical maxima over 25 years for more than 50% of the provinces in Thailand. Public health authorities should ramp up dengue epidemic preparedness given these results.

Motivation for the next chapter

In this study, we used canonical tools in EVT by first exploring how the block maxima and thresholding methods of subsetting data, together with using suitable extreme value distributions to fit these datasets influence statistical inference. However, these methods do not account for variations in time in parameters for EVT distribution and may not adequately characterize changes in dengue transmission dynamics temporally, nor use relevant covariates, such as weather, to further provide information on the extremes of dengue transmission. The influence of weather variables, such as humidity [117] and rainfall [127], on vector breeding is well established and may subsequently influence dengue transmission. This has not been incorporated in the methods of this chapter. Therefore, in the following study, I will attempt to further extend basic tools adapted from extreme value theory, by incorporating time-varying distribution behaviour as well as characterize the influence of covariates on extreme value distribution parameters.

Time varying methods to infer extremes

in dengue transmission dynamics This work has been published as: Lim JT, Han Y, Dickens BS, Ng LC, Cook AR. Time varying methods to infer extremes in dengue transmission dynamics. PLoS computational biology. 2020 Oct 12;16(10):e1008279.

Dengue is an arbovirus affecting global populations. Frequent outbreaks occur, especially in equatorial cities such as Singapore, where year-round tropical climate, large daily influx of travelers and population density provide the ideal conditions for dengue to transmit. Little work has, however, quantified the peaks of dengue outbreaks, when health systems are likely to be most stretched. Nor have methods been developed to infer differences in exogenous factors which lead to the rise and fall of dengue case counts across extreme and non-extreme periods. In this chapter, we developed time varying extreme mixture (tvEM) methods to account for the temporal dependence of dengue case counts across extreme and non-extreme periods. This approach permits inference of differences in climatic forcing across non-extreme and extreme periods of dengue case counts, quantification of their temporal dependence as well as estimation of thresholds with associated uncertainties to determine dengue case count extremities. Using tvEM, we found no evidence that weather affects dengue case counts in the near term for non-extreme periods, but that it has non-linear and mixed signals in influencing dengue through tvEM parameters in the extreme periods. Using the most appropriate tvEM specification, we found that a threshold at the 70th (95% credible interval 41.1, 83.8) quantile is optimal, with extreme events of 526.6, 1052.2 and 1183.6 weekly case counts expected at return periods of 5, 50 and 75 years. Weather parameters at a 1% scaled increase lead to a decrease in long-run expected case counts, but larger increases would lead to a drastic expected rise from the baseline correspondingly. The tvEM approach can provide valuable inference on the extremes of time series, which in the case of infectious disease notifications, allows public health officials to understand the likely scale of outbreaks in the long run.

Introduction

An estimated 390 million dengue infections occur annually, imposing major economic and health burdens globally [1]. It is widespread in South-east Asia, with outbreaks occurring annually, sometimes exhibiting synchronous behaviour [38]. Furthermore, it is hyper-endemic within the region due to all four serotypes being in active circulation. Increased urbanization and elevated human movement rates via both domestic and international travel have increased the transmission potential of dengue, particularly across highly connected cities such as Singapore. With favourable vector-breeding conditions due to the year-round tropical climate, a large daily influx of travelers and population density, Singapore has well-suited conditions for dengue infections to occur, with an average of over 100 cases being reported every week from 2000 to 2017. Primarily, vector control is used to mitigate dengue transmission in Singapore and its success is evidenced in the decreasing seroprevalence nationally for the past two decades [13, 186, 9]. However, this low seroprevalence complicates the implementation of vaccination using the tetravalent Dengvaxia (CYD-TDV) [187, 4] vaccine on the national scale [188] due to potentially longer-term risks of severe dengue in vaccinated but seronegative individuals [189]. Fogging and breeding site reduction, in conjunction with novel biocontrol techniques such as Wolbachia are utilized instead to prevent and control dengue epidemics [190]. However, major outbreaks still occur sporadically, with some attributed to population level phenomena such as serotype switching [191, 23, 47], in which a large change in the proportion of circulating serotypes lead to an increase in the number of reported infections. While sufficient healthcare capacity is usually available to deal with muted levels of dengue infections, a large and prolonged rise in the number of cases may significantly impact public health resources.

Being able to infer the properties of extreme values of dengue transmission allows public health planning at the national level to be equipped with the information necessary to handle outbreaks. Typically, modelling or forecasting case counts is conducted using mathematical, statistical and machine learning tools [78, 46]. These tools allow understanding of dengue transmission dynamics, aid short to medium term resource planning for disease surveillance and inform decisions about strengthening vector control for outbreak control [92]. However, these tools are used for predicting and inferring case counts to minimize overall error for the entire dataset and are not optimized to characterize extreme conditions such as the peak of dengue outbreaks [170]. Peaks of dengue outbreaks may also not occur in the near term, which may further degrade the quality of predictions for standard models [78, 92]. As a result, it may be difficult to implicitly quantify the long-term risk and scale of these events. Similar problems exist in other fields such as climatology, oceanography and geography, where the risk and scale of potential calamitous events such as floods, earthquakes and surge storms need to be quantified [175]. Characterization of these rare but serious events are conducted through tools developed from Extreme Value Theory (EVT), with statistical estimation and inference conducted on the extremes of observed events across time and/or space [175]. Some public health work has explored the use of standard EVT tools such as distributional inference on extreme health events such as infectious disease outbreaks; there have been particular applications of EVT to pneumonia and influenza (PI) death rates [82] and food-borne disease outbreaks [192], for instance. These works signal its promise for application in a public health setting. Yet, while relatively large leaps have occurred in the development of extreme value theory—such as inference on both extreme and non-extreme portions of data [167], hierarchical modelling on extreme distributions, formulating EVT parameter estimation as a regression problem through Bayesian data augmentation and quantifying space and/or time dependence of extreme values [193, 194]—to my knowledge, no work in biology nor public health has developed the use of these potentially highly informative extensions of EVT tools. This chapter therefore develops and explores the utility of tools derived from extended extreme value theory to investigate and compare the dynamic signature of extreme and non-extreme periods of dengue transmission in Singapore. Briefly, we aim to determine thresholds and thus classify extreme and non-extreme periods of dengue transmission, quantify the temporal dependence of extreme and non-extreme periods of dengue transmission as well as dengue’s potentially non-linear relationship with weather. To do so, we first developed four separate extreme-bulk mixture models which are able to characterize the different dynamic signatures of dengue transmission, in both extreme and non-extreme periods. These methods were compared against several diagnostic checks, such as residual autocorrelation, quantile-quantile plots, Bayes factor and deviance information criterion, to assess the most suitable model given our data generating process. Next, we explored the potential for climatic fluctuations to affect both extreme and non-extreme periods of dengue transmission and the differences in transmission behaviour between separate periods. Lastly, we projected the expected scale of dengue events in the long run using the most suitable EVT model and provide discussions on these results.

Data

Dengue incidence data in Singapore were collected by the Ministry of Health with mandatory notification of virologically confirmed or laboratory-confirmed cases. Laboratory confirmation of dengue cases is conducted through (1) nonstructural protein 1 (NS1) antigen detection, viral RNA detection by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), or (2) immunoglobulin M detection [195, 196]. Data were publicly available from the Infectious Disease Bulletin, published weekly by the Ministry of Health, Singapore. Data were available from 2010 to 2017. No ethical approval is required for this study. Weather data were obtained from ERA5, published by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts. ERA5 provides hourly estimates across a 30km grid [102], which we aggregate nationally over a weekly timescale and spatially averaged for Singapore. Mean, minimum and maximum air temperature at 2m is calculated to represent thermal forcing and stress on vector population growth, and weekly total rainfall is obtained for breeding site availability. Air temperature and dewpoint temperature is utilized to calculate saturation vapor pressure and actual vapor pressure using Teten’s formula, with which relative and absolute humidity could then be estimated using standard formula

Model Estimation

We estimated the parameters of all models (M1–M4) using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) approaches. In general, Gibbs sampling was conducted when suitable conditional conjugate distributions could be derived, such as the case of regression parameters in the bulk distribution or their respective noise terms. For the constant and time varying GPD parameters, there are no known conditionally conjugate priors. Marginal sampling of their parameters was conducted using Metropolis or Metropolis-Hastings steps instead. For all models, a total of 10 000 MCMC steps were taken with a burnin of 1000. Briefly, for the first model where parameters of interest are the gamma bulk shape and rate parameters a, b and static GPD tail distribution ›, ‡, u, we conducted Metropolis-within-Gibbs steps for each of their respective marginal distributions with proposal distributions tuned to allow efficient exploration of the posterior space. This was also conducted due to the GPD distribution not having any known conjugate prior distributions, which precludes the use of more efficient Gibbs sampling. Convergence of MCMC chains was first assessed by visual inspection of trace plots and Geweke convergence diagnostic checks. Residual autocorrelation is computed for up to 20 week lags to ensure that the transmission dynamics of dengue are properly accounted for and to determine the maximum autoregressive lag order for each specification. Quantile-Quantile plots are used to see whether the bulk and tail specifications adequately account for the data structure. The log Bayes factor was computed using naïve Monte Carlo simulation and deviance information criterion as detailed in Appendix D to provide summary measures of model fit and model appropriateness to data.

Model Assessment

Convergence of MCMC chains for parameters across all four competing models was indicated by Gewecke convergence tests, signalling that each marginal parameter posterior space is well explored. Visual inspection of trace plots was also conducted (Appendix D). Residual autocorrelation for the bulk distribution also indicate that an autoregressive (AR) order 3 model is sufficient to explain dengue transmission dynamics as each lag’s autocorrelation is within the 95% confidence interval bound. Comparing each model’s deviance information criterion (DIC) and log Bayes factor (logBF) showed increasing DIC across models and increasing logBF as models become more complex, indicating that more complex models are penalized as expected from the DIC but overall more favoured in explaining the data according to logBF. In general, a higher logBF and lower DIC indicates a more suitable model for the data. In particular, we obtained the highest DIC and logBF for the model with constant beta term in the tail distribution (Table 6.1 M4 DIC: 14 268, logBF: 663), followed by a trend of decreasing DIC and increasing logBF in the case where no regression structure was imposed on the tail distribution (Table 6.1 M3 DIC: 13 111, logBF: 545), no weather variables being added to the bulk distribution (Table 6.1 M2 DIC: 12 781, logBF: 510), as well as no regression structure being imposed on either bulk nor tail distribution (Table 6.1 M1 DIC: ≠1563). The quantile-quantile (QQ) plots compare the similarity of fitted distributions to data. Although the DIC for the first model (M1) is low, QQ plots indicate that fitting the gamma distribution to the bulk data leads to significant deviations from the line of unity as compared to regression structures (M2–4): this shows that M1 does not provide distributional characteristics which are similar to the data. For the extreme distribution, QQ plots for the regression structure (M4) generalized Pareto distribution (GPD) has points lying closest to the line of unity, indicating that it is the most suitable structure to account for extreme value data (Appendix D). There is a high logBF and favourable QQ plots but also higher DIC for M4 compared to other models. However, as regression structure was imposed on both the bulk and tail distribution, the increased penalty on model complexity under DIC is expected. M4 is also further able to compare the effects of biologically relevant covariates across non-extreme and extreme states of dengue transmission dynamics, compared to all other models. Further exposition on results will thus refer to M4 unless specifically mentioned otherwise.

Nonlinearities in climatic effects on extremes of dengue transmission

We have lastly examined how weather coefficients governing tvEM extreme distributions affect expected return levels. We have only included coefficients whose 95% credible intervals excluded 0, namely, temperature, absolute humidity and relative humidity. In general, we found the effects of weather on return levels to be highly nonlinear and dependent on the specific value of shocks applied to each coefficients. For a 1% positive shock across each parameter at each lag, we found in general a decrease in the expected return levels across time for temperature lag 1 and 3, absolute humidity lag 1 and 2 and relative humidity at lag 1 and 3 (Figure 6.4). However, the changes in return levels are almost negligible when shocks are applied to temperature lag 2, absolute humidity lag 3 and relative humidity lag 2, corresponding roughly to points when the effects of weather on the scale and shape parameter are positive and negative respectively for temperature lag 2 and absolute humidity lag 3. They also correspond to instances when the effects of weather on the scale and shape parameter are negative and positive, respectively, for relative humidity lag 3 (Figure 6.3a–c). For a 5% independent, positive shock across each parameter at each lag, we found markedly larger magnitudes of change in the expected return levels across time (Figure 6.5). The same decreases were found in the expected return levels across time for temperature lag 1 and 3 and relative humidity lag 1 and 3, but these are markedly larger compared to the 1% shock (Figure 6.4–6.5). However, with a 5% shock in absolute humidity lag 1 and 2, their effects on return levels are now positive. The formerly negligible change when shocks are applied to temperature lag 2, absolute humidity lag 3 and relative humidity lag 2 are now positive and rise much more steeply after extending the return period beyond 10 years (Figure 6.5).

Results indicate that the tvEM model with regression structure on the extreme and non-extreme distributions of dengue case counts (M4) could characterize the data well under model assessment checks (Table 6.1). Using M4 to infer weather effects below and above thresholds of dengue transmission showed a duality of weather effects below and above thresholds of dengue transmission, where it is found to affect the GPD distribution parameters but not case counts below the threshold (Table 6.2). The effects of weather on extremes in dengue transmission are also highly nonlinear and severe, with a mild to moderate increase in weather observations leading to long run changes in expected return levels over time (Figure 6.4–6.5). Inference of extreme and non-extreme periods of dengue transmission across time can be conducted using tvEM, with the model being able to characterize the temporal dependence of dengue case counts across time. In general, inference through tvEM indicates that weather measurements such as absolute humidity, relative humidity and temperature are able to influence projected return levels over time, but these same variables do not have a near term influence on dengue case counts directly. These results are in line with previous work [101] which found that dengue case counts in Singapore can be sufficiently described autoregressively. While said weather variables may exert influence on dengue case data over longer timelags, spurious correlations may result from such an analysis due to temporal dynamics already adequately accounted for in the bulk distribution Our results found that a mild to moderate shock in weather variables would lead to considerable changes in the expected return levels over a long return period. As indicated from this analysis, independent 1% positive shocks on weather variables on 1 to 3 week lags would lead to either little change or decreases in projected return levels, while larger 5% shocks would more often lead to increases in projected return levels in dengue case counts across time (Figure 6.4–6.5). Other work has also revealed non-linear, interactive effects of weather on dengue transmission dynamics, wherein weather measurements may often exhibit mixed, concave and/or second order interaction effects on reported cases. For example, large rises in precipitation may reduce viable breeding grounds immediately, but may also lead to the formation of future breeding grounds through rainwater accumulation [201, 202, 203, 117, 204]. Evidence also suggests that mosquito ovipositing, development from mosquito larva to adult, biting rate and viral incubation time in the dengue vector are enhanced at raised ambient temperatures, but these effects have marginal gains at much higher temperatures [105, 205]. Similarly, optimally humid conditions can boost vector egg development and subsequent adult population size that may itself be correlated with the transmission potential of dengue [206, 207, 139].

Using tvEM, events of at least 526.6, 1052.2 and 1183.6 weekly dengue case counts are expected to occur in 5, 50 and 75 years respectively in Singapore. This notion of return levels is helpful for public health resource planning purposes. In the case of dengue, the expected surge in vector control, primary and secondary care required to deal with dengue fever (DF) and dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) can easily be computed using prior data on DF/DHF occurrence [181] and the expected number of visits and medication required for DF/DHF patients [182], both as a factor of the return levels. This method is also easily translatable to other diseases in different localities, regions or countries where disease case counts are collected over a long period of time, where the notion of exceeding a certain number of cases in any disease can be obtained for risk management purposes. The methods described in this chapter are also able to resolve certain issues in empirical EVT applications. Thresholds are estimated automatically using the MCMC approach employed in this chapter, with threshold estimation uncertainty also quantified in its posterior distribution. This allows individuals to ignore the often subjective methods to select thresholds using graphical diagnostic tests and various rules of thumb approaches while working with extreme values empirically [175, 208, 170, 209]. Next, imposing an hierarchical temporal structure in tvEM also resolves the temporal dependence of observed data for the extreme value distribution, allowing stronger inference on time dependent data such as dengue case counts. This could easily be translated for use in other endemic infectious disease data such as influenza, for which case counts are also temporally dependent. Lastly, by inducing a regression structure on the extreme value distribution, statistical learning on relevant covariates could be conducted, which then allows meaningful interpretations of the covariates on projected return levels. These covariate effects could then also be compared to that of non-extreme value data within a single model structure, as evidenced by tvEM in this chapter. Several limitations are recognised. First, explicitly quantifying the effect of covariates is difficult in the extreme portion of data, as their effects are on the time-varying extreme value distribution parameters rather than the dependent variable itself. However, while direct comparison between extreme and non-extreme portions of the data is challenging, we used the shock notion to explore the effects on return levels. Time varying parameters estimated over the extreme value distribution are also difficult to interpret and return levels have to be computed after integrating out the parameters sampled over the range of values over the threshold. The large number of parameters estimated due to time varying extreme value parameters also makes the MCMC estimation approach slow and future work should explore the use of approximate but faster estimation methodologies such as particle MCMC or approximate Bayesian computation [210, 211]. The suitability for each EVT tool on data also cannot be evaluated directly using quantities such as the mean-square error or R2 as estimation above the threshold provides a time varying regression on parameters but not observed case counts unlike the bulk distribution formulation, leading us to use other diagnostic measures such as DIC or logBF in model comparison. The results of this chapter are also only based on passive surveillance data on dengue case counts in Singapore, and may not apply to other health systems with different case definitions or surveillance systems. Lastly, while dengue case counts are plausibly spatio-temporally dependent and may have a large number of past case counts, different multicollinear weather lags affecting contemporaneously reported dengue cases, only the most parsimonious model adequately accounting for temporal dynamics is used due to the inherent difficulty in estimating a time varying distribution with regression structure. Future work can explore nesting sparse regression priors or spatio-temporal priors on the extreme value regression to infer and incorporate spatio-temporal behaviours or the effect of many covariates on dengue extremes. Incorporation of sparse regression priors may also resolve the issue of multicollinearity between many variables in both bulk and extreme portions of data [212, 213, 214]. When data become available, the same assessment checks described in this chapter can also be conducted on separate dengue time series to examine model validity, climate-extreme value exposure-response relationships and disparities in return levels. The methods developed in this chapter are easily applicable to any other infectious disease where time series are recorded over a sufficient period of time. These methods are able to conduct statistical learning of covariates on extreme and non-extreme periods of time series data. Lastly, tvEM is able to provide meaningful notions of return levels on a pre-specified return period, which in the case of infectious disease data, allows public health officials to prepare for the likely scale of an infectious disease outbreak in the long run.

Motivation for the next chapter

Crucially, this chapter used dengue case count data from 2010 to 2017 to determine return levels and thereby provide the level of dengue risk forward in the future. However, the methods proposed in this chapter have underestimated the large rise in dengue case counts experienced in 2020 in Singapore, at a maximum of 1,800 weekly cases from June 7 to June 12. Various factors, including social distancing and a switch in the pre-dominant serotype are attributed to the large rise in dengue case counts. These are factors which cannot be accounted for in the time-varying extreme value framework. In the next 3 chapters, we will attempt to uncover the reasons why a larger than expected rise in dengue case counts was experienced in 2020 in Singapore, and more broadly, Thailand and Malaysia.

Introduction

An estimated 105 million dengue infections occur per year, with case counts reported in over 120 countries [215, 216]. Southeast Asia and the Western Pacific region is estimated to bear 75% of the global burden of dengue [217]. The Southeast Asia region comprises of 11 countries with a total population of 1.97 billion [218] and the Western Pacific Region comprises of 37 countries with a total population of 1.9 billion [219, 220] i.e. more than North or South America. Around 2.9 million dengue cases are reported annually in the region, with high incidence year-round, resulting in over 5500 dengue-related deaths on average [221]. The dengue virus is transmitted primarily by the Aedes aegypti and Ae. albopictus vectors. The tropical-subtropical climate of Southeast Asia is suitable [85] for the vector and in conjunction with increasing human population connectivity, population density, global warming and urbanisation within the region, creates an ideal environment for dengue transmission to occur [124, 222, 223]. Urban centers in Southeast Asia also often report the highest number of dengue case counts, which are postulated to seed outbreaks in other localities [124] and increases in temperatures due to climate change allow for optimal vector breeding conditions, shorten the incubation period of the dengue virus and increase the overall risk of dengue disease within the region [224]. Four serotypes of the dengue virus exist and predominate in different localities [225]. The active circulation of all four serotypes in Southeast Asia, where dengue is hyper-endemic, leads to complex cross-immunity dynamics at the population level, causing multi-annual cycles of outbreaks [124] which may be highly persistent [47, 226]. In Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand, all four serotypes are in active circulation, with switches in the predominantly reported serotypes over time observed in tandem with a dramatic rise in reported case counts [227, 228, 229]. Typically, people infected with dengue virus are asymptomatic or display mild symptoms such as febrile illness [230], but those infected a second or subsequent time by a different serotype are at a greater risk of more severe disease due to antibody dependent enhancement, causing potentially life-threatening conditions such as dengue hemorrhagic fever [116]. Individuals with severe dengue are provided treatment to manage clinical symptoms, but no cure exists for the virus [231]. Varying seroprevalence rates across populations also make vaccination using the tetravalent Dengvaxia (cyd-tdv) and tak-003 vaccines challenging [232], therefore, the primary public health intervention for dengue is currently vector control [231]. Intervention measures are implemented across Southeast Asia depending on the severity of the dengue outbreak observed. Cross-border surveillance systems have been set-up among the Southeast Asian countries to track plausible dengue case importation and other arboviruses [34]. Within countries, in Singapore, the management of dengue is primarily conducted through vector surveillance [233], vector control, public engagement [59] and other novel techniques such as Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes [55]. In Malaysia, breeding site reduction in households and water sources is conducted through domestic inspection [234] as well as larvicidal treatment [235]. The release of Toxorhynchites sp. mosquitoes was also conducted periodically in hotspots to reduce the vector population [236]. Similarly, Thailand applies a vector control management program to reduce breeding sites in urban and suburban locales [237]. In all three countries, control focuses on the environment in which contact between mosquitoes and humans is thought to be highest, primarily residential areas [238]. In general, these interventions are also focused on localities such as Bangkok, Thailand; Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia; and Singapore, high density urban areas where transmission risk is highest, with the largest number of reported case counts [239, 240]. Social distancing (sd) measures have been broadly implemented to reduce the transmission potential of the ongoing Covid-

pandemic [241, 242, 243]. However, despite the extant

literature detailing human mobility, geographical clustering, home-work infection patterns being determinants of vector-borne diseases [124, 44, 244, 245, 147], the effects of sd on the transmission potential of vector-borne diseases such as dengue are not known nor considered to be a standard form of intervention, due to sd’s minimal influence on vector dynamics as compared to targeted vector control measures on a localised scale.

The ongoing Covid-

sd across countries [246], with a rapidly deteriorating global situation leading to near complete lockdowns in Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand. Specifically, the reduction of movements through workplace closures and mass gatherings were implemented and heavily enforced in these countries, which have led to a large decrease in time spent in workplaces (see Appendix E for summary) over a period of 2 to 3 months before gradual relaxation of measures. This provides a natural experiment to estimate the effects of reducing human mobility and workplace exposure on dengue transmissions, due to the near-complete coverage of sd policies across populations and heavy compliance through enforcement of said measures [247]. We exploit the pre-post differences in human mobility and locations for exposure from sd as a quasi-experiment on reported dengue case counts across three countries in peninsular Southeast Asia, namely Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand, to estimate the treatment effects from sd on dengue transmissions. These were countries in Southeast Asia where reported dengue case counts were publicly available and recorded at the timepoints when social distancing policy was implemented. Primarily, by accounting for time-varying confounders such as climate and seasonality on the vector population [85], trends in dengue transmissions and country-specific geographical profiles, we estimate the causal effect of sd intervention by applying a regression discontinuity design (rdd) to the time point when the policy was implemented [248]. The immediate and clearly defined start date for sd policy implementation warrants the consideration of a sharp rdd rather than the fuzzy rdd of [249], which is primarily used in scenarios where the policy implementation is not prompt or when the cutoff point is not clearly defined. The treatment effect of sd policy is also applied to almost the entire population over the considered regions, with only a minimal number of essential workers allowed to work away from home and businesses to remain open. The sd policy thus encompasses a majority of each population over a large spatial scale, which minimizes the risks of substitution effects of human mobility on dengue transmissions from sd policy [250]. Compliance to the treatment is also near universal, with the primary reason being heavy enforcement by authorities, the strict penalties applied across the considered regions and individuals whom are away from home being easy to spot [251]. We first detail the data used and the rdd design considered for the three countries. Robustness checks and controlling for confounders are then conducted to ensure valid identification of sd treatment effects. The average treatment effect per month is computed for sd on dengue to look at the overall benefits attributable to sd. Finally, the implications of this study are discussed.

Dengue Case Count Data

Monthly provincial dengue case data from Thailand were obtained from the National Disease Surveillance report system from January 2010 to May 2020 which is maintained by the Bureau of Epidemiology, Thailand [252]. Weekly dengue case data in Malaysia from 2010 to 2017 were obtained from Malaysia’s open data portal run by the Malaysian Administrative Modernisation and Management Planning Unit (MAMPU) [239]. The weekly dengue case data from 2018 to 2020 was extracted from the World Health Organisation Institutional Repository for Information Sharing [253]. Weekly dengue case data from 2012 to 2020 were obtained from the Weekly Infectious Diseases Bulletin published by Ministry of Health, Singapore [240]. For Singapore and Malaysia, data up until the 20th and 14th epidemiological week of 2020 were used respectively.

Climate Data

Climate data were obtained from ERA5, published by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts. ERA5 provides hourly estimates across a 30km grid [102], which we spatially averaged for all provinces of Thailand at a monthly time scale. For Singapore and Malaysia, the data were aggregated to a weekly timescale and spatially averaged. Mean temperature at 2m was calculated to represent thermal forcing and stress on vector population growth, and total rainfall as a proxy for breeding site availability. Air temperature and dewpoint temperature were utilized to calculate saturation vapor pressure and actual vapor pressure using Teten’s formula, where relative and absolute humidity could then be estimated using standard formula [109].

Intervention Data

Intervention data for social distancing were obtained through national governmental and local news websites for the start and end dates of social distancing as well as the intensity of social distancing measures implemented. The full source listing is detailed in the supplementary information of [254].

Population Census Data

Population census for Thailand by province from 2010 to 2020 were obtained from Thailand’s official statistics registrations systems which is managed by the Office of Registration Administration, Department of Local Administration. Population census data for Malaysia from 2010 to 2019 were obtained from Department of Statistics, Malaysia which contains population information by state and by country [255]. Population census data for Singapore from 2010 to 2019 were obtained from the Department of Statistics, Singapore [256]. The data and code used for this chapter has been attached as supplementary material in [226].

Robustness Checks

We built our rdd specifications (7.1–7.2) by sequentially adding the confounders discussed in the previous section. This was conducted to ensure robust identification of the social distancing policy effect estimated in each country and to fully ensure that the policy effect estimated was not an artefact of other phenomena which affect dengue transmission periods across the periods before and after policy implementation [262]. Taking dengue case counts per 10 000 as the dependent variable, we first estimated (7.1–7.2) only using the regression discontinuity policy term. Next, seasonality was added, followed by the first to third order polynomial spline terms. Lastly, climate variables were added for additional sensitivity analysis. Sensitivity of the policy effect estimates to error term misspecification was also considered by estimating (7.1–7.2) both by ordinary least squares and the generalized least squares approach taking into account serial correlation between observations. Sensitivity of discontinuity coefficient effect size, values and standard errors to the time window of data used before the discontinuity were also checked by restricting the number of observations allowed in estimating (7.1–7.2). Multicollinearity and error normality assumptions for linear regression are checked using the variance inflation factor and quantile-quantile plots. The full output for these robustness checks are provided in Appendix E.

Robustness Checks

The policy effect of sd on number of dengue cases per 10 000 in Thailand remains positive and statistically significant at the 0.001% level even under sensitivity analysis of our rdd design to model misspecification or omitting biologically important covariates. We conducted exercises by omitting either serial correlation between provinces or within province correlations in the panel generalized least squares procedure, shortening the time period of data for estimation, or the addition/omission of one month lag climatic variables but no change in direction nor statistical significance was observed (Appendix E). Similarly, the policy effect of sd on number of dengue cases per 10 000 in Singapore and Malaysia has 95% confidence intervals containing 0 under sensitivity analysis of our rdd design to model misspecification or omitting biologically important covariates. However, one instance where the 95% confidence intervals excluded 0 was when serial correlation was omitted and no time dependence between successive dengue case count observations were taken into account yielded a negative sd policy effect on dengue cases per 10 000 in Malaysia at -0.03048 and negative or positive sd policy effect on dengue cases per 10 000 in Singapore. These estimates however were sensitive to addition or omission of covariates and did not take into account serial correlation of the dependent variable.

Results indicate that implementation of sd has led to an increase in dengue cases in Thailand, as compared to Singapore and Malaysia where this effect was not found. One key pathway that may explain this result is the propensity for dengue infections to surface at home rather than work addresses, and the disparity in sd policy effects being driven by across country differences in residential structure. Implementation of sd policies result in an increase in time spent within home addresses and thus increased risk of dengue infections if infections mostly occur at the home. Although it is possible for dengue infections to occur in workplaces, one study found that 60% of dengue cases living less than 200m apart came from the same transmission chain. This demonstrates that residential areas are a focal point of dengue transmission [238]. Additionally, an increased frequency of movement within urban and predominantly residential neighbourhoods was found to increase the risk of dengue infection [265]. Patterns in urban area structure and population density could also influence the rate of dengue incidence, as agglomerations of high-rise buildings have a lower dengue incidence as compared to low-rise buildings [266]. Urban and rural areas with predominantly low-rise residential buildings with denser drainage networks could thus increase the breeding habitats of dengue vectors [266]. While large differences in urbanisation demographics may potentially affect dengue preventive practices especially in Malaysia and Thailand with significant rural populations [267], multiple studies have shown that there is no large difference in dengue incidences between urban and rural areas in either Malaysia [268, 269] or Thailand [270, 271]. Also, spatiotemporal studies of countries with similar climate comparing dengue incidence between urban and rural also found no significant difference in dengue incidence between study areas [272, 273]. Thus, in terms of geographical confounding, the disparities in sd policy effects on dengue incidence between these countries is likely to be driven by across country differences in residential structure rather than differences in urban-rural structure [274]. Serotype switching is well studied as a possible cause of dengue outbreaks in dengue endemic regions such as Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand [35, 227]. This is not explicitly accounted for in the rdd design due to data availability issues. In Singapore, a sustained switch in the predominant serotype from denv-1/denv-2 to denv-3 has led to a drastic rise in dengue case counts in 2020 [275]. The inception of serotype switching could influence reported case counts by providing temporary or partial cross immunity that is passed onto others within the community [276, 228], but these population level dynamics were not explicitly accounted for in our rdd design. However, switches to/ from denv-1 and denv-2 have been linked to large outbreaks in Singapore have also suggested contemporaneously low population immunity to these strains [227]. Currently, immunity to denv-3 is even lower as it has not been the predominant serotype in Singapore’s dengue history until 2020 [275]. These effects may have confounded the effect of social distancing on dengue incidence in Singapore, but given that the transmission leadership of denv-3 has not been observed in the past, it is difficult to determine the direction of effect on reported case counts. Conversely, the phenomenon of serotype switching has not been reported for Thailand or Malaysia around the time where sd is implemented, which discounts the possible effect of cross-immunity dynamics on reported case counts in these populations. Other long term effects from cross immunity dynamics such as changing sero-prevalence and source-sink importations are also expressed in multi-annual cycles [228, 124], which is unlikely to bias estimates of the discontinuity at the point of social distancing in either direction for Thailand and Malaysia. In Thailand, we estimate an additional number of dengue cases per month to be 2008.34 (95% CI: 1170.40 to 2846.28) due to social distancing policy, primarily through increased exposure in residences across the country as discussed. However, as the genetic diversity of dengue around one’s home is likely to remain fixed in absence of locality specific importation, recovery from infection from a serotype confers life-long immunity to that serotype [228] which may strengthened the herd immunity effect in each neighborhood, leading to a gradual decrease in the rise of dengue cases over a longer period of time should sd policies persist. Increases in mixing patterns away from home by exiting from sd policy however, may lead to an increased risk of locality specific importation into residences. With countries gradually easing social distancing policies and dengue exposure patterns shifting to the pre-sd policy regime (See Appendix E for policy summary), the future impact of exiting from sd policy on dengue transmissions is unknown and necessitates further study. However, should sd policy be re-instituted, vector control in residential areas is necessary to reduce the transmission potential of dengue [277]. Social distancing policy is used as a public health intervention for respiratory diseases as it reduces human contact by increasing time spent in residences and a reduction in time spent elsewhere. This decreases the transmission potential for these diseases by changing the mixing patterns within the host population [278, 242]. sd policies motivated by the sars-covpandemic also reduced the transmission potential of sars-covas well as other respiratory illnesses [279, 280]. A change in human movement patterns can likewise influence the transmission pattern of vector-borne diseases, as evidenced from this study— primarily through changing the host risk of exposure to vectors, but in the opposing direction. More time spent at a site was shown to increase transmission risk of vector-borne diseases irregardless of vector density [281]. The transmission risk of vector-borne diseases could be higher at home and in one’s neighbourhood, such as with dengue [238], malaria [282] and Chikungunya [133]. Social distancing and increasing time spent at residences could lead to higher incidence of vector-borne diseases, which corroborates the policy effect seen for Thailand. It is of interest to generalize the natural experiment design of social distancing on other vector-borne diseases as the policy effect on the reported incidence of other diseases may be different. The Aedes mosquito vector, which transmits dengue,

Chikungunya, Zika virus and yellow fever prefers breeding in artificial containers around the house or holes in trees, while the Anopheles mosquito vector, which transmits malaria, prefers permanent water features such as lakes [283]. The differences in habitats for these vectors may result in differences in exposure risk pre-post policy for the host population and further work is needed to ascertain the sd policy effect on these other vector-borne diseases. This study identifies the pre-post sd policy effects on dengue incidence while controlling for biologically important covariates which vary across time, such as seasonality, climate and time trend. Robustness checks also reveal the persistence of our results under omission/addition of these variables, with or without model misspecification. The heavy enforcement and compliance to sd policy measures on a set start date across the three countries studied also mean that treatment effects are likely to apply to a large proportion of each population and the sharp rdd is appropriate. There are however a few limitations to our study. The implementation of social distancing policies could result in the under reporting of dengue cases as individuals may be less willing to leave their homes and seek professional medical treatment. Under reporting may also result from the additional burden sars-covplaces on health systems, which decreases the number of non-covid-patients seeking treatment This could bias downwards the estimated sd policy effect on the incidence of dengue. When more data become available, compartmental models could be used to adjust for the reporting rate. Additionally, cross-immunity dynamics of dengue in populations are difficult to account for, however, we reason that these are likely long term trends which are unlikely to affect short term discontinuities generated by the sd policy. The lack of available spatial data for Malaysia and Singapore means that the rdd design is conducted at a national scale, which may not be able to account for differences in pre-post policy spatio-temporal effects on dengue transmission, though for both countries, the policy implemented was a national one so the impact of this should be limited.

Motivation for the next chapter

Heightened time spent in residences through social distancing measures may lead to increased exposure to the dengue vector, which explain the rise in number of dengue cases reported over a large spatial scale. This chapter used all available data for a timely analysis of the impacts of social distancing measures on dengue case counts at the height of the dengue epidemic in 2020. Controlling for all confounders, we failed to find any significant impact of social distancing measures on dengue case counts in Singapore, despite its large rise during the period of social distancing. In the following chapter, we further exploited age heterogeneity in treatment to create appropriate control and treatment groups during the time period where social distancing policy was enacted in Singapore, to further explore any pre-post differences in dengue case counts before and after social distancing policy were due to policy effects.

Background

An estimated 105 million dengue infections occur per year [215]. Primarily, the Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus vectors transmit the dengue virus. The combination of increasing human population connectivity, climate change and urbanisation within the region creates an ideal environment for dengue transmission and outbreaks to occur [47, 226]. The unprecedented response to the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic has led to widespread social distancing (SD) and other non-pharmaceutical interventions. Population-wide implementation of SD has led to near complete lockdowns in most countries. However, despite the extant literature detailing how human mobility, importation, home-work infection patterns are key determinants of dengue epidemic potential, the effects of SD on vector-borne diseases such as dengue are not known. SD may bring hosts and vectors into closer proximity, if it results in social changes to the time spent at home, if the density of the anthropophilic Ae. aegypti is higher peridomestically. In Singapore, the reduction of movements through workplace and school closures and cessation of mass gatherings were implemented and heavily enforced during SD policy. This has led to a large decrease in time spent in localities away from home ([284] for summary) over a period of 2 to 3 months before gradual relaxation of measures. Most individuals of employment age in Singapore work in air-conditioned premises due to the highly developed economy [256]. The Ae. aegypti vector is a day biter with breeding most concentrated around residential areas [285]. Dengue infections at workplaces are rare at baseline, but the increase in dengue exposure risk due to increased time spent in residences due to SD is likely. The near-complete coverage of SD policy across most age groups, regular and clear whole of government mass communication and heavy compliance through enforcement of said measures also greatly reduces policy leakage and non-compliance across the entire population. This provides a rare opportunity to identify the effects of shifting exposure patterns to residences on dengue transmissions by considering the implementation of SD policy as a natural experiment.

We obtained reported weekly case counts of dengue in Singapore by 5-year age bands from 2003 to 2020, where data were available till the 21th epidemiological week (EW) of 2020. All confirmed cases of dengue are legally required to be notified to the Ministry of Health for surveillance and control. We controlled for the effects of weather on potential vector breeding, using weather data obtained from Meteorological Services Singapore. The measurements of each weather station for each day were averaged and temporally aggregated to the weekly frequency. Maximum and mean temperature, together with relative humidity, were used to represent thermal forcing and stress on vector population. Intervention data for SD were obtained through the Ministry of Health, Singapore website. SD measures were implemented from 7 April 2020 to 1 June 2020, with varying degrees of strictness ([284] for summary). The primary goal of our analysis is to identify the effects of SD policy in Singapore on reported dengue case counts over time. In Singapore, SD policy led to a closure of around 95% of workplaces as well as all schools and recreational facilities [286]. The increase in time spent in residences and reduction in mobility patterns from SD policy would therefore most drastically affect individuals who are schooling or working prior to policy implementation. Treatment effects from SD policy can therefore be ascribed to those who are above 5 or below 65 years of age, which is around the retirement age in Singapore [256]. In Singapore, those who fall outside these bands mostly do not have drastic changes in mobility patterns from SD, as less than 50% of individuals below 5 attend child care centres for full-day programmes and less than 1% of individuals in retirement age reside in elderly care facilities [256]. A majority of individuals in the control group thus spends most of their time in residences or in the neighbourhood even before SD policy implementation.

Results indicate that implementation of SD is associated with an increase in the number of reported dengue cases in Singapore. One key pathway that may explain this result is the increased time spent in home addresses due to SD policy together with the propensity for dengue infections to surface at home rather than work addresses in Singapore [287]. Although workplace infections may surface, it was found that, in many areas, residences were the most common place of vector-borne disease infection, rather than workplaces [287]. Concentration of vector breeding sites around residential areas further compounds risk of transmission in these localities as compared to workplaces [285]. In conjunction with shifting work patterns into homes with naturally ventilated spaces which do not offer protection during the biting period in the day, these SD policy motivated pathways together converge risk factors of dengue transmission.

Cross immunity dynamics as a potential confounder

Cross immunity dynamics is well studied as a possible cause of dengue outbreaks in dengue endemic regions such as Singapore [23]. Virological surveillance in Singapore revealed an emergence of DENV-3 leading to co-dominance of DENV-2 and DENV-3 in 2020 in the same period SD policy occurred. While prior infection of individuals may have provided temporary/partial cross immunity, which thereby reduces the transmission potential of dengue in the community [94], this may have waned since the last outbreak in 2013. Antibody dependent enhancement, the posited pathway by which secondary infections of dengue become more severe and likely to be reported clinically, may have occurred in far more individuals, due to the emergence of DENV-3 and prior lack of exposure to this serotype in large proportions of the population. Previous serotype switches to/from DENV-1 and DENV-2 have been linked to large outbreaks in Singapore have also suggested low population immunity to these strains [23]. Currently, immunity to DENV-3 even lower as it is not the predominant serotype in Singapore’s dengue history until recently. These effects may have confounded the effect of SD on reported dengue cases in Singapore. The effects of serotype switching and other cross-immunity dynamics are however expressed in multi-annual cycles, which is unlikely to greatly bias policy effect estimates when policy is implemented only for the period of 2–3 months.

Implications for continued SD policy

Prior studies have showed that the likelihood of genetic diversity of dengue around home addresses to remain fixed in the absence of mobility and importation [31]. In conjunction with life-long immunity conferred after recovery from a specific serotype [94], the herd immunity within localities may increase, which will possibly lead to a decrease in the rise of dengue cases should SD policy persist. Conversely, should SD policies ease and with dengue exposure patterns shifting back to the pre-SD policy regime, increases in mixing patterns away from home may lead to an increased risk of locality specific importation into residences. It is possible that an outbreak may be prolonged due to increased human mobility spatially spreading the high prevalence of virus. The impact of exiting from SD policy on dengue transmissions is however not quantified in this study and future work is necessary. Of concern are also regions where SD policies have been instituted, relaxed and re-instituted such as Hong Kong, several states in the United States and China. Similar policy behaviour has not been observed in dengue hyper-endemic regions such as Singapore and Malaysia, but the effects of multiple entry and exits from SD policy on dengue transmissions are also not known. It is likely that SD policy institution, relaxation and re-institution regimes may amplify, mix then seed and amplify new areas of vector-borne disease transmission and should SD policy be re-instituted, vector control in residential areas should be pre-emptively employed to mitigate the transmission of dengue.

Implications for other vector-borne diseases

The efficacy of SD policy as a public health intervention for reducing the onward spread of respiratory diseases is well known as it reduces human contact by increasing time spent in residences. Increase in time spent in residences during the day and thus their exposure to vectors can likewise influence the transmission pattern of vector-borne diseases–but in an opposing direction. Increasing time spent at a locality elevates transmission risk of vector-borne diseases despite of vector density [281]. Prior studies have shown that the transmission risk of vector-borne diseases could be higher at home and in one’s neighbourhood [238]. The highly urban environment of Singapore only allows identification of SD policy effects on reported dengue case counts, as other vector-borne diseases are not widespread. It is of interest to generalize the natural experiment design of SD on other vector-borne diseases as the transmission patterns of malaria are vastly different, in terms of vector breeding behaviour and viral dynamics. The differences in habitats for the respective vectors may result in differences in exposure risk before and after distancing policies. The indirect effects of sars-covnon-pharmaceutical interventions beyond that of sars-covtransmissions need to be urgently quantified. This study reveals that SD policy has led to a sharp increase in dengue incidence in Singapore. Additional measures to pre-emptively mitigate the risk of other communicable diseases such as dengue must be considered during the implementation of SD measures.

Motivation for the next chapter

Crucially, this short study attributed a large rise in dengue case counts due to social distancing policies in Singapore. Finer scale data using age groups allowed us to use a more appropriate identification strategy which takes into account the differences in policy treatment between age groups to reveal the impacts of social distancing policy on dengue case counts. Motivated by this strong result, our last study extends the notion in the current chapter by exploring how differences between populations, policies (social distancing or quarantining) and age groups influence dengue transmissions.

Background

The ongoing coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, caused by the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has led to the widespread implementation of non-pharmaceutical interventions to curb transmission in the general population and subpopulations, including migrant workers [243]. COVID-19 infection risk in migrant workers living in dormitories can be elevated due to their dense living environment, inability to socially isolate and access to healthcare and prevention [288, 289]. Singapore has a foreign workforce of approximately 1.4 million, of whom approximately 300 000 work in construction, cleaning or related industries [290]. A majority resides in dormitories—high density housing with shared living spaces [291]. In the six months between March and August 2020, over 50 000 PCR confirmed SARS-CoV-2 cases were reported in migrant worker dormitories, versus ≥2000 in the general population of more than 5 million, highlighting the differential risks in COVID-19 transmission among the two groups. [290, 291, 292, 293] Dengue is a global problem which increasingly affects travellers, including migrant workers who pursue work outside their home country [294]. In tandem with the COVID-19 pandemic, Singapore experienced a record-breaking dengue outbreak in 2020, with an unprecedented 30 000 diagnosed cases reported at the end of September. A two-month imposition of social distancing measures nationally in response to the COVID-19 outbreak, similar to lockdown in other countries, contributed to the rise in dengue cases in the general population [284], presumably due to increased peridomestic contact between humans and mosquitoes. In the same period, strict quarantine policies have been enacted in migrant worker dormitories due to the large number of cases in these locales and to prevent onward community spread of COVID-19 [289]. It is not clear, however, how social distancing and quarantine measures imposed on foreign workers might have altered their risk of acquiring dengue. Construction sites, where a large proportion of migrant workers reside in the work day, are typically hard to manage environments, being previously demonstrated to be nexuses for both dengue and Zika transmission [66, 295]. Answering this question may contribute to our understanding of the risks associated with workplace exposures, and the impacts of the shift of exposure risk from workplaces to homes, for both the general community and migrant worker populations. This study aimed to identify the potential causal effects of confining migrant workers to dormitory sites on reported dengue cases by considering social distancing and quarantine policies as a natural experiment.

The notification of all confirmed cases of dengue in Singapore is legally mandated by the Ministry of Health for the purpose of disease surveillance and control. We obtained reported case counts of dengue in Singapore by 5-year age bands from 2013 to 2020 (data were available to epidemiological week [e-week] 23, 2020) and analyzed the data on a weekly timescale for the community and migrant workers. We controlled for the effects of weather on potential vector breeding, using weather data obtained from Meteorological Services Singapore [296]. Maximum and mean temperature, and relative humidity, were included as these were correlated with mosquito population activity in Singapore [117]. Two months of enhanced social distancing measures lasted from 7 April (e-week 15) to 1 June (e-week 23) [290]. During this period, around 95% of workplaces were closed, including all schools and recreational facilities, and all construction sites [297]. To prevent widespread transmission of COVID-19 in the dormitories, all migrant construction workers were quarantined in their residences during this period. This therefore changed the ratio of time exposed to mosquitoes at home versus at (mostly) air-conditioned workplaces—for the general population—or (mostly) worksites—for migrant construction workers, and thereby the risk of dengue infection if these locations have heterogeneous risk. The treatment effects of social distancing and quarantine policies on dengue exposure in the general community was therefore expected to be experienced more strongly by working-age adults and school-going children (i.e. those aged 5–65), and also migrant workers living in dormitories. Those outside these age ranges were assigned as the control group. These individuals were likely to have limited change in their mobility patterns from social distancing policies, as fewer than 50% of individuals below 5 attend child care centres for full-day programmes and fewer than 1% of retired individuals reside in elderly care facilities [115, 298]. The majority of these individuals spent their time in residences or very localised childcare facilities within the neighbourhood both before and during social distancing policy implementation. A difference-in-difference (DiD) identification strategy was used to determine the causal effects of social distancing and quarantining policy on dengue case counts over the 10-week period. We took observations of reported dengue case counts in four treatment groups separately; (1) working adults aged 20–65 years during lockdown, (2) schooling population aged 5–19 years old during lockdown, (3) migrant workers in dormitories in quarantine. The total control group for each separate comparison comprised of cases in all age bands before social distancing policies, and those in ages 5 or younger and over 65 during social distancing policies. The implementation of COVID-19 quarantine measures among migrant workers living in dormitories, mostly limited their movements to within managed accommodation and away from their usual place of work, decreased the number of reported dengue cases among that population. This contrasted with the impact in the general community, wherein dengue cases rose during lockdown due to social distancing policies. This dichotomy implied between group differences in mosquito exposure at worksites and residences. Outdoor construction and similar worksites are known to be problematic for vector breeding control due to the presence of persistent surface water from rainfall or manmade inputs [66]. In Singapore, although less than 5% of dengue case clusters were recorded in construction sites between 2013 and 2016, clusters that did form at construction sites were substantially more likely to develop into major clusters [66]. These construction site clusters have also led to spill-over dengue transmission in surrounding residential areas [66]. Similar evidence was found in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, with a preliminary correlation observed between dengue incidence rates and construction rubble accumulation [301]. Additionally, in Zhanjiang Prefecture, China, a dengue outbreak began at a construction site with 102 cases [302], and in Vientiane, Lao People’s Democratic Republic, a construction site was a point source for an outbreak of 27 cases [303].

Dormitory accommodation is centrally managed, designed for efficiency, and often have communal toilets to facilitate cleaning and prevention of mosquito breeding. The paucity of space for personal effects may also reduce mosquito breeding opportunities. This study suggests that time spent at dormitories leads to lower exposure to dengue-infected mosquitoes than time spent at worksites. In addition, many of these dormitories are sited well away from the residential areas that house the general population, offering increased protection from public and private residential developments that were historically more prone to dengue outbreaks [284]. In terms of accommodation, migrant construction worker dormitories in Singapore are well-ventilated but have relatively higher population densities compared to public housing, which likely contributes to dengue transmission risk should mosquitoes exhibit multiple host biting behaviour [304]. There has therefore been a strong drive over the years to improve the housing standards of these dormitories to prevent dengue transmission within these residences, coupled with frequent checks by National Environment Agency Officers for mosquito breeding sites [8]. The results suggest that transmission risk was consequently reduced at dormitory sites relative to workplaces where despite the ongoing initiatives, workers are at higher risk of dengue transmission in workplaces, relative to their place of residence [305]. Within the general community, dengue transmission risk increased during the period of intensified social distancing measures, suggesting that home residences remained as key transmission sites [284]. Domestic and relatively localised outdoor transmission was identified as a primary pathway in Thailand where 60% of dengue cases living less than 200 metres apart were identified as being from the same transmission chain as opposed to 3% which were separated 1 to 5 kilometres apart [238]. In Brazil, 70.4% (95% CI: 58.2%–79.8%) of individual transmission events occurred within 500m [306], and in Vietnam, households were described as foci for dengue transmission in highly urbanised areas [307]. For Singapore, increased dengue transmission during the social distancing intervention period may be partially attributed to an overall higher exposure to mosquito populations in the home relative to workplaces, which may be air conditioned and therefore relatively segregated from outdoor mosquito breeding and resting sites.

Our results illustrate the opposite effect of social distancing measures on dengue risk between the migrant construction worker and community populations during the 2020 dengue and COVID-19 epidemics of Singapore. The current dominance of DENV-3 is problematic due to its lower seroprevalence and consequent population wide immunity [13], requiring greater mosquito population surveillance and intense vector management, which is particularly challenging during the social distancing and quarantine intervention period. An increase in dengue cases in the general community may be attributed to longer dwelling times and a higher probability of mosquito bites within the home, with the inverse among migrant construction workers who may be at relatively higher risk of dengue transmission at working sites [308]. Several limitations are however present in our study. The implementation of social distancing and quarantine policies could result in under reporting of dengue cases as individuals may be less able to seek professional medical treatment. Under reporting may also result from the additional burden SARS-CoV-2 places on health systems during the height of the pandemic, which may decrease the number of non-COVID-19 patients seeking treatment. This may bias downwards the estimated policy effects on the incidence of dengue. When more data becomes available, compartmental models could be used to adjust for the reporting rate. The lack of spatially resolved dengue case data also means that the identification strategy can only be conducted at a national scale, which may not be able to account for differences in pre-post policy effects on dengue transmission across both space and time, though these policies considered were implemented on a national scale, so the impact of this should be limited. The study highlights the vulnerability of migrant workers to dengue infection under their usual working conditions at worksites. Increased measures for migrant worker protection against dengue should be considered. Such measures are likely to benefit the general population who may live within close proximity to construction sites.

Conclusion and Future Work

Models to characterize the transmission dynamics of vector-borne diseases are well explored. Studies to further extend and improve the utility of these models is likely to continue. A large body of work has already been done to characterize the risk factors, forecast and develop interventions for vector-borne diseases [85, 46, 77], but certain aspects remain neglected, such as (1) characterizing outbreaks and understanding their characteristics (2) characterizing the extreme patterns of transmission (3) spatio-temporal interactions between regions (4) policy implications from population-wide interventions. This thesis aims to fill these key gaps. Below, I will summarize the thesis and highlight key areas for future work. Chapters 2 and 3 of this thesis explore the utility of regime switching models to identify characteristic changes in the behaviour of dengue case counts and whether the proposed model can classify periods where outbreaks occur appropriately as well as describe the behaviour of outbreak and non-outbreak periods. These chapters employed the regime switching model to the context of dengue in Singapore and Thailand respectively. In Singapore, we found that these outbreaks occur sporadically, in multi-year cycles and are highly persistent. While climate factors were found to influence dengue case counts in Singapore previously in other work [117], their impacts on defining outbreak from non-outbreak periods were found to be ambiguous, suggesting that dengue outbreaks are driven structurally by the changes in dengue counts themselves rather than climatic factors. When further extending the regime switching model notion to a larger spatial scale such as Thailand, we found several key climate and spatial drivers, such as urbanicity, population density and flight patterns being associated to the outbreak prone regions of dengue, highlighting the need to further examine outbreak risk from the spatial perspective.

Preliminary epidemiological work has suggested that switches in the pre-dominant serotype and establishment of fitter genotypes pre-empt looming outbreaks [23, 191] and further study should be carried out to understand whether the incorporation of these multi-strain and genetic features can be used to further restrict our frameworks and predict looming outbreaks. In Chapter 4, I proposed a regression based method to characterize dengue transmission over multiple regions while incorporating spatial dependencies and model sparsity. Our proposed methods were found to beat competing models in forecasting dengue case counts over multiple regions. We showed that the model is not an artifact of a priori knowledge on connectivity, through assessing model predictive densities with each assessed region’s counts being assumed missing. Through forecast impulse responses, we found that substantial dengue transmission in each region is externally attributable and highly connected regions transmit long run, persistent shocks to other regions. The utility of incorporating sparsity to forecast infectious diseases is well-known [92, 46], beating competing models due to reduction of out-of-sample errors by virtue of parameter shrinkage. Future work can and should explore different forms of shrinkage adapted to each disease inference and forecasting problem. The large number of parameters used for these models also poses a problem for inference of risk factors, should this be the goal of the study. Future work should also explore the utility for these tools to infer risk factors for disease transmission. Chapter 5 adapts tools from extreme value theory (EVT) for inferring infectious disease extremes. Chapter 6 extends these tools further to incorporate time-varying parameters and threshold uncertainty. In Chapter 5, conventional tools developed from EVT were examined in their applicability for modelling infectious disease extremes, by first exploring how the block maxima and thresholding methods of subsetting data, together with using suitable extreme value distributions to fit these datasets influence statistical inference. These were employed to the dengue case count data in Thailand. Using these methods, we found that return levels for dengue are expected to exceed beyond that which is observed in all provinces of Thailand previously and public health authorities must ramp up dengue epidemic preparedness given these results. However, this study used methods which did not account for time-varying parameters in the EVT distribution and may not adequately characterize changes in dengue extremes across time. These methods also did not use relevant covariates, such as weather, to further provide information on the extremes of dengue transmission. Therefore, we further generalized the extreme value notion to a case study of dengue in Singapore, incorporating time varying parameters and a regression structure. Thresholds are estimated automatically using a Bayesian approach employed in this study, with threshold estimation uncertainty also quantified in its posterior distribution. This allows individuals to ignore the often subjective methods to select thresholds using graphical diagnostic tests and various rules of thumb approaches while working with extreme values empirically [175, 208, 170, 209]. Next, imposing a hierarchical temporal structure in tvEM also resolves temporal dependence of observed data for the extreme value distribution, allowing stronger inference on time dependent data such as dengue case counts. Future work should explore the utility and translate these tools for use in other endemic infectious diseases such as influenza, for which case counts are also temporally dependent. Exploring spatio-temporal generalizations of the proposed methods can also be conducted, especially for dengue case counts recorded for a large spatial scale, such as Thailand. Finally, chapters 7, 8 and 9 adapt regression-based identification strategies and the notion of natural experiments to explore how changes in population mixing conditions due to SARS-CoV-2 non-pharmaceutical interventions can affect dengue transmission. These 3 chapters respectively found, after controlling for all potential confounders and the temporal dependence of dengue transmission: (1) excess burdens of dengue in Thailand due to social-distancing measures (2) a large rise in dengue case counts in the working age group in Singapore due to lockdown policies and (3) a large decrease in dengue case counts in the migrant worker populations in Singapore due to quarantining policies. These studies provide a framework to identify the implications of SARS-CoV-2 non-pharmaceutical interventions on other diseases or phenomenon. Future work should generalize, when data become available, the impacts of these interventions on vector-borne diseases endemic to other dengue countries, such as malaria and Chikugunya, whose transmission pathways differ in terms of the type of vectors and locations. The impacts of non-pharmaceutical interventions on other classes of infectious diseases, such as those which are respiratory in nature, can also be quantified but more work is needed to ascertain whether these are appropriate identification schemes.

Final Statement

This thesis has proposed a set of tools to characterize the transmission dynamics of vector-borne diseases, using dengue as the primary theme. We have proposed methods to classify and characterize outbreaks, the extremes of dengue transmission and the spatio-temporal evolution of dengue transmission. Furthermore, we have developed frameworks to identify the impacts of SARS-CoV-2 non-pharmaceutical interventions on dengue transmission. These methods will serve as a base for future studies to develop methods to understand vector-borne diseases.
